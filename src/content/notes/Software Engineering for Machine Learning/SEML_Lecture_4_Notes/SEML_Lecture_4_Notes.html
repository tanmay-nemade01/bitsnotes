Lecture Summary

Session 4 extends goal-oriented requirements engineering and connects requirements to architecture through quality attributes.

GR for ML modelling continues from the business view into the analytics design view — specifying whether analytics are descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, or prescriptive, which algorithms apply, which metrics matter (accuracy, precision, recall, F1), and soft goals like interpretability and robustness. The credit-risk example evolves: case workers process applications, historical data trains a classifier refreshed monthly, and outputs drive approve/reject decisions. The data preparation view documents cleaning, feature engineering, and preprocessing steps that must align between training and serving.

Quality attributes (QAs) generalise non-functional requirements: performance, scalability, availability, security, maintainability, fairness, explainability, and ML-specific concerns like reproducibility and concept drift tolerance. Architecturally significant requirements (ASRs) are QAs that strongly influence structural choices — for example, low-latency inference pushes toward edge deployment, while auditability pushes toward explainable models.

Case studies in smart healthcare, Apollo autonomous vehicles, and RAG-based enterprise chatbots illustrate how QAs drive component boundaries. The distinction between software architecture (code structure) and system architecture (data, model, serving, and governance layers) is made clear. Trade-off analysis is emphasised: optimising accuracy may hurt latency; strong consistency may conflict with scalable reads. Reproducibility requires versioning data, code, environments, and models together.

Key Learning Objectives

  • Complete analytics design and data preparation views for GR for ML case studies.
  • Classify analytics goals as descriptive, predictive, or prescriptive with appropriate metrics.
  • Define quality attributes and identify architecturally significant requirements for ML systems.
  • Relate QAs to architectural choices using healthcare, autonomous driving, and RAG examples.
  • Distinguish software architecture from system architecture and recognise common ML patterns.

4.1 GR for ML - Requirements Modelling (Continued)

In the previous session, we introduced Goal-oriented Requirement Engineering for Machine Learning (GR for ML), a modelling notation analogous to UML in traditional software engineering. It provides standardised views for capturing and communicating requirements of ML systems. Just as UML provides use-case diagrams, class diagrams, and sequence diagrams, GR for ML provides multiple complementary views.

Why Modelling Notations Matter

A modelling notation ensures that any designer, architect, or developer anywhere in the world interprets the system description identically. This eliminates ambiguity — exactly as a UML class diagram unambiguously specifies inheritance, aggregation, and associations regardless of who reads it.

The three views of GR for ML are:

  1. Business View (covered in Session 3) — captures high-level business goals and stakeholders.
  2. Analytics Design View — defines what type of analytics will be applied and which algorithms/metrics to use.
  3. Data Preparation View — specifies data cleaning, reduction, and pre-processing steps.

4.1.1 Recap: Business View (Credit Risk Prediction)

The running example throughout this chapter is a credit risk prediction system for a bank.

Scenario: Credit Risk Prediction
  • Actor: Case worker (bank employee) processes loan applications.
  • Business Goal: Process applications and take a final decision on credit (approve/reject).
  • Data Source: Applicant profile (demographic info, salary, qualifications, etc.)
  • Model: A predictive model trained on 48 months of historical data, refreshed monthly.
  • Output: Credit risk classification: HIGH or LOW.

4.1.2 Layering Goals: From Organisation to Model

Goals can be discussed at multiple layers, and untangling them helps understand the system's purpose better. The textbook identifies four key layers:

Four Layers of Goals
  1. Organisational goals: The highest-level goals of the organisation building the system. For profit-seeking companies, these almost always relate to revenue, profit, or stock price. Since these are long-term and hard to measure directly, organisations often use leading indicators — observable proxy measures expected to correlate with future success (e.g., number of attorneys licensing a chatbot, subscription renewal rates). Many organisations frame leading indicators as key performance indicators.
  2. Product goals: Concrete outcomes the product should produce. For a credit risk system, the product goal is to help banks accurately and efficiently process loan applications.
  3. User goals: What users of the system try to achieve. Multiple user types may have different goals — in the credit risk scenario, case workers want to process applications efficiently, while applicants want fair and timely decisions. Even indirectly affected stakeholders (regulators, auditors, society at large) may have goals that influence requirements.
  4. Model goals: From the perspective of the machine-learned model, the goal is almost always to optimise prediction accuracy. However, this must be contextualised within the larger system.
Goals Do Not Always Align

Different layers of goals often support each other — better models should lead to better products, which should satisfy users and generate revenue. However, this alignment is not automatic. In a legal chatbot scenario: more advanced NLP capabilities may answer more legal questions without involving an attorney, making clients happy but reducing attorneys' satisfaction (fewer client referrals). A model that is "good enough" may be perfectly adequate for the organisational, product, and user goals — higher accuracy may not always be better.

Identifying goal conflicts early is valuable because it fosters deliberation and enables designs that balance competing objectives.

From Goals to ML Opportunities

Goals are an excellent bridge to explore the business case for machine learning. To identify where ML adds value:

  1. Identify the decisions people routinely make in support of a goal.
  2. Ask which of those decisions could be supported by predictions.
  3. Determine which predictions can be provided by machine learning.

This can be captured as user stories: "As [role], I need to make [decision] to achieve [goal]" and "As [role], I need to know [question] to make [decision]."

4.1.3 Analytics Design View

Once we capture the business requirement (“We want to predict credit risk”), the next question is: What kind of analytics should we apply?

Analytics Design View

The Analytics Design View specifies:

  1. The type of analytics (descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, or prescriptive).
  2. The algorithms that can be used to achieve the analytics goal.
  3. The performance indicators/metrics (accuracy, precision, F1 score, etc.).
  4. The soft goals (non-functional quality attributes such as robustness, interpretability).

Types of Analytics

Understanding Indicators as Thresholds

Indicators such as accuracy and precision act as selection criteria. For example:

Indicator as Selection Criterion

Suppose you set the accuracy indicator to ≥ 85%. You train three models:

  • SVM achieves 82% accuracy → Rejected
  • Naïve Bayes achieves 88% accuracy → Candidate
  • Random Forest achieves 91% accuracy → Selected (highest above threshold)

The indicator serves as a minimum threshold; among all qualifying models, the best performer is chosen.

Soft Goals: Interpretability, Robustness, and Tolerance

Soft goals are quality attributes that influence algorithm selection beyond raw accuracy.

Interpretability of ML Models

Interpretability (or explainability) refers to how easily a human can understand why the model made a particular decision.

High interpretability example: A Decision Tree for applicant classification produces a clear, traceable path from root to leaf. You can literally hand-trace the logic: “If age > 30 AND salary > 50,000 AND credit history = good ⇒ LOW risk.”

Low interpretability example: A Deep Neural Network for the same task has thousands of weights across multiple hidden layers. While it may achieve higher accuracy, explaining why a specific applicant was classified as HIGH risk is extremely difficult.

Trade-off: There is often a trade-off between model accuracy and interpretability — simpler models (linear regression, decision trees) are more interpretable but may be less accurate, while complex models (deep learning) can achieve higher accuracy but at the cost of interpretability.

Tolerance to Missing Values: If a non-mandatory field (e.g., secondary phone number) has a high percentage of missing values (say > 60%), it may not be a useful feature. The tolerance threshold helps decide whether to include or drop a feature.

Connecting Soft Goals

In the GR for ML diagram, soft goals are connected with “leads to” relationships (shown as +/− influence links). For instance, high tolerance to missing values leads to (positively influences) robustness of the overall model.

4.1.4 Accuracy vs. Precision: A Crucial Distinction

These terms are often conflated but have distinct meanings:

Aspect Accuracy Precision
Definition Proportion of all predictions that are correct Proportion of positive predictions that are truly positive
Formula (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN) TP / (TP + FP)
Focus Overall correctness Reliability of positive predictions
Analogy “How many shots hit any valid target?” “How tightly clustered are the shots?” (consistency)
Intuitive Example: Medical Diagnosis

A model predicts whether patients have a disease.

  • Out of 100 patients: 90 healthy, 10 sick.
  • Model predicts everyone is healthy.
  • Accuracy = 90/100 = 90% (looks great!)
  • Precision for “sick” class = 0/0 (undefined — no positive predictions made)
  • Recall for “sick” class = 0/10 = 0% (catastrophic!)

This shows why accuracy alone can be misleading, especially with imbalanced datasets. Precision and recall provide a more nuanced view.

In the context of measurement theory, precision also refers to the consistency (reproducibility) of repeated measurements. If you survey 100 users about chatbot satisfaction and repeatedly get scores around 3.5, the measurement is precise (consistent) regardless of whether 3.5 is the “true” value.

Measurement Accuracy vs. Measurement Precision

In measurement theory (distinct from ML classification metrics), these terms have complementary meanings:

  • Accuracy of measurement: How closely measured values represent the true real-world value (e.g., does our subscriber count measure reflect actual subscribers?). Inaccuracy is systematic error (bias).
  • Precision of measurement: How reliably the measurement process produces the same result (e.g., repeatedly counting subscriptions in a database always yields the same number). Imprecision is random noise.

These must be addressed differently: Imprecision is easier to identify and handle — noise is visible in measurements, averages out over time, and can be managed with statistics. Inaccuracy is far more dangerous — it represents a systematic problem that cannot be detected by statistical means. For example, accidentally counting expired subscriptions produces a perfectly precise but inaccurate measure, and only discrepancies with other observations may reveal the error.

Evaluating Measure Quality: Validity

For important custom measures, developers should evaluate measurement validity through at least three lenses:

  • Construct validity: Do we measure what we intend to measure? Does the abstract concept match the operationalization?
  • Predictive validity: Does the measure reduce uncertainty in the decision we want to make?
  • External validity: Does the measure generalise beyond the specific observations used to develop it?

As an absolute minimum, plot the distribution of observations and manually inspect a sample to ensure results make sense.

4.1.5 Measures and Metrics in GR for ML

Measure / Metric

A measure or metric is a quantifiable indicator related to a goal that is:

  • Related to the goal — directly connected to what you are trying to achieve.
  • A quantifiable objective — expressed as a number, percentage, or score.
  • Practical to collect — can actually be measured in practice.
Everything is Measurable

Douglas Hubbard defines measurement as "a quantitatively expressed reduction of uncertainty based on one or more observations." The argument: (1) If we care about a property (quality, risk, security), then it must be detectable. (2) If it is detectable at all, then there must be some way of distinguishing better from worse, hence we can assign numbers. These numbers may not be precise, but they reduce uncertainty and help make better decisions.

However, measurement is not always economical — the cost of measurement must be weighed against the benefit of reduced uncertainty in decision-making. For example, when hiring, we can invest more in progressively accurate candidate evaluation (reviewing past work → sample tasks → extended try-out periods) depending on the expected payoff of a better hiring decision.

The Three Ingredients of Measurement

Every measure should be described precisely enough that an independent party could reimplement and evaluate it. The textbook recommends specifying:

  1. Measure: A description of what we try to capture (e.g., "monthly revenue," "90%-quantile response latency," "customer satisfaction of subscribing attorneys").
  2. Data collection: What data is collected and how (e.g., "log each request timestamp," "email a survey link to 5% of attorneys monthly").
  3. Operationalization: How to compute the measure from the data (e.g., "sum of active subscription fees," "delta between request and response timestamps, select fastest 90% and report the slowest among them").

When standard tools already operationalize the measure (e.g., coverage.py for branch coverage), pointing to them is sufficient. For custom measures, more detailed descriptions are warranted.

The Streetlight Effect

It is tempting to rely on cheap-but-less-reliable proxy measures — for instance, counting chatbot messages exchanged (easy from logs) as a proxy for user satisfaction. This is known as the streetlight effect: looking for answers where it is easy to look, not where the answers actually are. Approaches like the Goal-Question-Metric (GQM) design strategy — first clearly identify the goal, then the questions that indicate goal achievement, then concrete metrics that answer those questions — help avoid this trap.

Example: Chatbot Usefulness
  • Goal: Improve chatbot usefulness (vague/abstract).
  • Metric: User satisfaction score on a Likert scale of 1–5.
  • Method: Survey 100 users, compute average satisfaction score.
  • Threshold: Average ≥ 3.0 indicates the chatbot is useful.
  • Result: Score = 3.5 ⇒ Goal met.

Every stage (business view, analytics view, data preparation view) requires clearly defined metrics.

4.1.6 Data Preparation View

Data is the foundation of any ML system. Without properly prepared input data, no meaningful analytics can be performed.

Data Preparation View

The Data Preparation View specifies:

  1. The data entity (what data we are working with — tables, documents, images, etc.)
  2. Data cleaning tasks (handling missing values, outliers, duplicates)
  3. Data reduction tasks (feature selection, dimensionality reduction)
  4. Pre-processing algorithms (normalisation, encoding, transformation)
  5. Mechanism (the process/pipeline for executing the preparation)

Note: The data preparation elements and tasks are visualized as part of the integrated UML conceptual model diagram above.

Do Not Confuse

Algorithm in Data Preparation View ≠ Algorithm in Analytics Design View

  • Data Preparation: Normalisation, one-hot encoding, label encoding, PCA, standard scaling — these are pre-processing algorithms.
  • Analytics Design: SVM, Naïve Bayes, Random Forest, Neural Networks — these are the learning/prediction algorithms.

Data Cleaning

Data cleaning involves preparing raw data for analysis:

  • Missing value imputation: Replace missing entries with mean, median, mode, or use advanced methods (KNN imputation, MICE)
  • Outlier detection & treatment: Z-score, IQR-based filtering
  • Duplicate removal: Identify and remove repeated records
  • Noise filtering: Smoothing, signal processing (especially for audio/image data)
  • Type correction: Fixing incorrect data types (string dates → datetime)
  • Consistency checks: Ensuring data integrity across fields

Data Reduction

Data reduction involves reducing the volume of data while maintaining analytical quality:

  • Feature selection: Identify features with the highest importance relative to the outcome variable. Not all features influence the output equally.
  • Dimensionality reduction: Principal Component Analysis (PCA) transforms high-dimensional data into fewer dimensions that capture the most variance.
  • Feature engineering: Creating new, more informative features from existing ones.
  • Sampling: For very large datasets, representative sampling may be used.

Pre-processing Algorithms

Common Pre-processing Algorithms
Task Algorithm When to Use
Encoding One-Hot Encoder Nominal categorical data (no ordinal relationship), e.g., colours
Encoding Label Encoder Ordinal categorical data, e.g., education level
Normalisation Min-Max Scaling Scale features to [0, 1]; sensitive to outliers
Standardisation Z-Score Scale to μ = 0, σ = 1; preferred when data has outliers
Reduction PCA Reduce dimensions while preserving max variance
Missing Values Mean/Median Imputation Simple, fast; works for MCAR data
Missing Values KNN Imputation More sophisticated; uses nearest neighbours

The Operator: One-to-Many Relationship

The operator notation in the data preparation view indicates a 1:N (one-to-many) relationship. For a single dataset, you may need to apply multiple pre-processing algorithms — normalisation AND encoding AND imputation. The operator represents this iteration over multiple preparation steps.

Example: Multi-format Data Preparation

For an applicant profile dataset, you might need:

  1. Encoding: Convert “Gender” (Male/Female) using label encoding.
  2. Imputation: Fill missing “Secondary Phone” values with a default.
  3. Normalisation: Scale “Salary” and “Age” to comparable ranges.
  4. Feature Selection: Drop low-importance features.

All four algorithms are applied to the same data entity.

4.2 Case Studies: Why Quality Attributes and Architecture Matter

Before diving into quality attributes, let us examine two contrasting real-world case studies that demonstrate the critical importance of getting quality attributes and system architecture right.

4.2.1 Case Study 1: Flipkart Big Billion Day Failure (2014)

Flipkart Big Billion Day — 6 October 2014

Context: One of India's first major e-commerce flash sales. Discounts across 70+ categories. 3 lakh (300,000) orders in 6 hours.

What went wrong:

  • Website crashed due to unprecedented traffic.
  • Selected products vanished from carts after recovery or showed as “sold out”.
  • Money was deducted from accounts, but orders were not placed.
  • Massive customer complaints; reviews were hidden.
  • No proper refund or cancellation process.

Root causes:

  1. Wrong architecture: Flipkart was running a monolithic architecture that could not scale.
  2. Quality attributes not identified: Scalability and performance requirements were not adequately addressed.

Outcome: Flipkart subsequently migrated from monolith to microservices architecture. Today, Big Billion Day sales run successfully thanks to proper architectural decisions.

4.2.2 Case Study 2: Hotstar ICC World Cup Streaming Success (2019)

Hotstar — ICC World Cup Semi-Final, India vs. New Zealand (2019)

Context: Live streaming of India–New Zealand semi-final on Hotstar (OTT platform).

The numbers:

  • Day 1 (NZ batting): 13.9 million concurrent users
  • Day 2 (India batting, Dhoni era): 25.3 million concurrent users — highest ever recorded concurrent viewership at that time (surpassing YouTube's previous record of 18M).
  • After Dhoni's dismissal: viewership dropped from 25.3M to ≈4 million within minutes.

What Hotstar did right (deployed on AWS):

  • Used five 9x-large EC2 machine types.
  • Deployed across 3,000 machines distributed over 8 AWS regions.
  • Conducted tsunami testing (extreme load testing beyond expected peaks, including sudden scale-down scenarios).
  • Notably, they did not use AWS auto-scaling — they pre-provisioned infrastructure based on predicted demand.

Why Scale-Down is Also a Problem

The Scale-Down Challenge

Scaling up is an obvious necessity, but scaling down is equally challenging:

  1. Cost: Idle infrastructure costs money (pay-per-use model). Unused resources must be de-provisioned promptly.
  2. Service redistribution: When 25 million users leave the “Play” microservice simultaneously, they don't just close the app — many navigate to the home page, triggering the recommendation service. This service, which was not scaled for 25M users, now needs to handle the sudden surge.

In a microservices architecture, scaling down one service often means scaling up another service that receives the redirected traffic.

4.2.4 Case Study 3: Twitter's Complete Redesign (2011-2012)

Twitter — From Monolith to Microservices

Context: Twitter was originally built by three friends as a monolithic database-backed web application in Ruby on Rails. Once it became popular, the system became slow and nearly impossible to scale.

What went wrong:

  • Developers introduced caches throughout the application and bought many machines, but could not handle traffic spikes.
  • Fundamental design was deeply baked into the system — monolithic codebase, single database, Ruby on Rails — making it hard to change to improve performance.
  • Changes that marginally improved performance often made the system harder to maintain and debug.
  • Scaling was mostly achieved by buying more hardware, resulting in unsustainable operating costs.

The solution — complete redesign driven by four quality goals:

  1. Improve latency and operating cost
  2. Improve reliability by isolating failures
  3. Improve maintainability with clearer module boundaries
  4. Improve modifiability so small teams could release features quickly

Architectural decisions: Monolithic system → microservice architecture (each functionality in separate, independently scalable processes); Ruby on Rails → Scala for performance; new distributed storage solution avoiding single-point bottlenecks; automated failover, monitoring, and load balancing built into all remote procedure calls.

Trade-offs accepted: The new system was more complex (inherent in distributed systems) and more costly to develop, but this was deemed necessary to achieve the primary quality goals. The architecture proved remarkably robust — it survived Twitter's ownership change in 2022 when operated by a much smaller team.

Key Architecture Lesson from Twitter

Notice how the original monolithic Ruby application was appropriate at the time — when Twitter started, scalability was less important than releasing a prototype quickly to gain users and funding. The quality goals of the system changed over time. The problem was that architectural decisions are fundamentally hard to change later without a full redesign. This tension between initial agility and future scalability is a core challenge in software architecture — and even more relevant when ML components introduce additional design decisions and new quality requirements.

4.3 Quality Attributes (Non-Functional Requirements)

Quality Attribute — Definition

A quality attribute is a measurable or testable property that specifies how well a system meets the needs of the stakeholder along a specific dimension of interest, acting as a qualification of the functional requirements or the overall system.

Key Insight: Qualification, Not Function

Quality attributes are attributes (qualifications) of functional requirements — not standalone features. Just as “cream” is an attribute (colour) of a shirt, “scalability” is an attribute of the “order food” function.

No functional requirement ⇒ no need for quality attributes. If nobody uses a system, its scalability, performance, and security are irrelevant.

4.3.1 General Quality Attributes (Applicable to All Systems)

These quality attributes apply to both ML and non-ML systems.

Operational Attributes

  1. Availability
    Availability

    How long (what percentage of time) the system is operational and accessible to users. Measured as uptime percentage.

    Typical SLAs:

    • 99.9% (“three nines”) ≈ 8.76 hours downtime/year
    • 99.95% ≈ 4.38 hours downtime/year
    • 99.99% (“four nines”) ≈ 52.6 minutes downtime/year

    No provider guarantees 100% availability.

  2. Reliability
    Reliability

    How consistently the system delivers its intended functionality without failures. Reliability is about correctness of output.

    Availability vs. Reliability — The Fast Math Joke
    • Person A: “I am very fast in math!”
    • Person B: “What is 10 + 2?” → A: “12” → B: “Excellent!”
    • Person B: “What is 1024 × 1024?” → A: “100”
    • Person B: “That's wrong!” → A: “I said I'm fast, not correct!”

    Availability = system is there (fast to respond).
    Reliability = system gives the correct answer.

    • Availability is the more basic/foundational attribute.
    • Availability does NOT ensure reliability. A system can be available (responsive) but unreliable (giving wrong results).
    • You need availability first, then reliability on top of it.

  3. Performance
    Performance

    How quickly the system responds to user actions. Performance is primarily from the end-user perspective. It measures response time, throughput, and latency.

    Example

    If a mobile app takes 1 minute to load the home screen, users will abandon it. A ChatGPT prompt should return a response within seconds.

  4. Scalability
    Scalability

    The system's ability to handle increasing load (users, requests, data volume) without degradation. Scalability is internal to the application/infrastructure, not dependent on individual user conditions.

    Critical Relationships
    • Scalability ⇒ Availability: A scalable system should be available. ✓
    • Scalability ⇓ Reliability: A scalable system may still give wrong answers. ✗
    • Scalability ⇓ Performance: A system can be scalable (infrastructure is fine) but an individual user may have poor performance due to local factors (e.g., slow internet). ✗

    Scalability is a system perspective. Performance is an individual user perspective.

Functional vs. Non-Functional: Common Confusion
  • Functional: “Order food”, “Cancel order”, “Get refund”, “Customer support” — these are features.

4.4.1 Accuracy (ML-Specific)

Accuracy

How accurately the ML model predicts or classifies. It is the ratio of correct predictions to total predictions.

Why Accuracy is a Quality Attribute, Not Functional

A common question: “Isn't accuracy part of the functionality?” No.

  • Functionality: Predict whether a patient is diabetic — this is the function (input → output).
  • Quality: The prediction should be ≥ 90% accurate — this qualifies the function.

Any algorithm (SVM, Naïve Bayes, etc.) can make a prediction. Even a 56% accurate model “works” functionally. It is the accuracy threshold that turns it from merely functional to practically useful. This is analogous to Flipkart's monolith: it worked functionally, but collapsed under load because scalability (a QA) was not addressed.

4.4.2 Scalability in ML Systems

In ML systems, scalability has additional dimensions beyond user requests:

  • Data volume: The system must handle ever-increasing data (data deluge). Every second, massive amounts of data are generated.
  • User requests: Number of concurrent requests to the prediction API.
  • AI workloads: Training jobs, inference jobs, RAG pipelines running concurrently on cloud or on-premise infrastructure.

4.4.3 Model-Level Quality Attributes (from Textbook)

Beyond prediction accuracy, ML models have several additional quality attributes that influence system design and operating economics:

Inference Latency and Throughput
  • Inference latency: How long it takes to make a single prediction. Some models are near-instant (e.g., log n evaluations in a decision tree), while others require massive computation (e.g., billions of floating-point multiplications in a large language model). Latency may depend on the specific input, so it is common to report median latency or 90th-percentile latency. If the model is hosted remotely, network latency is added to inference latency.
  • Inference throughput: How many predictions can be made in a given amount of time, especially relevant for batch processing.
Model Size, Memory Footprint, and Energy
  • Model size: File size of the serialised model, which influences how much data must be transmitted for every model update. Large files become problematic when versioning models and storing many versions. For example, a simple MNIST Fashion classifier with three layers (300, 100, 10 neurons) has ~267,000 parameters requiring ~1 MB for serialised storage. In contrast, OpenAI's GPT-3 (2020) has ~175 billion weights and needs ~700 GB in memory — an order of magnitude more than high-end desktop computers at the time.
  • Memory footprint: The RAM required to load and run the model, constraining what hardware can be used.
  • Energy consumption per prediction: On given hardware, this can seriously constrain deployment on mobile and battery-powered devices. In data centres, energy costs and cooling needs for repeated inference on large models add up significantly.
Cost per Prediction — The Economic Viability Gate

Cost per prediction captures the total operating cost of serving a model (including training cost amortised across predictions). If revenue per prediction does not exceed cost per prediction, the model is simply not economically viable. For example, Microsoft reportedly initially lost an average of $20 per paying customer per month on their $10 GitHub Copilot subscription due to high inference costs. Similarly, Netflix engineers declined to productionise competition-winning recommendation models because "the additional accuracy gains… did not seem to justify the engineering effort needed."

Deterministic vs. Non-Deterministic Predictions

Almost all traditional ML models are deterministic during inference — the same input always produces the same output (same decision tree path, same neural network computations). Deterministic inference simplifies monitoring and debugging. Many generative models intentionally introduce non-determinism by sampling from probability distributions during inference to produce diverse outputs, but this is usually optional and can be disabled.

Training-Related Quality Attributes

When models must be retrained regularly, quality attributes of the training process become critical system concerns:

  • Training latency: How long it takes to train or retrain a model. Some algorithms scale better to larger datasets or more features than others.
  • Hardware requirements: Memory, CPU, and GPU needs vary widely. Deep learning benefits substantially from GPUs, while traditional algorithms may not.
  • Training cost: For large models, costs can be extraordinary — GPT-3 is estimated at $4–12 million for a single training run (~355 GPU-years). However, high training costs may be amortised if the model is used extensively and not frequently retrained (as with foundation models).
  • Incremental training: Some algorithms (e.g., neural networks) support incremental training — continuing to train with new data without restarting from scratch. Others (e.g., standard decision trees) require examining all training data at once and must retrain from scratch when data changes.
  • Reproducibility of training: Deep neural networks are highly non-deterministic in training (random seeds, timing, batching differences) and may produce models with substantially different accuracy from the same hyperparameters. In contrast, basic decision tree algorithms are entirely deterministic given the same data and hyperparameters.

4.4.4 Explainability (ML-Specific)

Explainability

The ability for human beings to understand model decisions and predictions — the reasoning behind a particular output. Explainability is needed when humans need to understand the model; it is less relevant for system-to-system communication (e.g., two agents interacting).

When Explainability Matters
  • A bank rejects a loan application. The applicant has a right to know why.
  • A medical diagnosis AI flags a patient as high-risk. The doctor needs to understand the reasoning.
  • A hiring system rejects a candidate. Regulatory requirements may demand an explanation.

4.4.5 Robustness (ML-Specific)

Robustness

A system's ability to maintain performance despite noisy, incomplete, or adversarial inputs. Robustness is especially critical in ML systems because, unlike traditional software, input data patterns change over time.

In traditional software, the same input always produces the same output. In ML, the model must handle evolving, noisy, and sometimes adversarial data.

Robustness Examples
  1. Fraud Detection: A customer's transaction pattern changes over years. A static rule (“flag transactions > INR 50,000/month”) will fail. The model must continuously adapt to changing patterns with potentially noisy or incomplete transaction data.
  2. Self-Driving Car: On a sunny day in Bangalore, the vision system works perfectly. But in heavy fog in Delhi (November–January), under poor lighting or heavy rain — can the car still detect lanes, traffic signals, and obstacles? This is robustness.
  3. Speech Recognition: Does the system work reasonably well despite significant background noise? Are there effective pre-processing filters to remove noise before recognition?

4.4.6 Fairness (ML-Specific)

Fairness

The ML model produces unbiased outcomes across different user groups or demographics. Fairness exists as a QA specifically because ML systems learn from data, and data can encode historical biases.

Amazon Hiring Bias (2013–2018)

Amazon automated resume screening using ML. The training data was predominantly from male applicants (reflecting the tech industry's historical demographics). Result: the model systematically penalised female applicants.

Bias = inability to learn the true pattern (instead learning a spurious demographic correlation).

This is a classic example of why fairness must be explicitly addressed:

  • Underfitting ≈ High Bias: Model is too simple to capture the real pattern, falls back on demographic proxies.
  • Overfitting ≈ High Variance: Model memorises training data (including its biases) and fails on new data.

4.4.7 Data Drift Adaptability (ML-Specific)

Data Drift

The ability of the system to adapt to changes in the statistical properties of input data over time. Data drift occurs when the distribution of incoming data differs from the distribution the model was trained on.

4.4.8 Model Drift Monitoring (ML-Specific)

Model Drift

The degradation of model performance in production over time. Even if the model was excellent at deployment, its accuracy may decline as real-world data patterns evolve.

COVID Model Drift — A Real Example

Phase 1 (Post COVID Wave 1, September 2021):

  • 1,200 patient records, labelled dataset.
  • Classification: Recovered vs. Expired.
  • Random Forest achieved 92% accuracy — deployed to production.

Phase 2 (COVID Wave 2):

  • Virus mutated — new symptoms, different severity patterns.
  • A new output class was needed: Shifted (patient transferred to another centre due to ventilator shortage).
  • New features became important (e.g., whether Remdesivir was administered).
  • The same Random Forest model was no longer accurate for the new data distribution.
  • Model drift occurred — necessitating retraining with updated data and features.

Key insight: Model drift is often driven by data drift. When the underlying data distribution changes, the model's assumptions become invalid.

Ground Truth and Drift Detection

How and when to detect drift:

  1. The same client data used for predictions is also stored as ground truth data.
  2. Data scientists/ML engineers perform continuous monitoring on this ground truth.

4.4.9 Reproducibility

Reproducibility

Using the same training process, the system should consistently produce the same results under ideal conditions. This is important for both ML and non-ML systems but has unique challenges in ML due to stochastic elements (random seeds, GPU non-determinism, data shuffling).

4.5 Introduction to System Architecture

4.5.1 Software Architecture vs. System Architecture

Software Architecture (IEEE Definition)

“The software architecture of a program or computing system is the structure or structures of the system which comprise software elements, the externally visible properties of those elements, and the relationships among them.”

This definition has remained valid for over 5 decades (1970s to present) and applies to every software architecture from monoliths to microservices. Its beauty lies in its generality:

  • Elements: Individual components (e.g., each microservice in Netflix)
  • Properties: What each element does and exposes
  • Relationships: How elements communicate (synchronous? asynchronous? via message queue?)
System Architecture

Architecture that includes hardware + software + AI/ML + infrastructure + communication components of a system. For ML systems, we typically work with system architecture rather than pure software architecture, because ML systems involve hardware (sensors, GPUs), ML components (models, embeddings), and infrastructure (cloud, Kubernetes).

4.6 Introduction to Architectural and Design Patterns

4.6.1 What is a Pattern?

Pattern

A pattern is a solution to a problem in a context. It is a proven, reusable approach that has been validated through decades of practice by architects and designers worldwide.

Everyday Analogy: Class Timetable
  • Context: The university is running an AIML M.Tech programme with multiple courses.
  • Problem: How to schedule classes for 100+ students across multiple courses?
  • Bad solution (ad hoc): Professor sends email 5 minutes before class: “I'll take class now.” This doesn't scale, creates conflicts, and is unreliable.
  • Pattern (solution): Create a fixed timetable — every Saturday 10:30–12:30 is SA for ML. Predictable, scalable, shared understanding.

The timetable is a “pattern” — a proven solution to the scheduling problem in the academic context.

RAG = Pipe and Filter + CQRS

The Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) architecture is a combination of two well-established patterns:

  1. Pipe and Filter: Documents → Chunking → Embedding → Vector DB (a sequential pipeline).
  2. CQRS: The indexing pipeline (write path — storing embeddings) is separated from the query pipeline (read path — retrieving and generating answers).

These are not new inventions for ML — they are proven software engineering patterns applied to a new domain.

4.6.4 Concrete Pattern Examples (from Textbook)

The Heartbeat Tactic (Architecture Pattern for Availability)

Name: Heartbeat (aka dead-man timer).

Intent: Detect when a component is unavailable to trigger mitigations or repair.

Motivation: Detect with low latency when a component or server becomes unavailable to automatically restart it or redirect traffic.

Solution: The observed component sends heartbeat messages to a monitoring component in regular predictable intervals. When the monitoring component does not receive the message, it assumes the observed component is unavailable and initiates corrective actions.

Trade-offs: Higher detection latency can be achieved at the cost of higher network traffic with more frequent heartbeat messages; higher confidence in detection can be achieved at the cost of lower latency by waiting for multiple missing heartbeats. Alternatives include the ping/echo tactic where the monitoring component requests responses.

The Feature Store Pattern (ML Design Pattern for Reuse)

Name: Feature Store.

Intent: Reuse features across projects by decoupling feature creation from model development and serving.

Motivation: The same feature engineering code is needed during model training and model serving; inconsistencies are dangerous. Some features are expensive to compute but useful across multiple projects. Data scientists often lack a systematic mechanism for feature reuse.

Solution: Separate feature engineering code and reuse it both in the training pipeline and the model inference infrastructure. Catalog features with metadata to make them discoverable. Cache computed features used in multiple projects.

Benefits: Reusable features across projects; avoiding redundant feature computations; preventing training-serving skew; central versioning of features; separation of concerns.

Costs: Non-trivial infrastructure; extra engineering overhead in data science projects.

4.6.5 CQRS: A Preview

CQRS — Command Query Responsibility Segregation

The core insight: separate the read model from the write model.

Why? Research shows that on platforms like Instagram and Facebook:

  • ∼70–80% of operations are reads (browsing, scrolling)
  • ∼20–30% of operations are writes (posting, commenting)

If reads vastly outnumber writes, you should scale the read system more aggressively than the write system. Having a single database for both creates bottlenecks.

Connection to Flipkart failure: During Big Billion Day, millions were reading (browsing products) and writing (placing orders) to the same monolithic database. CQRS, by separating these concerns, is now a key enabler of successful flash sales.

4.6.6 Architecturally Significant Requirements (ASR)

ASR — Architecturally Significant Requirements

Not all requirements equally impact the architecture. ASRs are the subset of functional and quality requirements that are most critical for architectural decisions. Due to cost and complexity constraints, architects typically focus on the top 3–4 quality attributes most important for their application and design the architecture around those.

Industry Examples of Top Quality Attributes
Banking: Security, Consistency, Availability
Automotive: Performance, Safety, Reliability
Telecom: Performance, Scalability, Security
Medical: Usability, Safety, Reliability
Cloud SaaS: Scalability, Availability, Performance
Supply Chain: Availability, Performance, Reliability

4.6.7 Trade-offs in Architecture

No Silver Bullet

Architecture is about trade-offs. There is no single “best” architecture.

  • Amazon Prime Video moved from microservices back to monolith (cost savings, reduced complexity for their specific use case).
  • Netflix continues with microservices (their scale and complexity demand it).
  • Both are valid decisions — the right architecture depends on requirements.

Golden rule: Architecture follows from requirements. Don't choose an architecture because it's popular — choose it because it suits your needs.

Design Space Exploration: Constraints and the Pareto Front

Trade-off analysis can be formalised through design space exploration. The design space is the set of all possible designs (choices of ML algorithms, hyperparameters, hardware, feature engineering investment, data collection effort, etc.).

  1. Hard constraints reduce the design space: Any solution that does not meet a non-negotiable constraint is discarded. For example, cost per prediction cannot exceed $0.001; inference latency must stay under 40 ms for real-time video at 25 fps. These constraints eliminate clearly infeasible designs immediately.
  2. Dominated solutions are removed: If design A is worse than design B on all quality attributes, A does not need further consideration — it is dominated by B.
  3. Pareto front: The remaining designs form the Pareto front — where each design is better than every other design on at least one quality attribute, but worse on at least one other. For example, one model may be more accurate but costlier per prediction; another may be more explainable but less accurate.

Which design to choose on the Pareto front depends on the relative importance of the involved qualities. In practice, this involves negotiation between stakeholders (data scientists, engineers, business owners, legal teams) and engineering judgment. Being explicit about trade-offs forces discussions about which quality attributes are more important for achieving the overall system goals.
