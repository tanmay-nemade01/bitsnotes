4.2 Why Bandits Are Not Enough: The Need for States

In a multi-armed bandit, there is no notion of a state. You simply face a fixed set of actions and pull one of them, irrespective of the world’s current configuration. But real life is not like that.

Example: Driving Up a Steep Hill

Imagine an autonomous car driving uphill. There is a vehicle ahead, the road is steep, the asphalt is wet. The decision (accelerate / brake / turn / hold) depends on the state of the world, not just on a fixed list of options. The next time you’re at the same hill but with no vehicle ahead, the optimal action could be very different.

Example: Other Real-World State-Dependent Decisions
  • Soccer-playing robot: every action depends on positions of teammates, ball, opponents.
  • Portfolio management: buy/sell/hold depends on market state, company state, your profile.
  • Game of Chess: every move depends on the board configuration.
Key Idea: Moving from Bandits to Full RL

In full RL, we always say: “Given a state, take an action.” The action is never decoupled from the state. This is the fundamental shift from the bandit setting to the full RL problem.

2. The Agent–Environment Interface

The agent–environment interface is the heart of any RL problem. The agent is the learner; the environment is everything else.

Figure 1: The agent–environment interaction loop.

4.4 The Agent-Environment Boundary

A fundamental design question in RL is: where do you draw the line between the agent and the environment? Sutton & Barto provide a clear principle:

Key Idea: The Boundary Principle

“Anything that cannot be changed arbitrarily by the agent is considered to be outside of it and thus part of its environment.”

This means the boundary is drawn at the limit of the agent’s absolute control, not at the limit of its knowledge. For example, a robot’s motors, mechanical linkages, and sensors are typically considered part of the environment, even though they are physically inside the robot’s body. The agent may know exactly how its environment works and still face a difficult RL problem — just as we may know how a Rubik’s cube works but still struggle to solve it.

Practical Consequence: Reward Is Always External

The reward signal defines the task facing the agent and must therefore be beyond its ability to change arbitrarily. Even if reward computations happen inside the robot’s physical body, they are considered part of the environment. This is why we always say the agent receives reward from the environment rather than generating it internally.

4.5 The Interaction Sequence

At each time step t:

  1. The agent observes state \( S_t \).
  2. The agent takes action \( A_t \).
  3. The environment responds with reward \( R_{t+1} \) and the next state \( S_{t+1} \).

This produces the trajectory \( S_0, A_0, R_1, S_1, A_1, R_2, S_2, A_2, R_3, S_3, \dots \)

Key Idea: Objective

The agent’s objective is to choose actions in such a way as to maximize the cumulative reward (return) over the long term, not just the immediate reward.

Intuition: Long-term vs. Short-term

You might get a huge immediate reward and then nothing forever after. Alternatively, you might get small but sustained rewards that, summed over time, far exceed that one big reward. RL is all about playing the long game.

4.6 Industrial MDP Examples (from Sutton & Barto)

Example 3.1: Bioreactor

An RL agent controls moment-by-moment temperatures and stirring rates for a bioreactor (a large vat of nutrients and bacteria producing useful chemicals). States: thermocouple and sensory readings (filtered and delayed), plus symbolic inputs representing ingredients and target chemical. Actions: target temperature and stirring rate vectors passed to lower-level controllers. Rewards: moment-by-moment measure of the rate at which the useful chemical is produced. This illustrates that both states and actions are typically structured vectors in real applications, while rewards remain a single scalar.

Example 3.2: Pick-and-Place Robot

A robot arm performs a repetitive pick-and-place task. States: latest readings of joint angles and velocities (low-latency information about mechanical linkages). Actions: voltages applied to each motor at each joint. Rewards: +1 for each object successfully picked up and placed, plus a small negative reward proportional to the moment-to-moment “jerkiness” of motion to encourage smooth movements. This demonstrates how multiple reward components are combined into a scalar signal to balance competing objectives (speed vs. smoothness).

3. The Markov Property

When the agent needs to pick an action at time t, what should it use? The whole history \( S_0, A_0, R_1, S_1, \dots, S_t \) or just the most recent state \( S_t \)? The Markov property addresses this choice.

Definition: Markov Property

A state \( S_t \) is said to satisfy the Markov property if it summarizes all relevant information from the past that is needed to make a decision. Formally:

\[ \Pr(S_{t+1} = s', R_{t+1} = r \mid S_t, A_t) = \Pr(S_{t+1} = s', R_{t+1} = r \mid S_0, A_0, \dots, S_t, A_t) \]

Golden Rule: The future depends only on the present, not on the past. The historical path taken to reach this state does not matter.

Intuition: The Chess Board

Imagine stepping into a chess game mid-way to play for White. You do not need to know the history of the previous 40 moves to choose your next best move. You only need to look at the current arrangement of pieces on the board. This board snapshot is a Markov state; it encapsulates all historical relevance into the present moment.

Analogy: Data Architecture Perspective

In a layered system like a Data Vault or Medallion architecture, a well-modeled "Gold" dimension table encapsulates business reality. A downstream transformation or BI dashboard query only needs this current snapshot. It does not need to parse legacy code or re-process raw historical logs from the Bronze layer. The current state is sufficient.

Why Markov is Crucial for DRL
  • Computational Sanity: Neural networks only need to receive the current observation, saving substantial memory and compute. There is no need to store or process an infinite, ever-growing log of past events.
  • Mathematical Foundation: It enables Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) to mathematically compute expected future rewards, supporting algorithms like Q-learning and PPO.

4.7 What a State Should (and Should Not) Be Expected to Provide

Sutton & Barto emphasise an important subtlety: a state signal should not be expected to inform the agent of everything. If the agent plays blackjack, we shouldn’t expect it to know the next card in the deck. If it answers the phone, we shouldn’t expect it to know who the caller is in advance. There is always hidden state information in the environment. As they put it:

Golden Principle

“We don’t fault an agent for not knowing something that matters, but only for having known something and then forgotten it.”

A Markov state summarises past sensations compactly while retaining all relevant information. This requires more than immediate sensations but never more than the complete history of all past sensations. The art of state design lies in finding the sweet spot between these two extremes.

4.8 The Formal Comparison: General vs. Markov Dynamics

To appreciate the power of the Markov property, compare the two equations side by side:

General (Non-Markov) Dynamics: \[ \Pr(R_{t+1} = r, S_{t+1} = s' \mid S_0, A_0, R_1, \dots, S_{t-1}, A_{t-1}, R_t, S_t, A_t) \]

This depends on the entire history. Impossible to store or compute for long sequences.

Markov Dynamics: \[ p(s', r \mid s, a) = \Pr(R_{t+1} = r, S_{t+1} = s' \mid S_t = s, A_t = a) \]

This depends on only the current state and action. The state is a sufficient statistic that makes the full history unnecessary. The difference is not merely computational — it is what makes the entire mathematical framework of RL tractable.

Example 3.5: Pole-Balancing State (Sutton & Barto)

In the cart-pole task, a Markov state would need to specify exactly: the position and velocity of the cart, the angle of the pole, and the angular velocity. In an idealised system, these four variables are sufficient to predict all future behaviour given the actions taken. In practice, real sensors introduce distortion and delay, and physical effects (bending of the pole, bearing temperatures, backlash) cause violations of the Markov property.

Yet early reinforcement learning systems successfully solved the pole-balancing task using coarse, non-Markov state representations (e.g., dividing cart position into just three regions: right, left, middle). This coarse representation may have actually helped learning by forcing the agent to ignore irrelevant fine distinctions. The lesson: strict adherence to the Markov property is not always required for practical success.

Example 3.6: Draw Poker (Sutton & Barto)

In draw poker, each player knows their own cards but can only guess at others’ hands. A common mistake is to think a Markov state should include the contents of all hands and the deck. But in a fair game, these are fundamentally unobservable from past sensations. The practical state includes: your own cards, the bets made, the number of cards drawn by others, and your memory of opponents’ playing styles (does Ellen bluff? is Joe playing differently late at night?). Human poker players use distinctly non-Markov representations, yet make very good decisions. Sutton & Barto conclude: “The inability to have access to a perfect Markov state representation is probably not a severe problem for a reinforcement learning agent.”

When the Property Breaks (POMDPs)

In reality, systems are often only partially observable (POMDPs). For example, a single static frame of Pong tells you the ball's position, but not its velocity or direction. The state \( S_t \) is incomplete, and the future depends on past frames.

Solution: DRL practitioners use frame stacking (e.g., passing the last 4 frames as a single input) to reconstruct velocity and make the input state Markovian.

4.9 Defining the Grid World as an MDP

States: Each cell of the grid is a state. The state simply tells the agent its location (row, col).

Actions: 4 actions: Up, Down, Left, Right (or N/E/S/W).

Noisy Actions:

  • With probability 0.8, the action succeeds as intended (e.g., Up actually moves up).
  • With probability 0.2, the action goes sideways (split equally between the perpendicular directions).
  • Walls: if the resulting movement hits a wall or boundary, the agent stays in place.

Rewards:

  • \( -1 \) at every step (encourages reaching the goal quickly—like a draining battery).
  • +1 for reaching the goal state.
  • \( -1 \) for falling into a pit.
Intuition: Why give a \( -1 \) at every step?

This is a step penalty. Without it, an agent could wander around forever and never be punished. The step penalty acts like a battery clock—it forces the agent to find the shortest path.

4.10 Design Choice: Should the State Include the Battery?

If you want the agent to also respond to its battery level (e.g., do nothing if battery is low and wait for someone to recharge it), then the state must include the battery level: State = (position, battery level).

If you don’t care about battery and just want fastest navigation, location alone is enough.

Key Idea: Modelling Wisdom

A state should include everything the agent needs to make a good decision, and nothing more. Designing the state space is one of the most important parts of formulating an RL problem.

5. Formal Definition of a Markov Decision Process

Definition: Markov Decision Process (MDP)

A (finite) Markov Decision Process is a tuple \( \langle S, A, p, R, \gamma \rangle \) where:

  • \( S \): the (finite) set of states.
  • \( A \): the (finite) set of actions.
  • \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \): the model dynamics — probability of transitioning to state \( s' \) with reward \( r \) given current state \( s \) and action \( a \).
  • \( R \): the reward function (implicit in p).
  • \( \gamma \in [0, 1] \): the discount factor.
Caution: Finite MDP Assumption

For this course (and to make the math tractable in lectures), we focus on finite MDPs where \( S \), \( A \), and the set of possible rewards are all finite (countable). The general theory extends to continuous spaces, but the intuition is the same.

4.11 Element 1: States (\( S \))

The state must capture everything the agent needs to make a good decision (Markov property). Examples:

  • Grid world: cell location.
  • Atari game: stack of last 3–5 frames (so velocity/direction is encoded).
  • Traffic signal: queue lengths, average waiting times at each lane.

4.12 Element 2: Actions (\( A \))

The set of admissible actions. Important:

  • Actions may be deterministic (action ⇒ exactly one outcome) or stochastic / noisy (multiple possible outcomes).
  • In some MDPs, the action set may depend on the state: \( A(s) \).

4.13 Element 3: Transition Probabilities (Model Dynamics)

This is the most subtle element. The environment behaves probabilistically.

Definition: Model Dynamics

The function \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \doteq \Pr[S_{t+1} = s', R_{t+1} = r \mid S_t = s, A_t = a] \) gives the probability of transitioning to next state \( s' \) with reward \( r \), given that the agent is in state s and takes action a.

This is a four-argument probability table. For any fixed \( (s, a) \):

\( \sum_{s' \in \mathcal{S}} \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} p(s', r \mid s, a) = 1 \).

Intuition: Model Dynamics in Plain English

“If I’m here and I do this, what’s the chance I land there with that reward?” That’s literally the model of the world. If you know it, you know the environment perfectly.

4.13.1 Visualising Transitions

A simple deterministic case: in state \( s \), taking action \( a \), you go to \( s' \) with reward 7 for sure: \( p(s', 7 \mid s, a) = 1 \).

Figure 3: Noisy braking transition.

A stochastic case: pressing the brake in a car may or may not stop the car:

  • \( s \rightarrow \text{action } a \text{ (brake)} \rightarrow s_1 \text{ (stops)} \): \( p = 0.95 \), \( r = 100 \)
  • \( s \rightarrow \text{action } a \text{ (brake)} \rightarrow s_2 \text{ (skids)} \): \( p = 0.05 \), \( r = -1000 \)

So we write: \( p(s_1, 100 \mid s, a) = 0.95, \qquad p(s_2, -1000 \mid s, a) = 0.05 \).

4.14 Element 4: Reward Function

The reward signal \( R_t \) is what guides learning. It is a single scalar number that the environment sends back at every step.

Caution: Reward Signal Must Be Scalar

The environment does not hand back a multi-criteria table of feedback. It returns one number. Designing a good scalar reward (especially when there are competing criteria) is often the hardest part of an RL formulation.

Intuition: Be Careful What You Reward!

“If you reward wrong actions, you’re bringing up a wrong agent.” The reward signal must clearly express what good behaviour means. Misdesigned rewards lead to bizarre exploitative behaviour (the reward-hacking problem).

4.14.1 Where Do Rewards Come From?

  • In games (e.g., chess, Atari): rewards typically come directly from the environment (score change, win/lose signal).
  • In real-world tasks (autonomous driving, traffic control): the engineer must design the reward function by combining multiple raw signals (speed, smoothness, lane adherence, safety, etc.) into a single scalar. This requires domain expertise.

6. Quantities Derived from Model Dynamics

Once you have \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \), you can compute many useful things by summing (marginalising) appropriately.

4.15 State-Transition Probability

The probability of landing in state \( s' \) (regardless of reward) given \( (s, a) \):

\[ p(s' \mid s, a) = \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} p(s', r \mid s, a) \]

4.16 Expected Reward Given \( (s, a) \)

\[ r(s, a) \doteq \mathbb{E}[R_{t+1} \mid S_t = s, A_t = a] = \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} r \sum_{s' \in \mathcal{S}} p(s', r \mid s, a) \]

4.17 Expected Reward Given \( (s, a, s') \)

\[ r(s, a, s') \doteq \mathbb{E}[R_{t+1} \mid S_t = s, A_t = a, S_{t+1} = s'] = \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} r \frac{p(s', r \mid s, a)}{p(s' \mid s, a)} \]

4.18 Worked Example: Promotion

Let me use an intuitive example. Suppose you are in state \( R \) (currently a Manager), and the actions are apply-for-promotion (\( \text{start} \)) or don’t-apply (\( \text{stop} \)).

Table 1: Model dynamics for the promotion problem.
s a \( s' \) r \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \)
\( R \)\( \text{start} \)\( W \) (promoted)\( +4 \) (40% raise)\( 0.6 \)
\( R \)\( \text{start} \)\( W \) (promoted)\( +2 \) (20% raise)\( 0.2 \)
\( R \)\( \text{start} \)\( R \) (rejected)\( 0 \)\( 0.2 \)
\( R \)\( \text{stop} \)\( T \) (terminal)\( +1 \)\( 1.0 \)

Sanity check: For each \( (s, a) \), the probabilities must sum to 1.

  • \( (R, \text{start}) \): \( 0.6 + 0.2 + 0.2 = 1 \). ✓
  • \( (R, \text{stop}) \): \( 1.0 \). ✓

Q1: What is the probability of getting promoted (landing in \( W \)) given you apply?

\( p(W \mid R, \text{start}) = \sum_{r} p(W, r \mid R, \text{start}) = 0.6 + 0.2 = 0.8 \).

Q2: What is the expected raise (reward) given you apply?

\( r(R, \text{start}) = 4 \cdot 0.6 + 2 \cdot 0.2 + 0 \cdot 0.2 = 2.4 + 0.4 + 0 = 2.8 \).

Q3: What is the expected raise given that you actually got promoted?

\( r(R, \text{start}, W) = 4 \cdot \frac{0.6}{0.8} + 2 \cdot \frac{0.2}{0.8} = 3 + 0.5 = 3.5 \).

Key Idea: Takeaway

Model dynamics is one big table. From it, you can derive everything else by summing over the variables you don’t care about (marginalisation).

7. Worked Examples of MDP Formulation

4.19 Example 1: Atari Video Game

Example: Classic Atari Game
  • State: the last k frames of the game screen (typically k = 3 or 4); this captures direction and velocity of moving objects.
  • Actions: joystick directions Up, Down, Left, Right (plus possibly Fire).
  • Reward: change in the game’s score from one frame to the next.
  • Transition: dictated by the game’s emulator—you apply an action and the emulator gives you the next frame.
  • Policy: the strategy the agent learns to map states to joystick actions.

4.20 Example 2: Traffic Signal Control

Example: Smart Traffic Signal
  • State: for each lane: current queue length and average waiting time. Both as vectors.
  • Actions: (a) extend current phase, or (b) switch to a new admissible phase. There may be 8 admissible signal phases.
  • Reward: a weighted scalar combination of (negated) average delay, (negated) max queue length, and number of vehicles cleared per unit time. Engineered by the designer.
  • Transition: the world responds based on physical traffic dynamics—not a simple finite table; learned through interaction.
Intuition: Designer’s Role in Real Systems

For toy MDPs we can write out a full transition table. But in real systems (driving, robotics, traffic), the dynamics are too complex to enumerate. We either learn them, simulate them, or work model-free.

8. The Reward Hypothesis

Definition: Reward Hypothesis (Sutton)

“All of what we mean by goals and purposes can be well thought of as the maximization of the expected value of the cumulative sum of a received scalar signal (called reward).”

To dissect this:

  1. There is a scalar reward signal \( R_t \) at every step.
  2. We accumulate it into a cumulative sum (the return).
  3. We seek to maximize the expected value of that return.
Key Idea: The Three Ingredients
  1. Reward (scalar, per-step).
  2. Cumulative sum of rewards (the return).
  3. Expected value of the return (averaged over randomness).
Critical Principle: Reward Communicates What, Not How

Sutton & Barto emphasise a point that newcomers often get wrong: “The reward signal is not the place to impart to the agent prior knowledge about how to achieve what we want it to do.” A chess-playing agent should be rewarded only for actually winning, not for achieving subgoals such as taking opponent’s pieces or controlling the centre. If subgoals were rewarded, the agent might find a way to achieve them without achieving the real goal — for example, taking pieces even at the cost of losing the game. The reward signal is your way of communicating what you want achieved, not how you want it achieved. Better places for imparting prior knowledge are the initial policy, initial value function, or other influences on these.

9. Return: Cumulative Reward

4.22 Simple (Undiscounted) Return

Starting at time t, the return is the sum of all rewards going forward:

\[ G_t \doteq R_{t+1} + R_{t+2} + R_{t+3} + \dots = \sum_{k=0}^{T-t-1} R_{t+1+k} \]

where \( T \) is the (possibly infinite) terminal time step.

4.23 The Problem with Undiscounted Return

If the task continues forever (\( T = \infty \)), the simple sum can be infinite, and comparing infinities makes no sense.

Intuition: Why we want a finite number

Suppose you want to choose between action A and action B in state \( s \). You compute the return for each and pick the larger. If both are infinite, you can’t compare them. We need a way to keep the sum finite while still preferring earlier rewards a bit more.

4.24 Discounted Return

Definition: Discounted Return \[ G_t \doteq R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \dots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1} \]

\( \gamma \in [0, 1] \) is the discount factor (also called the discount rate).

Interpretation:

  • Each additional step into the future is weighted by an extra factor of \( \gamma \).
  • Rewards far away are heavily down-weighted.
Intuition: Why discount the future?

“Rs. 1000 today vs. Rs. 1000 two years from now”—these are clearly not the same. There’s inflation, uncertainty, opportunity cost. The discount factor \( \gamma \) formalises that the present is more valuable than the same amount in the future.

4.25 Convergence of the Discounted Sum

If \( \gamma < 1 \) and the rewards are bounded, then \( G_t \) is finite, even for an infinite-horizon task.

Proof Sketch.

Suppose \( |R_t| \le R_{\text{max}} \) for all \( t \). Then

\[ |G_t| \le \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{\text{max}} = \frac{R_{\text{max}}}{1 - \gamma} < \infty \]
Example: Constant Reward 1, \( \gamma = 0.9 \) \[ G = 1 + 0.9 + 0.81 + 0.729 + \dots = \frac{1}{1 - 0.9} = 10 \]

Despite infinitely many terms, the return is just 10. Finite. Comparable.

4.26 Two Extreme Cases of \( \gamma \)

  • \( \gamma = 0 \): the agent is myopic / hedonist—only the immediate reward matters. \( G_t = R_{t+1} \).
  • \( \gamma = 1 \) (when valid): the agent treats all future rewards equally, as if they were happening now (“be a yogi”).
  • Typical values used in practice: \( \gamma \in [0.9, 0.99] \).

4.27 Recursive Form of \( G_t \)

A very important reformulation:

\[ \begin{aligned} G_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \dots \\ &= R_{t+1} + \gamma (R_{t+2} + \gamma R_{t+3} + \dots) \\ &= R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]
Key Idea: Recursive Return

\( G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1} \)

This is the seed of the Bellman equation, which we will use repeatedly going forward.

4.28 Episodic vs. Continuing Tasks

Definition: Episodic Task

A task that naturally ends—e.g., a single game of chess, one delivery run of a robot. The trajectory is finite and ends in a terminal state.

Definition: Continuing Task

A task that goes on indefinitely—e.g., a process control system that runs 24/7. There is no terminal state.

4.29 Unified Notation: The Absorbing State Trick

Sutton & Barto introduce an elegant convention that lets us use a single return formula for both episodic and continuing tasks. The trick: treat episode termination as entering a special absorbing state that transitions only to itself and generates only rewards of zero:

Unified Return via Absorbing State \[ G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1} \]

For an episodic task with \( T = 3 \) and undiscounted rewards +1, +1, +1:

\( R_1 = +1 \rightarrow R_2 = +1 \rightarrow R_3 = +1 \rightarrow [\text{absorbing}] \, R_4 = 0 \rightarrow R_5 = 0 \rightarrow \dots \)

The infinite sum automatically stops accumulating after the terminal state because all subsequent rewards are zero. This single formula now covers both cases, and we can even use \( \gamma = 1 \) for episodic tasks since the sum remains finite.

Intuition: When to Use Discounting
  • Continuing task: you must discount (\( \gamma < 1 \)) to keep \( G_t \) finite.
  • Episodic, short horizon: you may set \( \gamma = 1 \) or use a high \( \gamma \) (e.g., 0.99); the sum is anyway finite.

Throughout this course we will adopt the discounted-return formalism as the default, even for episodic tasks. You can always recover the undiscounted case by setting \( \gamma = 1 \).

Example: Cart-Pole

A cart on a track must move left/right to balance an upside-down pole on top of it.

  • Is it episodic (ends when the pole falls) or continuing (ideally balances forever)? You can model it either way.
  • If continuing: must use \( \gamma < 1 \) or the return is infinite.
  • Reward: +1 for every step the pole stays balanced (encourages longer survival).

10. Policy

Definition: Policy

A policy \( \pi \) is a mapping from states to a probability distribution over actions:

\[ \pi(a \mid s) = \Pr(A_t = a \mid S_t = s) \]

For a deterministic policy, \( \pi(s) \) simply returns one action with probability 1.

Intuition: Policy = the Agent’s Behaviour

A policy is the agent’s behaviour rule. It tells the agent: “When you see this state, do this action (with some probability).” Learning in RL means finding a good policy.

4.31 Stochastic vs. Deterministic Policies

Stochastic policy (example for grid world):

\( \pi(\text{up} \mid s) = 0.9, \quad \pi(\text{left} \mid s) = 0.04, \quad \pi(\text{right} \mid s) = 0.03, \quad \pi(\text{down} \mid s) = 0.03 \).

Deterministic policy:

\( \pi(\text{up} \mid s) = 1, \text{ everything else } = 0 \).

i.e., “in state \( s \), always go up.”

11. Value Functions

Now we get to the crown jewel: how good is a state? How good is taking an action in a state?

4.33 The State-Value Function \( v_\pi(s) \)

Definition: State-Value Function

The value of state \( s \) under policy \( \pi \) is the expected return starting from \( s \) and acting according to \( \pi \):

\[ v_\pi(s) \doteq \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid S_t = s] = \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1} \;\middle|\; S_t = s\right] \]
Intuition: A Position’s Worth Depends on Behaviour

A CEO position has high potential, but its actual value depends on the behaviour of the person holding it. A bad CEO leads to ruin; a great CEO leads to spectacular returns. The same state has different values under different policies—hence the subscript \( \pi \).

4.34 The Action-Value Function \( q_\pi(s, a) \)

Definition: Action-Value Function

The value of taking action \( a \) in state \( s \) and then following policy \( \pi \) is:

\[ q_\pi(s, a) \doteq \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid S_t = s, A_t = a] \]
Key Idea: Why Both \( v_\pi \) and \( q_\pi \)?
  • \( v_\pi(s) \) tells you how good a state is.
  • \( q_\pi(s, a) \) tells you how good taking a specific action is, even if you weren’t going to under \( \pi \).
  • \( q_\pi \) is more directly useful for decision making: just pick the action with the highest \( q \) value.

4.35 Relationship Between \( v_\pi \) and \( q_\pi \)

\[ v_\pi(s) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi(a \mid s) q_\pi(s, a) \]

In other words: the state’s value is the policy-weighted average of the action values from that state.

4.36 Estimating Value Functions from Experience

Sutton & Barto describe two fundamental approaches to estimating value functions, foreshadowing the algorithm families covered in the next lectures:

Monte Carlo Estimation

If an agent follows policy \( \pi \) and maintains an average, for each state encountered, of the actual returns that have followed that state, then the average will converge to \( v_\pi(s) \) as the number of visits approaches infinity. Similarly, separate averages for each action in a state converge to \( q_\pi(s, a) \). These are called Monte Carlo methods because they involve averaging over many random samples of actual returns. This approach does not bootstrap — it waits until the end of an episode to compute the true return.

The Bootstrapping Alternative

If there are very many states, keeping separate averages for each state becomes impractical. Instead, the agent maintains \( v_\pi \) and \( q_\pi \) as parameterised functions (e.g., neural networks) and adjusts the parameters to better match observed returns. This is the bridge to function approximation and deep RL. The key idea of bootstrapping — updating estimates based on other estimates — is what distinguishes temporal-difference learning from Monte Carlo methods.

4.37 The Backup Diagram Concept

Sutton & Barto introduce backup diagrams as a visual language for understanding how value information propagates through an MDP. Unlike transition graphs (which show all possible dynamics), backup diagrams show the update operations at the heart of RL algorithms:

  • State nodes (open circles): represent states whose values are being computed.
  • Action nodes (solid circles): represent state–action pairs.
  • Arcs from state to action: represent the policy (averaging or max, depending on context).
  • Arcs from action to state: represent the environment dynamics \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \).
  • Time flows downward: the root is at the top, successors below.

These diagrams will be used throughout subsequent lectures to visualise and compare different algorithms (DP, Monte Carlo, TD learning). Each algorithm has a characteristic backup diagram that captures exactly what information it uses for updates.

13. Bellman Expectation Equations

The value functions we defined earlier satisfy self-consistency equations called Bellman equations, named after Richard Bellman. These equations express the value of a state in terms of the values of its possible successor states, linking immediate rewards to future returns.

Definition: Bellman Equation for State-Value Function \( v_\pi \) \[ v_\pi(s) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s' \in \mathcal{S}, r \in \mathcal{R}} p(s', r \mid s, a) [r + \gamma v_\pi(s')] \]

This equation says: the value of state \( s \) is the average over all actions the policy might take, of the average over all possible next states and rewards, of the immediate reward plus the discounted value of the next state.

Why This Works: Derivation

Starting from the recursive return \( G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1} \):

\( v_\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid S_t = s] \)

\( = \mathbb{E}_\pi[R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1} \mid S_t = s] \)

\( = \mathbb{E}_\pi[R_{t+1} + \gamma v_\pi(S_{t+1}) \mid S_t = s] \)

Expanding the expectation over policy and environment dynamics gives the full Bellman equation above. This single equation is the foundation of nearly every RL algorithm.

4.39 Backup Diagram for \( v_\pi \)

A backup diagram visualises how value is propagated. Starting from state \( s \) (open circle), the policy \( \pi \) selects an action \( a \) (solid circle). The environment then produces reward \( r \) and next state \( s' \) (open circle) according to \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \). The value at \( s \) is the weighted average (over policy × dynamics) of \( r + \gamma v_\pi(s') \).

Think of it as: value flows backward from future states to the present. The value of a state is built up from the values of states that can follow it.

Definition: Bellman Equation for Action-Value Function \( q_\pi \) \[ q_\pi(s, a) = \sum_{s' \in \mathcal{S}, r \in \mathcal{R}} p(s', r \mid s, a) \left[ r + \gamma \sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}} \pi(a' \mid s') q_\pi(s', a') \right] \]

Here, after taking fixed action \( a \) in state \( s \), the environment moves to \( s' \) with reward r. Then the policy \( \pi \) chooses the next action \( a' \) from \( s' \). The value of \( (s, a) \) averages over all possible transitions and subsequent policy choices.

4.41 Verifying the Bellman Equation

Let’s verify the Bellman equation for an ordinary cell (row 3, col 3, value 0.7). Its four neighbours: up = 2.3, right = 0.4, down = \( -0.4 \), left = 0.7. Each action has probability 0.25, reward for non-boundary moves is 0, and \( \gamma = 0.9 \):

\( v_\pi(3,3) = \frac{1}{4} \left[ (0 + 0.9 \cdot 2.3) + (0 + 0.9 \cdot 0.4) + (0 + 0.9 \cdot (-0.4)) + (0 + 0.9 \cdot 0.7) \right] \).

\( = \frac{1}{4} [2.07 + 0.36 - 0.36 + 0.63] = 0.675 \approx 0.7 \quad \checkmark \).

Now check special state A (value 8.8). From \( A \), all four actions give reward \( +10 \) and transport to \( A' \) (value \( -1.3 \)):

\( v_\pi(A) = (0.25 \times 4) \left[ 10 + 0.9 \cdot (-1.3) \right] = 10 - 1.17 = 8.83 \approx 8.8 \quad \checkmark \).

Intuition: Why is \( A \)’s value 8.8 and not 10?

The immediate reward is +10, but A teleports the agent to \( A' \), which has a negative value (\( -1.3 \)) under the random policy. The continuation value drags the total down. If the agent were smarter (not random), \( A' \) would have a better value, and \( A \)’s total would be higher.

15. Bellman Optimality Equations

So far we’ve computed values under a given policy \( \pi \). But what we really want is the best possible behaviour. A policy \( \pi \) is better than or equal to \( \pi' \) if \( v_\pi(s) \ge v_{\pi'}(s) \) for all states \( s \). An optimal policy \( \pi_* \) is one that is not worse than any other policy.

Intuitive Example: Golf (Sutton & Barto, Example 3.9)

Consider formulating a hole of golf as an RL problem. State: the location of the ball. Actions: choice of club (putter or driver) and how to aim/swing. Reward: \( -1 \) for each stroke until the ball is in the hole. The value of a state is the negative of the expected number of strokes to the hole.

Under a policy that always uses the putter, the state-value function \( v_{\text{putt}}(s) \) shows concentric contour lines. States on the green have value \( -1 \) (one putt to hole). States within putting range of the green have value \( -2 \) (putt to green, then putt to hole), and so on. Sand traps have value \( -\infty \) because putting cannot get you out of them.

Now consider the optimal action-value function \( q_*(s, \text{driver}) \). Using the driver lets you hit farther but with less accuracy. The \( -1 \) contour is tiny (only very close to the hole). The \( -2 \) contour is much larger (drive to anywhere on the green, then putt). From the starting tee, the optimal sequence is: two drives and one putt = 3 strokes. The action-value function tells you both what to do now (the driver) and what the best possible continuation is afterward.

Definition: Optimal State-Value Function \[ v_*(s) \doteq \max_\pi v_\pi(s), \quad \forall s \in \mathcal{S} \]

This is the best expected return achievable from state \( s \) over all possible policies.

Definition: Optimal Action-Value Function \[ q_*(s, a) \doteq \max_\pi q_\pi(s, a), \quad \forall s \in \mathcal{S}, \, a \in \mathcal{A}(s) \]

This is the best expected return after taking action \( a \) and then behaving optimally forever after.

4.42 Bellman Optimality Equation for \( v_* \)

Definition: Bellman Optimality Equation for \( v_* \) \[ v_*(s) = \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) [r + \gamma v_*(s')] \]

Instead of averaging over the policy (as in the Bellman expectation equation), we now take the maximum over actions. The optimal value of a state equals the best one-step return achievable from that state.

Why \( q_* \) Is Especially Powerful: Model-Free Optimality

There is an important practical distinction between \( v_* \) and \( q_* \):

  • With \( v_* \): you still need to do a one-step lookahead using the dynamics \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \) to find the greedy action. You need the model.
  • With \( q_* \): for any state \( s \), you can simply pick \( \operatorname{argmax}_a q_*(s, a) \) without knowing anything about the environment’s dynamics. The action-value function effectively caches the results of all one-step lookahead searches.

This is why model-free RL algorithms (Q-learning, DQN) learn \( q_* \) rather than \( v_* \) — it makes optimal action selection possible without a model of the world. The cost is that \( q_* \) has \( |S| \times |A| \) entries instead of \( |S| \), requiring more memory and more data.

4.43 Bellman Optimality Equation for \( q_* \)

Definition: Bellman Optimality Equation for \( q_* \) \[ q_*(s, a) = \sum_{s' \in \mathcal{S}, r \in \mathcal{R}} p(s', r \mid s, a) \left[ r + \gamma \max_{a' \in \mathcal{A}(s')} q_*(s', a') \right] \]

The first action \( a \) is fixed (given). After the environment transitions to \( s' \), the agent chooses the best next action \( a' \). The “max” over \( a' \) makes this an optimality equation, not just an expectation.

Intuition: Expectation vs. Optimality
  • Bellman expectation: average over policy \( \pi \rightarrow v_\pi(s) \). Tells you: “if I follow this policy, what do I get?”
  • Bellman optimality: maximise over actions \( \rightarrow v_*(s) \). Tells you: “what’s the best I could possibly do starting here?”

In the backup diagram for \( v_* \), the arc from state to action is labelled “max” instead of representing a policy average. The agent chooses the action that gives the largest expected return.

4.44 Example: Bellman Optimality for the Recycling Robot

Using (3.17) from Sutton & Barto, the Bellman optimality equations for the recycling robot (with states h = high, \( l = \text{low} \) and actions \( s = \text{search} \), \( w = \text{wait} \), \( \text{re} = \text{recharge} \)) are:

For state high: \[ v_*(h) = \max \begin{cases} \alpha[r_s + \gamma v_*(h)] + (1-\alpha)[r_s + \gamma v_*(l)], \\ 1[r_w + \gamma v_*(h)] + 0[r_w + \gamma v_*(l)] \end{cases} \] \[ = \max \begin{cases} r_s + \gamma[\alpha v_*(h) + (1-\alpha)v_*(l)], \\ r_w + \gamma v_*(h) \end{cases} \]
For state low: \[ v_*(l) = \max \begin{cases} \beta r_s - 3(1-\beta) + \gamma[(1-\beta)v_*(h) + \beta v_*(l)], \\ r_w + \gamma v_*(l), \\ \gamma v_*(h) \end{cases} \]

For any choice of \( r_s \), \( r_w \), \( \alpha \), \( \beta \), and \( \gamma \) (with \( 0 \le \gamma < 1 \)), there is exactly one pair of numbers, \( v_*(h) \) and \( v_*(l) \), that simultaneously satisfy these two nonlinear equations. For finite MDPs with N states, the Bellman optimality equation is a system of N equations in N unknowns — one for each state. If the dynamics are known, this system can in principle be solved by any method for systems of nonlinear equations.

4.45 The Greedy Principle with \( v_* \)

Example: Using \( v_* \) for One-Step Lookahead

Suppose you know \( v_*(s') \) for all successor states and the dynamics \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \). Then the optimal action in state \( s \) is simply:

\( a_* = \operatorname{argmax}_a \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) [r + \gamma v_*(s')] \).

You look one step ahead, evaluate each action, and pick the best. If you have \( v_* \), you don’t need to know \( q_* \) directly. This is the greedy action with respect to \( v_* \).

4.46 Worked Lookahead Example

Suppose in state \( s \), two actions are available:

  • Action \( a_1 \): gives reward 2 and moves to \( s_1 \) with probability 1.0. Known \( v_*(s_1) = 5 \).
  • Action \( a_2 \): gives reward 0 and moves to \( s_2 \) with probability 1.0. Known \( v_*(s_2) = 8 \).

With \( \gamma = 0.9 \):

  • \( a_1 \): \( r + \gamma v_*(s_1) = 2 + 0.9 \cdot 5 = 6.5 \)
  • \( a_2 \): \( r + \gamma v_*(s_2) = 0 + 0.9 \cdot 8 = 7.2 \) → greedy choice!

Even though \( a_1 \) gives a higher immediate reward, \( a_2 \) leads to a state with much better long-term prospects. The optimal decision favours the long game.

16. Optimality and Approximation

4.48 Exact Optimality Is Rarely Possible

For small finite MDPs, the Bellman optimality equations can be solved exactly using dynamic programming. But real-world problems — video games, traffic networks, robotics — have enormous state spaces. Storing exact values for every state is impossible.

Intuition: Why Approximation Is Unavoidable
  • Atari games: even with a downsampled 84×84×4-pixel state, the number of distinct screen configurations is astronomical.
  • Traffic control: continuous queue lengths and arrival rates create an infinite-dimensional state.
  • Robotics: joint angles, velocities, sensor readings — all continuous and high-dimensional.

In such cases, the goal shifts from exact optimality to useful approximation. The agent may learn approximate value functions, approximate policies, or use sampled experience rather than complete dynamics. This is the bridge from tabular methods to function approximation and deep reinforcement learning.

4.49 The Curse of Dimensionality and the Power of GPI

Sutton & Barto identify a profound insight: the same framework that makes exact optimality impossible also gives us a way forward. The Bellman optimality equation can be solved approximately in at least three ways highlighted in the text:

Three Roads to Approximation (from Sutton & Barto)
  1. Heuristic Search: Expand the right-hand side of the Bellman optimality equation several times, forming a tree of possibilities up to some depth, then use a heuristic evaluation function at the leaf nodes. Methods like A* search fit this pattern.
  2. Dynamic Programming: Use iterative sweeps through the state space, bootstrapping from value estimates. This requires a complete model but is exponentially faster than direct search in policy space (\( n^m \) policies vs. polynomial time).
  3. Reinforcement Learning: Use actual experienced transitions in place of knowledge of expected transitions — approximately solving the Bellman optimality equation from sampled data. This eliminates the need for a model entirely.

4.50 The Online Advantage of RL

A critical insight from Sutton & Barto: RL's online nature creates a unique advantage for approximation. In approximating optimal behaviour, there may be many states the agent faces with such low probability that selecting suboptimal actions for them has little impact on total reward. Tesauro’s TD-Gammon backgammon player, for example, plays with exceptional skill even though it might make very bad decisions on board configurations that never occur in games against experts. This is a key property that distinguishes RL from other approaches to approximately solving MDPs — it can focus computational effort on frequently encountered states at the expense of rarely seen ones.

Key Idea: The Central Principle

The four ingredients — rewards, transitions, value estimates, and action selection — remain the same whether we are solving a 25-state gridworld exactly or training a deep neural network to play Go. Bellman’s insight — that optimal behaviour satisfies a recursive self-consistency condition — scales from the simplest tabular case to the most complex deep learning systems.
