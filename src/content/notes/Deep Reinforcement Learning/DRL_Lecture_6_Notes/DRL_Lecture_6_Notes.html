1. Foundations: MDPs and the Bellman Equations

Before we can solve Markov Decision Processes with dynamic programming, we need to know exactly what we are solving. This section recaps the core building blocks of reinforcement learning and distinguishes the two forms of the Bellman equation — expectation and optimality — that drive every algorithm in this chapter.

6.1 What an MDP Captures

A Markov Decision Process (MDP) is the mathematical language for describing a reinforcement learning problem. It captures a world where an agent makes decisions, the world responds, and the agent sees the result. Every RL problem you will encounter — from a robot navigating a room to an algorithm recommending products — can be cast as an MDP once you define five things.

Core definition. An MDP is defined by five elements:

  • \( \mathcal{S} \) — the set of all states the agent can be in. We sometimes write \( \mathcal{S}^+ \) for all states including terminal states, and \( \mathcal{S} \) for non-terminal states only.
  • \( \mathcal{A}(s) \) — the set of actions available in state \( s \).
  • \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \) — the transition probability function. Given state \( s \) and action \( a \), this tells us the probability of landing in next state \( s' \) and receiving reward \( r \). This is the model of the environment.
  • \( r \) — the immediate reward received after a transition.
  • \( \gamma \in [0, 1] \) — the discount factor. It weights future rewards versus immediate ones. A value close to 0 makes the agent short-sighted; close to 1 makes it far-sighted.

If a problem is given to you, defining these five elements is the first step toward solving it.

6.2 Policies and Value Functions

A policy \( \pi \) is the agent's behaviour rule. Formally, \( \pi(a \mid s) \) is the probability of taking action \( a \) when in state \( s \). A deterministic policy picks exactly one action per state; a stochastic policy assigns probabilities across multiple actions.

Given a policy, we can ask: how good is each state? The state-value function \( V^\pi(s) \) answers this question. It is the expected cumulative discounted return when starting from state \( s \) and following policy \( \pi \) thereafter:

\[ V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi \left[ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1} \;\middle|\; S_t = s \right] \]

The notation \( \mathbb{E}_\pi \) means "the expectation under policy \( \pi \)" — we average over all the randomness in both the policy's action choices and the environment's transitions. The symbol \( \gamma^k \) (gamma to the power of k) discounts rewards \( k \) steps into the future.

6.3 The Bellman Expectation Equation

The idea in plain words. The value of a state equals what you get right now plus the discounted value of wherever you land next, averaged over all possibilities. Think of it like deciding whether a restaurant is good. You experience the meal you eat today (immediate reward). You also form an expectation of how much you will enjoy future visits (discounted future value). And you weight each dish by how likely it is to be ordered (policy probabilities).

The Bellman expectation equation decomposes \( V^\pi(s) \) into a one-step lookahead:

\[ V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \big[ r + \gamma V^\pi(s') \big] \]

Let me name every piece. We sum over all actions \( a \), each weighted by the policy probability \( \pi(a \mid s) \). For each action, we sum over all possible next states \( s' \) and rewards \( r \), weighted by the transition probability \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \). Inside the brackets: the immediate reward \( r \) plus \( \gamma V^\pi(s') \) — the discounted value of the next state. The outer expectation over actions reflects the fact that the policy itself is probabilistic. This equation is at the heart of policy evaluation.

6.4 The Bellman Optimality Equation

The idea in plain words. Instead of averaging over actions like a cautious traveller who considers all routes, the optimality equation acts like a traveller who only cares about the single best path at every junction. Where the expectation equation says "what's the average outcome over all actions I might take?", the optimality equation says "what's the outcome of the single best action I could take?"

The Bellman optimality equation defines \( V^*(s) \), the optimal state-value function:

\[ V^*(s) = \max_a \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \big[ r + \gamma V^*(s') \big] \]

Look at the two differences from the expectation equation. First, the outer sum over actions weighted by \( \pi(a \mid s) \) is replaced by \( \max_a \) — we pick the action with the highest expected value. Second, the value function inside is \( V^* \) not \( V^\pi \), because we are following the optimal policy from the next state onward. There is no explicit policy in this expression; the optimal policy is implicit in the max operation. Once you know \( V^* \), the optimal policy \( \pi^* \) is the one that picks the maximizing action at every state.

Two equations, side by side.

Bellman expectation (for a given policy \( \pi \)):

\[ V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \big[ r + \gamma V^\pi(s') \big] \]

Bellman optimality (for the optimal policy):

\[ V^*(s) = \max_a \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \big[ r + \gamma V^*(s') \big] \]

When you see \( \Sigma \) (a weighted sum over actions), you are looking at an expectation equation — a specific policy is involved. When you see \( \max \), you are looking at optimality — no specific policy is involved; the optimal one emerges from picking the best action.

Common pitfalls with the Bellman equations.

  • Confusing the two forms. The expectation equation gives \( V^\pi \) for a fixed policy. The optimality equation gives \( V^* \) for the best possible policy. Using the wrong one in the wrong context produces meaningless values.
  • Thinking the max replaces the policy entirely. The optimal policy is derived from the max — it picks whichever action achieves it. The max operation is the optimal policy in implicit form.
  • Forgetting the discount factor's role. If \( \gamma = 1 \) with no terminal states, values can diverge to infinity. Always check that \( \gamma \) is appropriate for the problem.
  • Ignoring terminal states. The value of a terminal state is defined to be 0. This must be enforced — otherwise iterations can propagate spurious values from terminal states.

The key: The Bellman expectation equation evaluates a given policy by averaging over action choices. The Bellman optimality equation finds the best possible policy by always picking the action with maximum expected return. This distinction drives everything that follows.

2. Value Iteration

Now we put the Bellman optimality equation to work. Value iteration is the simplest dynamic programming algorithm for finding \( V^* \) — and from it, the optimal policy \( \pi^* \).

6.5 The Hook

You are designing a self-driving race car strategy. The car can go slow or fast from two engine temperatures (cool and warm). Sometimes it overheats and breaks down. You know the transition probabilities and the rewards. What is the best strategy to maximise your score? Value iteration gives you the answer — by starting from wild guesses and refining them until the truth emerges.

6.6 Intuition and Analogy

The idea in plain words. Value iteration starts by guessing that every state is worth zero. Then it looks around from each state — if I take each possible action, where do I land and what do I get? It picks the best outcome, updates the state's value, and repeats. With every pass, the values become more accurate. It is like estimating house prices in a new neighbourhood. You start by guessing all houses are worth the same. Then you notice one has a garden (adds value), one is near a train station (adds value), one needs repairs (subtracts value). Each time you re-evaluate using the Bellman optimality equation, your estimates get sharper. After enough passes, the numbers stop changing — you now know the true value of every house.

6.7 The Algorithm, Step by Step

Value iteration takes a known MDP model and computes \( V^*(s) \) for every state. Here is the full procedure:

Value Iteration Algorithm.

  1. Initialise: Set \( V(s) = 0 \) for all states \( s \in \mathcal{S}^+ \). Terminal states are initialised to 0 and remain 0.
  2. Repeat:
    • Set \( \Delta = 0 \) (tracks the maximum change in this sweep).
    • For each state \( s \in \mathcal{S} \) (non-terminal states only):
      • Store the old value: \( v \leftarrow V(s) \).
      • Update using the Bellman optimality equation: \[ V(s) \leftarrow \max_a \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \big[ r + \gamma V(s') \big] \]
      • Update the maximum change: \( \Delta \leftarrow \max(\Delta, |v - V(s)|) \).
  3. Stop when \( \Delta < \theta \) (a small threshold, e.g. \( 10^{-6} \)).
  4. Output the deterministic policy: \( \pi(s) = \arg\max_a \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) [r + \gamma V(s')] \).

One pass through all the states is called a sweep. Value iteration performs multiple sweeps. The delta tracks the largest value change in the current sweep. When even the biggest change is tiny, the values have converged. The final step extracts the optimal policy from the converged value function.

6.8 Worked Example: The Race Car Problem

The Race Car Problem. A car has three states: Cool, Warm, and Overheated. Overheated is a terminal state (value = 0, the car is broken). From Cool and Warm, two actions are available: Slow and Fast. The discount factor \( \gamma = 0.9 \).

The transition dynamics:

  • From Cool, Slow: 100% chance → Cool, reward +1.
  • From Cool, Fast: 50% → Cool (reward +2), 50% → Warm (reward +2).
  • From Warm, Fast: 100% → Overheated, reward −10.
  • From Warm, Slow: 50% → Cool (reward +1), 50% → Warm (reward +1).

Sweep 1 (all values initialised to 0):

State Cool: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Slow: } & 1 \times [1 + 0.9 \times V_0(\text{Cool})] = 1 \times [1 + 0.9 \times 0] = 1 \\ \text{Fast: } & 0.5 \times [2 + 0.9 \times V_0(\text{Cool})] + 0.5 \times [2 + 0.9 \times V_0(\text{Warm})] \\ & = 0.5 \times [2 + 0] + 0.5 \times [2 + 0] = 0.5 \times 2 + 0.5 \times 2 = 2 \\ V_1(\text{Cool}) &= \max(1, 2) = \mathbf{2} \end{aligned} \]

State Warm: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Fast: } & 1 \times [-10 + 0.9 \times V_0(\text{Overheated})] = 1 \times [-10 + 0] = -10 \\ \text{Slow: } & 0.5 \times [1 + 0.9 \times V_0(\text{Cool})] + 0.5 \times [1 + 0.9 \times V_0(\text{Warm})] \\ & = 0.5 \times [1 + 0] + 0.5 \times [1 + 0] = 0.5 \times 1 + 0.5 \times 1 = 1 \\ V_1(\text{Warm}) &= \max(-10, 1) = \mathbf{1} \end{aligned} \]

State Overheated: Terminal, remains 0.

After Sweep 1: \( V(\text{Cool}) = 2 \), \( V(\text{Warm}) = 1 \), \( V(\text{Overheated}) = 0 \).

Sweep 2 (using values from Sweep 1):

State Cool: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Slow: } & 1 \times [1 + 0.9 \times 2] = 1 + 1.8 = 2.8 \\ \text{Fast: } & 0.5 \times [2 + 0.9 \times 2] + 0.5 \times [2 + 0.9 \times 1] \\ & = 0.5 \times [2 + 1.8] + 0.5 \times [2 + 0.9] \\ & = 0.5 \times 3.8 + 0.5 \times 2.9 = 1.9 + 1.45 = 3.35 \\ V_2(\text{Cool}) &= \max(2.8, 3.35) = \mathbf{3.35} \end{aligned} \]

State Warm: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Fast: } & 1 \times [-10 + 0] = -10 \\ \text{Slow: } & 0.5 \times [1 + 0.9 \times 2] + 0.5 \times [1 + 0.9 \times 1] \\ & = 0.5 \times [1 + 1.8] + 0.5 \times [1 + 0.9] \\ & = 0.5 \times 2.8 + 0.5 \times 1.9 = 1.4 + 0.95 = 2.35 \\ V_2(\text{Warm}) &= \max(-10, 2.35) = \mathbf{2.35} \end{aligned} \]

After Sweep 2: \( V(\text{Cool}) = 3.35 \), \( V(\text{Warm}) = 2.35 \), \( V(\text{Overheated}) = 0 \).

Convergence: After additional sweeps, the values settle at approximately \( V(\text{Cool}) \approx 3.35 \), \( V(\text{Warm}) \approx 2.35 \), \( V(\text{Overheated}) = 0 \). These are the optimal state values for the race car problem.

6.9 In-Place vs Standard Updates

Two flavours of value iteration. In the standard (not-in-place) version, you maintain two separate arrays — \( V_{\text{old}} \) from the previous sweep and \( V_{\text{new}} \) for the current sweep. Every update in the current sweep reads only from \( V_{\text{old}} \). In the in-place version, you keep one array and always use the most recently computed values — even values computed earlier in the same sweep. Think of it like updating a shared spreadsheet. The standard version waits until everyone finishes their row before anyone reads. The in-place version lets you read whoever has already filled in their row, even if you are still mid-sweep. Both converge to the same optimal values, but the in-place version typically converges faster.

For example, in sweep 2 of the race car problem, when computing \( V(\text{Warm}) \), the standard version uses \( V(\text{Cool}) = 2 \) (from sweep 1). The in-place version, having already updated Cool to 3.35 in the same sweep, would use that newer value instead. This small difference can accelerate convergence.

Exam tip — know which version you are being asked for. A problem may specify "in-place" or "standard" value iteration. In the in-place case, always use the most recently updated value when a state's value is needed. Even mid-sweep, use the latest value. In the standard case, all updates in sweep \( k \) reference only sweep \( k-1 \) values.

6.10 Convergence Behaviour

An important observation: the policy often converges long before the values do. In a grid-world example, the optimal policy (which direction to move from each cell) stabilised around iteration 10, but the value estimates continued changing in tiny increments through iteration 100. This means the agent can start acting optimally much earlier than the value function reaches full numerical precision. The stopping criterion \( \Delta < \theta \) is a mathematical convenience; in practice, you might stop as soon as the policy stops changing.

But convergence depends on having the model. When DP is impossible — for instance in blackjack, where computing \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \) for all card combinations is complex and error-prone — Monte Carlo methods have a clear advantage. MC methods can estimate state values simply by simulating many games and averaging the returns, even though constructing the full transition model for DP would be extremely difficult. This is a recurring theme: the ability to work with sample episodes alone is a significant practical advantage, even when the environment's dynamics are fully known in principle.

The key: Value iteration applies the Bellman optimality equation repeatedly across all states. Each sweep sharpens the value estimates. The policy can stabilise well before the values reach full convergence. In-place updates accelerate the process. Now that we have \( V^* \), we need to extract the actual policy — our next topic.

3. Policy Extraction from Values

6.11 The Hook

You have run value iteration and have a table of optimal state values. But a value is not an instruction — it does not tell you what to do. How do you convert "this state is worth 3.35" into "when you are here, drive fast"?

6.12 Intuition and Analogy

The idea in plain words. Policy extraction answers a simple question at every state. Among all the actions you could take, which one leads to the highest expected return? You already know the value of every possible next state. So you simulate each action, see where it lands and what reward it yields, and pick the winner. Picture standing at a fork in the road with a map that labels each destination with a satisfaction score. You look at each path, calculate the journey cost plus the destination's score, and take the path with the best total.

6.13 The Formal Extraction Rule

Deterministic optimal policy from \( V^* \):

\[ \pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \big[ r + \gamma V^*(s') \big] \]

At each state \( s \), evaluate every action \( a \) using the same one-step lookahead as in value iteration. The action with the highest value becomes the deterministic policy for that state. If multiple actions tie for the maximum, any can be chosen.

6.14 Worked Example: Extracting Policy from the Race Car

Given: The converged values from the race car problem — \( V^*(\text{Cool}) = 3.35 \), \( V^*(\text{Warm}) = 2.35 \), \( V^*(\text{Overheated}) = 0 \). Discount factor \( \gamma = 0.9 \).

Extracting \( \pi^*(\text{Cool}) \):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Slow: } & 1 \times [1 + 0.9 \times 3.35] = 1 + 3.015 = 4.015 \\ \text{Fast: } & 0.5 \times [2 + 0.9 \times 3.35] + 0.5 \times [2 + 0.9 \times 2.35] \\ & = 0.5 \times [2 + 3.015] + 0.5 \times [2 + 2.115] \\ & = 0.5 \times 5.015 + 0.5 \times 4.115 = 2.5075 + 2.0575 = 4.565 \end{aligned} \]

\( \max(4.015, 4.565) = 4.565 \) → \( \pi^*(\text{Cool}) = \mathbf{Fast} \).

Extracting \( \pi^*(\text{Warm}) \):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Fast: } & 1 \times [-10 + 0.9 \times 0] = -10 \\ \text{Slow: } & 0.5 \times [1 + 0.9 \times 3.35] + 0.5 \times [1 + 0.9 \times 2.35] \\ & = 0.5 \times [1 + 3.015] + 0.5 \times [1 + 2.115] \\ & = 0.5 \times 4.015 + 0.5 \times 3.115 = 2.0075 + 1.5575 = 3.565 \end{aligned} \]

\( \max(-10, 3.565) = 3.565 \) → \( \pi^*(\text{Warm}) = \mathbf{Slow} \).

Final optimal policy: When Cool → drive Fast. When Warm → drive Slow. Overheated is terminal (no action).

6.15 The Cost of Policy Extraction

Extracting the policy requires evaluating every action at every state using the full transition model. For each state, you examine all \( |A| \) actions, and each action's evaluation can involve up to \( |S| \) possible next states. Across all \( |S| \) states, the worst-case cost is \( O(|S|^2 |A|) \). This is the same order of cost as one full sweep of value iteration — it is not free. This observation motivates computing Q-values directly, which we explore later.

The key: Policy extraction selects the action that maximises the expected one-step lookahead at each state. It costs \( O(|S|^2 |A|) \) because it re-evaluates every action-state transition. Computing Q-values instead of V-values eliminates this separate extraction step — we will return to this idea.

4. Policy Evaluation

6.16 The Hook

Imagine you are handed a chess strategy. "This is the strategy — tell me how good it is," you are told. You cannot change the strategy. You can only simulate it and score each board position. That is policy evaluation: given a fixed policy, compute the value of every state under that policy.

6.17 Intuition and Analogy

The idea in plain words. Policy evaluation answers: if an agent follows a fixed behaviour rule, what is each state worth? The behaviour does not change — you just keep simulating it. At every state, you look one step ahead using the Bellman expectation equation (since the policy is fixed, you average over its action probabilities), update the value, and repeat until convergence. It is like rating every seat in a stadium for a particular team's game. The team always plays the same way (fixed policy). A seat near the action is worth a lot if the team plays well; the same seat is worth little if the team plays badly. The seats themselves do not change — their value depends entirely on the fixed team strategy.

6.18 The Policy Evaluation Algorithm

Policy Evaluation Algorithm.

  1. Input: A fixed policy \( \pi \) and the MDP model.
  2. Initialise: \( V(s) = 0 \) for all \( s \in \mathcal{S}^+ \) (terminal states remain 0).
  3. Repeat:
    • \( \Delta \leftarrow 0 \).
    • For each non-terminal state \( s \):
      • \( v \leftarrow V(s) \).
      • \( V(s) \leftarrow \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) [r + \gamma V(s')] \).
      • \( \Delta \leftarrow \max(\Delta, |v - V(s)|) \).
  4. Stop when \( \Delta < \theta \).

The only difference from value iteration: the outer operation is a weighted sum over actions (using the fixed policy probabilities) instead of a max over actions. The policy is given and never changes during evaluation.

6.19 Worked Example: 5-State Grid Walk

The 5-State Grid Walk. Five states in a line: State 1 (Bad terminal, reward −1 on entry), State 2, State 3 (Start), State 4, State 5 (Good terminal, reward +1 on entry). All inter-state transitions have reward 0. Terminal states absorb and yield no further transitions. The policy is: always go left with probability 0.8 and right with probability 0.2, regardless of state.

Initial values: \( V(\text{Bad}) = 0 \), \( V(2) = 0 \), \( V(3) = 0 \), \( V(4) = 0 \), \( V(\text{Good}) = 0 \). Discount \( \gamma = 1 \) (episodic — terminal states end the episode).

Sweep 1:

  • State 2: \( V(2) = 0.8 \times [-1 + V(\text{Bad})] + 0.2 \times [0 + V(3)] = 0.8 \times (-1) + 0.2 \times 0 = -0.8 \).
  • State 3: \( V(3) = 0.8 \times [0 + V(2)] + 0.2 \times [0 + V(4)] = 0 \).
  • State 4: \( V(4) = 0.8 \times [0 + V(3)] + 0.2 \times [1 + V(\text{Good})] = 0.2 \).

After Sweep 1: \( V(2) = -0.8 \), \( V(3) = 0 \), \( V(4) = 0.2 \).

Sweep 2 (using updated values):

  • State 2: \( V(2) = 0.8 \times [-1 + 0] + 0.2 \times [0 + 0] = -0.8 \).
  • State 3: \( V(3) = 0.8 \times [0 + (-0.8)] + 0.2 \times [0 + 0.2] = -0.64 + 0.04 = -0.6 \).
  • State 4: \( V(4) = 0.8 \times [0 + 0] + 0.2 \times [1 + 0] = 0.2 \).

After Sweep 2: \( V(2) = -0.8 \), \( V(3) = -0.6 \), \( V(4) = 0.2 \).

Continued sweeps lead to convergence: \( V(2) \approx -0.779 \), \( V(3) \approx -0.779 \), \( V(4) \approx -0.28 \). All non-terminal values are negative under this "mostly go left" policy — even at State 4 near the good terminal, the tendency to drift left drags the value down.

Contrast with "mostly go right" policy (0.8 right, 0.2 left): After convergence, \( V(2) \approx 0.72 \), \( V(3) \approx 0.72 \), \( V(4) \approx 0.8 \). The goodness of being near the positive terminal propagates leftward through the state chain.

Sense check: A policy that biases toward positive rewards produces positive state values. A policy that biases toward negative rewards produces negative state values. The value of a state depends on the policy — not just where the state sits in the environment.

The key: Policy evaluation uses the Bellman expectation equation to compute \( V^\pi \) — the value of each state under a fixed, unchanging policy. The computed values reveal how good (or bad) that specific behaviour is. Next, we ask: can we improve the policy using these values?

How policy evaluation changes when the model is absent. In the model-free setting, you cannot compute the Bellman expectation update because \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \) is unknown. Two alternative approaches emerge:

  • Monte Carlo policy evaluation: Generate episodes by following \( \pi \), then for each state visited, average the returns that followed. The update target is the complete return \( G_t \). No bootstrapping — you wait until the episode ends. This is the approach of first-visit and every-visit MC prediction (Section 5.1).
  • TD(0) policy evaluation: After each transition \( (S_t, R_{t+1}, S_{t+1}) \), apply the update \( V(S_t) \leftarrow V(S_t) + \alpha [R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t)] \). The target \( R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1}) \) uses a sampled reward and a bootstrapped value estimate — combining Monte Carlo-style sampling with DP-style bootstrapping. This is the TD prediction method (Section 6.1) and it can learn online, step by step, without waiting for episode termination.

On stochastic tasks like the random walk (Example 6.2), TD(0) consistently converges faster than constant-\( \alpha \) MC. Under batch training — where the same finite set of episodes is processed repeatedly until convergence — TD(0) finds the certainty-equivalence estimate (the value function correct for the maximum-likelihood Markov model), while MC finds the minimum-squared-error estimate on the training returns. The certainty-equivalence estimate is generally a better predictor of future returns, which explains TD's empirical advantage.

5. Policy Improvement

6.20 The Hook

After evaluating a policy, you have a value for every state. Now look at those values and ask a natural question. You are at state \( s \) and know the value of every possible next state. Can you choose a better action than your current policy recommends?

6.21 Intuition and Analogy

The idea in plain words. Policy improvement is simple: look at the values you just computed, and at every state, pick the action that leads to the highest expected return. If your current policy already picks that action, great — it is already optimal with respect to these values. If not, update the policy to be greedy. This always produces a policy at least as good as the original. It is like re-routing your daily commute after checking traffic data. You evaluated your usual route and it scored 30 minutes. But the traffic data shows another route that scores 22 minutes. You switch — you improved your policy. Next week, traffic patterns shift, so you re-evaluate and improve again.

6.22 The Formal Update

Policy Improvement Step. Given a value function \( V^\pi \) (computed by policy evaluation), construct an improved policy \( \pi' \) by acting greedily:

\[ \pi'(s) = \arg\max_a \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \big[ r + \gamma V^\pi(s') \big] \]

If \( \pi' = \pi \) (the policy does not change after this step), then both \( \pi \) and \( V^\pi \) are optimal — you have reached \( \pi^* \) and \( V^* \).

The policy improvement theorem guarantees that \( V^{\pi'}(s) \geq V^\pi(s) \) for all states — the new greedy policy is at least as good everywhere. If it is strictly better at any state, you have made genuine progress.

Pitfalls.

  • Thinking improvement is always dramatic. One improvement step may produce only a tiny gain. The cumulative effect across many cycles is what drives convergence to optimality.
  • Forgetting to re-evaluate. After improving the policy, the old value function \( V^\pi \) is no longer accurate — it was computed for the old policy. You must re-evaluate with the new policy before improving again.
  • Assuming stochastic policies cannot be improved. A stochastic policy can always be deterministically improved by shifting probability mass to the best action. The optimal policy for a fully-known MDP is always deterministic.

The key: Policy improvement takes a value function and creates a strictly better (or equally good) policy by acting greedily. The guarantee that greedy improvement never makes things worse is the engine behind the generalized policy iteration framework.

Extending improvement to the model-free setting. In Monte Carlo control (Chapter 5), the same GPI alternation of evaluation and improvement is applied without a model. Policy evaluation is done by averaging sample returns from episodes rather than by Bellman expectation sweeps. Policy improvement then makes the policy greedy with respect to those action-value estimates: \( \pi(s) = \arg\max_a Q(s, a) \). Crucially, action values are used instead of state values because — without a model — you cannot look ahead one step to determine which action is best. The policy improvement theorem applies identically: \( q_{\pi_k}(s, \pi_{k+1}(s)) \geq v_{\pi_k}(s) \) for all states. The Monte Carlo ES (Exploring Starts) algorithm performs full episodes of evaluation followed by greedy improvement, converging to the optimal policy and optimal action-value function — but only under the assumption that every state-action pair can be selected as the episode start. In practice this assumption is relaxed through on-policy \( \varepsilon \)-soft policies or off-policy importance sampling techniques.

6. Generalized Policy Iteration and the Policy Iteration Algorithm

6.23 The Hook

Policy evaluation tells you how good your current strategy is. Policy improvement makes it better. Can you just alternate between the two — evaluate, improve, evaluate, improve — until you reach perfection?

6.24 The GPI Framework

The idea in plain words. Generalized Policy Iteration (GPI) is a framework, not a single algorithm. It says: keep a value function and a policy. Make the value function consistent with the policy (evaluation). Then make the policy greedy with respect to the value function (improvement). Repeat. These two processes push against each other — evaluation pulls the value function toward the true \( V^\pi \), improvement pushes the policy toward greediness. They converge together to optimality. It is like tuning a guitar while playing it. You pluck a string (evaluate — hear the current pitch), then turn the tuning peg (improve — adjust toward the correct pitch). Pluck again, tune again. Each cycle brings you closer to the perfect note. The string and the peg work together; neither alone would get you there.

The GPI framework underlies almost every reinforcement learning algorithm. Different algorithms differ in how much evaluation they do per improvement step:

  • Policy iteration: Full evaluation to convergence before each improvement step.
  • Value iteration: Just one sweep of evaluation (one Bellman update per state) before implicitly improving.
  • In between: Any number of evaluation sweeps — 3, 5, 50 — before improving.

Beyond DP, GPI extends to every major family of RL algorithms. In Monte Carlo methods, policy evaluation is done by averaging sample returns from episodes rather than using a model to compute expected values — the returns are collected by following the policy, and the average replaces the Bellman expectation sum. Policy improvement then makes the policy greedy (or \( \varepsilon \)-greedy) with respect to the estimated action values. In temporal-difference (TD) learning, evaluation uses bootstrapped targets like \( R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1}) \) instead of full-episode returns, combining the sampling of Monte Carlo with the bootstrapping of DP. In Sarsa and Q-learning, GPI runs on an episode-by-episode (or even step-by-step) basis. The core alternation — make values consistent with the policy, then make the policy greedy with respect to values — remains the engine in every case.

On-policy vs Off-policy in GPI. The exploration-exploitation trade-off splits GPI-based control into two classes:

  • On-policy methods (Sarsa): evaluate and improve the same policy that generates behaviour. The policy remains \( \varepsilon \)-soft — it commits to always exploring — and the algorithm finds the best policy among those that still explore. The policy improvement theorem holds for \( \varepsilon \)-greedy policies: moving to an \( \varepsilon \)-greedy policy with respect to \( q_\pi \) is guaranteed to be at least as good as \( \pi \) for all states.
  • Off-policy methods (Q-learning): the behaviour policy (used for exploration) is different from the target policy (being learned). The target policy can be fully deterministic and greedy while the behaviour policy continues to explore. This separation is powerful because it lets the agent learn about optimal behaviour while acting safely or exploratively. The cost is that off-policy learning requires handling the mismatch between the two policies — in Monte Carlo methods via importance sampling ratios, and in TD methods through the max operator in the Q-learning update.

6.25 The Policy Iteration Algorithm

Policy Iteration Algorithm.

  1. Initialise: Choose an arbitrary policy \( \pi_0 \) and value function \( V \) (e.g., all zeros).
  2. Policy Evaluation: Run the full policy evaluation algorithm (Section 4) to convergence, obtaining \( V^\pi \) — the true value of the current policy.
  3. Policy Improvement: For each state \( s \), compute: \[ \pi_{\text{new}}(s) = \arg\max_a \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) [r + \gamma V^\pi(s')] \]
  4. If \( \pi_{\text{new}} = \pi_{\text{old}} \), stop — the policy is optimal. Otherwise, set \( \pi \leftarrow \pi_{\text{new}} \) and return to step 2.

6.26 Value Iteration vs Policy Iteration

Comparing the two dynamic programming algorithms.

Value Iteration:

  • Each iteration: one Bellman optimality update per state → \( O(|S|^2 |A|) \) per sweep.
  • Does one "mini" evaluation step and one improvement step folded into a single update.
  • Many sweeps needed, but each sweep is fast.
  • Policy emerges gradually alongside values.
  • Policy often stabilises long before values converge.

Policy Iteration:

  • Each outer iteration: full policy evaluation (many sweeps to convergence) + one improvement step.
  • Each evaluation sweep costs \( O(|S|^2 |A|) \), and evaluation may require dozens or hundreds of sweeps.
  • Fewer outer iterations — the policy typically converges in a handful of cycles.
  • Each outer iteration is expensive because of the full evaluation inside.

Which is better? There is no universal answer. For small state spaces where policy evaluation converges quickly, policy iteration can finish in fewer total operations. For large state spaces, the cost of full evaluation per iteration becomes prohibitive, and value iteration (or truncated policy iteration with only a few evaluation sweeps) wins. The literature notes that it is difficult to mathematically prove superiority for all problem classes.

In practice, value iteration is more widely used because it produces a usable policy quickly — often long before numerical convergence. Asynchronous variants (Section 7) further tilt the balance toward value-iteration-style approaches.

The key: GPI is the alternating engine of evaluation and improvement at the heart of RL algorithms. Policy iteration runs full evaluation per cycle — expensive but converges in few cycles. Value iteration compresses both into one Bellman optimality update per state — cheaper per sweep, more sweeps needed. The choice depends on the problem's state-space size and the cost of evaluation.

7. Asynchronous Value Iteration

6.27 The Hook

Standard value iteration touches every state in every sweep — even states whose values barely change. In a problem with a billion states, that is a billion wasted computations per sweep. Can we be smarter about which states we update and when?

6.28 Intuition and Analogy

The idea in plain words. Asynchronous value iteration breaks the rigid sweep structure. Instead of visiting all states in order, it maintains a priority queue of states whose values are likely to change significantly. When you update a state, its neighbours become candidates for future updates (because their values depend on the updated state). States with the highest expected change get updated first. It is like a newsroom that tracks developing stories. A reporter does not re-check every story every hour. Stories that just broke get top priority. When a story updates, any related stories (neighbouring states) get bumped up in priority because the new information might change them too. Quiet stories sit at the bottom until something relevant happens.

6.29 How It Works

Asynchronous Value Iteration — Key Idea.

  1. Maintain a priority queue of states. The priority of a state reflects how much its value is expected to change — higher priority means the state is more likely to see a large update.
  2. Pop the highest-priority state and apply the Bellman optimality update.
  3. If the update changes the state's value significantly, add all its predecessor states (states that can transition into this one) to the queue with updated priorities.
  4. Repeat until the queue is empty or the maximum change falls below a threshold.

Some states may be updated dozens of times before other states are touched even once. The algorithm focuses computation where it matters most — on states whose values are actively changing — and ignores states that have already converged.

6.30 Why It Matters

In problems with enormous state spaces, synchronous sweeps are impossible. Backgammon, for example, has roughly \( 10^{20} \) states. Asynchronous methods make DP feasible by focusing updates on the states the agent actually visits or that are near the solution frontier. They pair well with function approximation, where exact sweeps are not even defined.

The key: Asynchronous value iteration replaces rigid synchronous sweeps with a priority-driven update schedule. It focuses computation on states most likely to change, making dynamic programming feasible for problems too large to sweep exhaustively. This idea is a bridge toward sample-based and model-free methods.

From priority queues to eligibility traces. Asynchronous DP and eligibility traces (Chapter 7) solve different faces of the same underlying problem — temporal credit assignment. Asynchronous DP uses a priority queue to decide which states to update first, propagating value information efficiently through the state space. Eligibility traces in TD(\( \lambda \)) take the complementary approach: instead of selecting which states to update, they maintain a decaying memory trace for every recently visited state. When a TD error occurs, all states with non-zero traces receive updates proportional to their trace magnitude. The forward view of TD(\( \lambda \)) averages \( n \)-step returns weighted by \( \lambda^{n-1} \), creating a smooth spectrum from one-step TD (\( \lambda = 0 \)) to Monte Carlo (\( \lambda = 1 \)). Both mechanisms — selective state visitation and decaying credit traces — accelerate learning by avoiding the wasteful uniform sweeps of synchronous DP. In the model-free setting, \( n \)-step TD and TD(\( \lambda \)) provide the same benefit: propagating reward information backward across multiple time steps without waiting for episode termination.

8. Model-Based Q-Learning

6.31 The Hook

Extracting a policy from \( V^* \) costs \( O(|S|^2 |A|) \) — you have to re-simulate every action from every state. What if the value function itself stored per-action values, so extracting the policy was as simple as picking the maximum?

6.32 Intuition and Analogy

The idea in plain words. Instead of storing one number per state, store one number per state-action pair. For example: "taking action Up is worth 4.5, Down is worth 2.1, Left is worth 3.8, Right is worth 5.2." This is the Q-function. With Q-values, policy extraction is trivial: at each state, simply pick the action with the highest Q-value. It is like rating restaurants by dish rather than by restaurant. Instead of saying "this restaurant is a 7/10" (V-value), you say "the pasta is 9/10, the steak is 6/10, the salad is 5/10" (Q-values). When you arrive, you order the pasta — no additional calculation needed.

6.33 The Q-Star Bellman Equation

The optimal action-value function \( Q^*(s, a) \) is the expected return when taking action \( a \) in state \( s \) and thereafter following the optimal policy. Its Bellman optimality equation is:

\[ Q^*(s, a) = \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \big[ r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^*(s', a') \big] \]

Compare with \( V^*(s) = \max_a Q^*(s, a) \). The key difference: \( Q^* \) takes an action as input, so the max moves inside to the next state's action selection.

Policy extraction from Q-values: \( \pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a Q^*(s, a) \). This is \( O(|S| |A|) \) — just a linear scan over actions per state, with no need to re-simulate transitions.

6.34 Worked Example: Q-Value Calculation in a Grid

Given: A grid cell with four possible actions (Up, Down, Left, Right). Assume deterministic transitions — each action goes exactly where intended. From the current cell \( s \), going Right leads to \( s' \) with \( V^*(s') = 0.85 \) and immediate reward \( r = 0 \). Going Up leads to a cell with \( V^* = 0.3 \). Going Left leads to a cell with \( V^* = 0.1 \). Going Down leads to a cell with \( V^* = 0.6 \). Discount \( \gamma = 0.9 \).

\[ \begin{aligned} Q^*(s, \text{Right}) &= 0 + 0.9 \times 0.85 = 0.765 \\ Q^*(s, \text{Up}) &= 0 + 0.9 \times 0.30 = 0.270 \\ Q^*(s, \text{Left}) &= 0 + 0.9 \times 0.10 = 0.090 \\ Q^*(s, \text{Down}) &= 0 + 0.9 \times 0.60 = 0.540 \end{aligned} \]

The optimal action is Right, with \( Q^* = 0.765 \). This also gives \( V^*(s) = \max(0.765, 0.270, 0.090, 0.540) = 0.765 \).

Policy extraction from Q-values requires only comparing four numbers — no re-simulation of transition probabilities, no summing over outcome states. This is why Q-values are preferred when policy extraction speed matters.

6.35 Model-Based vs Model-Free Q-Learning

Important distinction. The Q-learning presented here is model-based Q-learning — it uses the transition probability function \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \) inside the update. This is not the same as the more famous model-free Q-learning (Watkins, 1989) that you will encounter later. Model-free Q-learning learns Q-values from experience samples without requiring a model. The Q-star Bellman equation itself is the same — what differs is how the update is computed (from a model vs from sampled experience).

6.36 The Model-Free Q-Learning Update Rule (Preview)

In model-free Q-learning, the agent does not sum over all possible next states using \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \) — it simply observes a single transition sample \( (S_t, A_t, R_{t+1}, S_{t+1}) \) and applies the following update:

\[ Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha \big[ R_{t+1} + \gamma \max_a Q(S_{t+1}, a) - Q(S_t, A_t) \big] \]

Compare this with the model-based Q-star equation from Section 8.3. The model-based version computes an expected value over all possible outcomes (requires the model). The model-free version uses a single sampled outcome and moves the current Q-estimate toward the target \( R_{t+1} + \gamma \max_a Q(S_{t+1}, a) \) using a step-size parameter \( \alpha \). This is the algorithm that made reinforcement learning famous — it learns optimal action values directly from raw experience, without ever building a model of the environment's dynamics. Watkins (1989) proved that Q-learning converges to \( q^* \) with probability 1, provided all state–action pairs continue to be visited and the step-size parameter satisfies standard stochastic approximation conditions.

The learned action-value function \( Q \) directly approximates \( q^* \) — the optimal action-value function — independent of the policy being followed. This is a profound property: the agent can behave according to an exploratory policy (e.g., \( \varepsilon \)-greedy) while learning about the optimal deterministic policy. This separation of behaviour and learning is why Q-learning is classified as an off-policy control method.

The difference between on-policy and off-policy learning becomes critical when exploration matters. An on-policy method like Sarsa — whose update rule is \( Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha [R_{t+1} + \gamma Q(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1}) - Q(S_t, A_t)] \) — learns the value of the policy it actually follows, including exploratory actions. Q-learning, by contrast, learns the optimal policy regardless of exploration. In the cliff-walking problem, Sarsa learns a safer path that stays away from the cliff (because it accounts for the risk of random exploratory steps), while Q-learning learns the optimal but riskier path right along the cliff edge.

The key: Computing \( Q^*(s, a) \) directly makes policy extraction trivial — just pick the action with the highest Q-value per state. The Q-star Bellman equation embeds the max at the next state, creating a self-consistent definition of optimal action values. This idea will reappear as model-free Q-learning, one of the most important algorithms in reinforcement learning.

9. Limitations and Practical Considerations

6.37 The Core Dependency on a Model

The fundamental constraint. Every dynamic programming algorithm in this chapter requires the transition probability function \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \). Value iteration, policy iteration, and model-based Q-learning all depend on it. Without this model, you cannot compute the Bellman update. This is why DP algorithms are sometimes called planning algorithms. If you have an accurate model of the world on paper, you can plan an optimal policy without ever stepping into the real environment. You do offline optimisation, not online learning through interaction.

6.38 Computational Complexity

Each sweep of value iteration or each evaluation sweep in policy iteration costs \( O(|S|^2 |A|) \) in the worst case. For problems where the number of states \( |S| \) is in the millions or billions, this is infeasible. Even storing the value table of size \( |S| \) becomes impossible. This is the curse of dimensionality — the state space grows exponentially with the number of state variables.

6.39 When DP Fails and What Comes Next

Dynamic programming is appropriate when:

  • The state space and action space are small and discrete.
  • A complete and accurate model of the environment is available.
  • You can afford the memory to store a value for every state (or every state-action pair).

DP is not appropriate when:

  • The state space is continuous or combinatorially large (e.g., real-world robotics, Go, poker).
  • No model is available — you only have experience samples (state, action, reward, next state).
  • The model is too uncertain or expensive to construct.

When the model is missing, we turn to model-free reinforcement learning methods. These build directly on the Bellman equations and GPI framework established in this chapter:

Monte Carlo methods (Chapter 5) estimate state or action values by averaging the actual returns observed after visiting each state across many episodes. They do not bootstrap — they wait until the end of an episode and use the complete return \( G_t \) as the update target. First-visit MC averages returns after the first occurrence of each state in an episode; every-visit MC averages returns after all occurrences. MC methods have three key advantages over DP: (1) they require no model, (2) they can focus estimation on a subset of states without computing the entire value table, and (3) they may be less harmed by violations of the Markov property since they do not rely on successor-state estimates. Their main limitation is the need for episodic tasks — they cannot learn from incomplete episodes — and the requirement for sufficient exploration, addressed through exploring starts or \( \varepsilon \)-soft policies.

Temporal-difference learning (Chapter 6) combines the sampling of Monte Carlo with the bootstrapping of DP. The simplest TD method, TD(0), updates value estimates after every single step using the target \( R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1}) \) rather than waiting for the full return. This means TD can learn online during an episode — a critical advantage when episodes are long or in continuing (non-episodic) tasks. The TD target is an estimate for two reasons: the reward is sampled (like MC) and the value of the next state is itself an estimate (like DP). Under batch training, TD(0) converges to the certainty-equivalence estimate — the value function that would be exactly correct for the maximum-likelihood model of the Markov process — which is often a better solution than the minimum-squared-error estimate found by MC. The two flagship TD control algorithms are Sarsa (on-policy) and Q-learning (off-policy).

Eligibility traces (Chapter 7) bridge the gap between one-step TD and Monte Carlo methods through the TD(\( \lambda \)) algorithm. With \( \lambda = 0 \), you get one-step TD; with \( \lambda = 1 \), you get a Monte Carlo method; intermediate values of \( \lambda \) produce methods that often outperform both extremes. Mechanistically, an eligibility trace \( E_t(s) \) records how recently and how frequently each state has been visited — it decays by \( \gamma \lambda \) per step and is incremented when the state is visited. When a TD error \( \delta_t \) occurs, it updates all states proportional to their eligibility trace: \( \Delta V_t(s) = \alpha \delta_t E_t(s) \). This allows credit (or blame) to propagate backward efficiently across multiple time steps. The same idea extends to control through Sarsa(\( \lambda \)) and Watkins's Q(\( \lambda \)). Eligibility traces make TD methods more robust to long-delayed rewards and partially non-Markov environments.

All of these methods — Monte Carlo, TD, Sarsa, Q-learning, and TD(\( \lambda \)) — are different answers to the same question posed in this chapter: how do we compute value functions and improve policies? The Bellman equations and the GPI alternation of evaluation and improvement remain the unifying conceptual foundation.

The key: Dynamic programming is powerful and exact when a model is available and the state space is manageable. Its fundamental limitations — model dependence and \( O(|S|^2 |A|) \) complexity — motivate the model-free methods that follow. The Bellman equations and GPI framework, however, remain the conceptual foundation for all of reinforcement learning.

10. Real-World Application: Chronic Disease Treatment as an MDP

6.40 Problem Framing

Type-2 diabetes treatment involves periodic patient reviews. At each visit, a physician observes the patient's metabolic state and decides on a medication plan. The patient's future health evolves probabilistically — it may improve or deteriorate based on the treatment and natural disease progression. This sequential decision-making under uncertainty is naturally modelled as an MDP.

MDP Formulation for Diabetes Treatment.

  • State \( s \): A vector containing — survival status (living or deceased), key metabolic markers (HbA1c, total cholesterol, HDL, blood pressure), and a history of the last \( M \) medications received. The state captures everything needed to make the next medical decision.
  • Actions \( a \): Do nothing, start cholesterol medication, start blood pressure medication, or start both.
  • Transition probabilities \( p(s' \mid s, a) \): Learned from historical patient data. They encode how a patient's health markers are likely to change after a given treatment decision. If the patient reaches the deceased state, it is a terminal absorbing state.
  • Reward function: Based on Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs) — a composite measure of how long and how well the patient lives — minus treatment costs and penalty for medication side effects.

6.41 Solution and Insights

Sample Optimal Policy (simplified). After solving the MDP with dynamic programming, the resulting policy prescribes actions based on the patient's risk level and medication history:

  • Low risk, no current medication → Wait (do nothing).
  • Medium risk, no current medication → Start cholesterol or BP medication based on specific markers.
  • High risk, no current medication → Start both medications.
  • Deceased state → Do nothing (terminal).

The policy is deterministic — for each observed state, it gives a clear medical recommendation. This is exactly the kind of output expected from solving an MDP with value iteration or policy iteration.

6.42 Practical Takeaways

What this case study teaches us. Real-world MDPs require carefully constructed state representations. The state here includes both current health markers and medication history. A patient on medication for months is in a different situation from one who has never taken it, even if their current markers look similar. The transition probabilities must come from somewhere: domain expertise, historical data, or both. If these probabilities are unreliable, the resulting policy will be unreliable too. This is a key limitation of model-based DP. This application shows DP working at its best: a well-defined state space with a transition model from clinical data.

Cautions.

  • Model quality matters enormously. If the transition probabilities are estimated from noisy or biased data, the optimal policy will reflect those errors. A policy that looks optimal on paper may harm real patients.
  • State discretisation is a design choice. Real metabolic markers are continuous. Discretising them into "low/medium/high" bins loses information but makes DP tractable. The coarser the discretisation, the cruder the policy.
  • Scalability. As you add more state variables or finer discretisations, the state space explodes. This paper's approach works because the state is kept deliberately small. For richer state representations, function approximation (deep RL) becomes necessary.

The key: This case study demonstrates dynamic programming applied to a genuine sequential decision problem in healthcare. The same principles — state definition, transition modelling, reward engineering, and DP solution — apply to any domain where you have a model and a manageable state space. When the model is unavailable or the state space is too large, the field shifts to model-free and deep RL methods — the natural next step in the reinforcement learning journey.
