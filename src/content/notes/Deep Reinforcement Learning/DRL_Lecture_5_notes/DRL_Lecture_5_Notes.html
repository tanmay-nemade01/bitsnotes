5.1 Recap: From MAB to Full RL via MDP

In the earlier lectures we moved from the Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) problem — which has no state — to the full Reinforcement Learning problem, formalised as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).

Three concrete problems we discussed:

  • Traffic light control.
  • Atari game playing.
  • Recycling robot.

For each, we identified the MDP elements: states \( S \), actions \( A \), rewards \( R \), and the transition dynamics \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \).

Key Idea: The Reward Hypothesis

All of what we mean by goals and purposes can be well thought of as the maximisation of the expected value of the cumulative sum of a received scalar signal (reward).

In symbols, the agent’s job is to choose actions that maximise the expected return:

\[ \mathbb{E}[G_t] = \mathbb{E} \left[ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1} \right] \]

The agent does not look into the future — it acts in good spirit, using whatever it knows so far, hoping that the action it takes maximises the rewards on the road ahead.

5.1.1 Episodic vs. Continuing Tasks: Cart-Pole Revisited

Recall the cart–pole problem: a cart moves left/right and must keep an inverted pendulum balanced. There are two equally valid ways to formulate the reward signal, and the difference is instructive.

Example: Two ways to model Cart–Pole
  1. Episodic, +1 per timestep: For each timestep the pole is balanced, the agent gets +1. The episode ends when the pole falls. \[ G_t = 1 + 1 + \dots + 1 \quad \text{(until failure)} \] To maximise \( G_t \), the agent must keep the pole up for as long as possible. Problem: if the agent is so good that it never fails, the sum becomes infinite.
  2. Continuing, 0 per step and \( - \)1 on failure: Reward 0 at every step the pole is balanced; reward \( - \)1 at the failure step. With discounting: \[ G_t = -\gamma^K \] where \( K \) is the step at which the pole fell. The longer the agent holds, the closer this reward (still negative) gets to 0, which is better.
Intuition: Which formulation is “correct”?

Both are correct — they encode the same goal in different ways. The continuing-with-penalty form is slightly more general: it gracefully handles the (hypothetical) case where the agent balances forever, because \( \gamma^K \to 0 \) as \( K \to \infty \).

From here on, when we say “cumulative return” we mean the discounted sum.

5.1.2 Discounted Return in General

For a continuing task in which a constant reward \( c \) is received every step:

\[ G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k c = \frac{c}{1 - \gamma} \]

This shows that even with constant reward per step the sum stays finite as long as \( \gamma < 1 \).

5.1.3 Policy - A Quick Reminder

A policy \( \pi(a \mid s) \) specifies how the agent acts. Drawn on a grid, an arrow on each cell shows the action that the policy prefers (the dominant action). The policy may be:

  • Deterministic: \( \pi(\text{right} \mid X) = 1 \). Always go right from \( X \).
  • Stochastic: \( \pi(\text{right} \mid X) = 0.9 \), \( \pi(\text{down} \mid X) = 0.1 \). Mostly greedy, sometimes explore.

We are now ready to give numbers to states themselves, and that is the topic of this lecture.

5.2 The Value of a State: \( V^\pi(s) \)

5.2.1 Motivation: "How good is this state?"

When you choose an action, you cannot directly see the future. So you need a number for each state that tells you: “If I am here and I behave according to my policy, how much reward should I expect to collect from now on?” This number is the state-value function.

Intuition: The senior-manager analogy

Suppose you are promoted to senior manager. “You’re in a great state!” people say. But are you?

The value of being in that state depends on how you behave once you’re there. Two managers in the same position can end up with very different outcomes because they follow different policies. So the value of a state is meaningless without specifying whose policy we evaluate it under — hence the subscript \( \pi \).

5.2.2 Formal Definition

Definition: State-Value Function

For a policy \( \pi \), the state-value function is:

\[ V^\pi(s) \doteq \mathbb{E}_\pi [ G_t \mid S_t = s ] = \mathbb{E}_\pi \left[ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1} \;\middle|\; S_t = s \right] \]

In words: \( V^\pi(s) \) is the expected discounted return when we start in state \( s \) and follow policy \( \pi \) from then on.

5.2.3 Reading the Values on a Grid

If we annotate each cell of a grid with a number such as 70, 30, 20, ..., we are saying: if you start in this cell and follow \( \pi \), the expected total discounted reward is that number. Higher = better state to be in.

Example: \( V^\pi(s) \) Grid Visualization

5.3 The Value of an Action: \( Q^\pi(s, a) \)

5.3.1 Why We Need a Separate Function for Actions

\( V^\pi(s) \) measures the goodness of being in a state. But often we want to know: “If I take this particular action right now, what is the expected return?” That is the action-value (or \( Q \)-function).

Definition: Action-Value Function

For a policy \( \pi \), the action-value function is:

\[ Q^\pi(s, a) \doteq \mathbb{E}_\pi [ G_t \mid S_t = s, A_t = a ] \]

In words: \( Q^\pi(s, a) \) is the expected discounted return if we are in state \( s \), take action \( a \) right now, and follow policy \( \pi \) from the next step onward.

Intuition: A high-stakes project

Think of being assigned to a critical project (the state). The value of being in this project depends on what action you take next: do you take on the lead role? Delegate? Ask for help? Each action \( a \) has its own expected outcome — that is exactly what \( Q^\pi(s, a) \) captures.

5.3.2 Why \( Q \) Is Preferred Over \( V \) in Practice

Suppose we know only \( V^\pi(s) \) for every state. To choose the best action from \( s \), we would have to “look around” — check the value of every successor state and pick the one we want to move toward. But when actions have stochastic outcomes, a single action can lead to several different successor states with different probabilities; computing the action’s expected value requires extra work each time.

If, instead, we have \( Q^\pi(s, a) \) for every \( (s, a) \), action selection is trivial:

\[ \pi(s) = \arg\max_a Q^\pi(s, a) \]

No model needed at decision time. This is one of the central reasons that most practical algorithms (Q-Learning, DQN, ...) learn \( Q \) rather than \( V \).

Example: Recycling Robot — Action Outcomes are Stochastic

Recall the recycling robot from earlier lectures. From state \( \text{high} \):

  • Search: leads to \( \text{high} \) with prob. \( \alpha \) and to \( \text{low} \) with prob. \( 1 - \alpha \).
  • Wait: keeps you in \( \text{high} \) with prob. 1.

To decide between \( \text{search} \) and \( \text{wait} \) using only \( V \), you would have to compute the expected next-state value for each action. With \( Q \), you just read off \( Q(\text{high}, \text{search}) \) and \( Q(\text{high}, \text{wait}) \) and take the larger.

5.3.3 Linking \( V^\pi \) and \( Q^\pi \)

These two value functions are not independent. They are related in two natural ways:

Key Idea: Decomposition Identities

\( V \) in terms of \( Q \): averaging over actions under the policy,

\[ V^\pi(s) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi(a \mid s) Q^\pi(s, a) \tag{1} \]

\( Q \) in terms of \( V \): averaging over outcomes of the chosen action under the dynamics,

\[ Q^\pi(s, a) = \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \big[ r + \gamma V^\pi (s') \big] \tag{2} \]

5.4 Computing \( Q^\pi(s, a) \) from the Model

We now zoom in on Equation (2). It is the workhorse formula: the value of an action equals the expected immediate reward plus the discounted expected value of the next state.

5.4.1 Visualising the Action Outcomes

Imagine you are in state s and press the Up button. The intention is to move up, but the dynamics may scatter you in different directions with different probabilities and rewards.

Example: Concrete computation — pressing “Up”

Suppose pressing Up in state \( s \) has four possible outcomes:

Direction landed in Probability Immediate reward Next-state value
Up (intended) \( p_1 \) 20 70
Left (slipped) \( p_2 \) 10 100
Right (slipped) \( p_3 \) 30 80

Then, with discount \( \gamma \):

\[ Q^\pi(s, \text{Up}) = p_1(20 + \gamma \cdot 70) + p_2(10 + \gamma \cdot 100) + p_3(30 + \gamma \cdot 80) + p_4(20 + \gamma \cdot 100) \]
Intuition: Deterministic special case

If pressing Up always moves you up (no slipping), the sum collapses to a single term. With reward 20 on transition, next-state value 70, and \( \gamma = 0.9 \):

\[ Q^\pi(s, \text{Up}) = 20 + 0.9 \times 70 = 83 \]

That is just “reward now + discounted value next”. The whole sum we wrote is simply this same idea averaged over all the possible outcomes.

5.4.2 Worked Example: Start-Stop MDP - From \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \) to Expected Rewards

Before moving to the full Bellman equation, we practice the most fundamental numerical skill: taking the joint distribution \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \) and computing the marginal transition probability \( p(s' \mid s, a) \) and the expected immediate reward \( r(s, a) \).

Start-Stop MDP

A process has three states: \( R \) (Ready), \( W \) (Working), and \( T \) (Terminal). The agent starts in \( R \) and can choose Start or Stop.

\( s \) \( a \) \( s' \) \( r \) \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \)
RStartW40.6
RStartW20.2
RStartR00.2
RStopT11.0

Notice that the same next state \( W \) can appear twice under action Start, each time with a different reward. This is why \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \) stores the joint distribution over both.

Step 1 — Verify probabilities sum to 1 for each \( (s, a) \):

For (R, Start): 0.6 + 0.2 + 0.2 = 1.0   \( \checkmark \)
For (R, Stop): 1.0 = 1.0   \( \checkmark \)

Step 2 — Compute state-transition probability \( p(s' \mid s, a) \) by summing over all rewards:

\[ p(s' \mid s, a) = \sum_{r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \] \[ \begin{aligned} p(W \mid R, \text{Start}) &= 0.6 + 0.2 = \mathbf{0.8} \\ p(R \mid R, \text{Start}) &= \mathbf{0.2} \\ p(T \mid R, \text{Stop}) &= \mathbf{1.0} \end{aligned} \]

Step 3 — Compute expected immediate reward \( r(s, a) \):

\[ r(s, a) = \sum_{r} r \sum_{s'} p(s', r \mid s, a) \] \[ \begin{aligned} r(R, \text{Start}) &= 4(0.6) + 2(0.2) + 0(0.2) = 2.4 + 0.4 = \mathbf{2.8} \\ r(R, \text{Stop}) &= 1(1.0) = \mathbf{1.0} \end{aligned} \]

On immediate reward alone, Start (\( 2.8 \)) beats Stop (\( 1.0 \)). But a full MDP solution must also account for where each action leads and what value those next states carry.

Step 4 — Compute conditional expected reward \( r(s, a, s') \) when the next state is also known:

\[ r(s, a, s') = \sum_{r} r \cdot \frac{p(s', r \mid s, a)}{p(s' \mid s, a)} \] \[ \begin{aligned} r(R, \text{Start}, W) &= 4(0.6/0.8) + 2(0.2/0.8) = 4(0.75) + 2(0.25) = \mathbf{3.5} \\ r(R, \text{Start}, R) &= 0(0.2/0.2) = \mathbf{0} \\ r(R, \text{Stop}, T) &= 1(1.0/1.0) = \mathbf{1.0} \end{aligned} \]
Key Idea: The triple \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \) is the fundamental building block

Everything we compute in this lecture — \( p(s' \mid s, a) \), \( r(s, a) \), \( Q^\pi(s, a) \), \( V^\pi(s) \) — starts from this joint distribution. Two rows in the table can share the same \( s' \) but differ in reward; that is not a mistake, it is how stochastic environments are naturally modelled.

5.5 Value-Based RL: The Big Picture

Key Idea: What is a value-based algorithm?

A value-based Reinforcement Learning algorithm is one that:

  1. Spends most of its compute estimating value functions (\( V \), \( Q \), or both).
  2. Derives the policy from those values, typically by acting greedily with respect to them.

Value Iteration is the prototypical example. In later lectures we will see policy-based methods that work the other way around.

5.5.1 Where Value Iteration Fits

Value Iteration is a Dynamic Programming (DP) algorithm. It assumes that the dynamics \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \) are known. Once we drop this assumption and learn from samples instead, we get the family of model-free algorithms (Monte Carlo, TD, Q-Learning, ...) — the subject of upcoming lectures.

5.5.2 Two Primitive Steps Behind Every DP Algorithm

Every Dynamic Programming algorithm we will study can be decomposed into two primitive operations:

  • Policy Evaluation: given a policy \( \pi \), compute \( V^\pi \) by repeatedly applying the Bellman expectation equation.
  • Policy Improvement: given \( V^\pi \), derive a new policy \( \pi' \) that is greedy with respect to \( V^\pi \). The result is provably at least as good as \( \pi \).

Policy Iteration alternates these two steps explicitly. Value Iteration merges them into one step (each backup is simultaneously an evaluation and an improvement, because of the embedded max).

5.5.3 Generalized Policy Iteration (GPI)

Almost all RL algorithms, whether DP-based or sample-based, fit into the framework of Generalized Policy Iteration: the interaction of policy evaluation and policy improvement.

Key Idea: GPI unifies all value-based RL

Evaluation makes the value function more consistent with the current policy. Improvement makes the policy greedier with respect to the current value function. These two forces push toward the same goal — the optimal value function and optimal policy — like two competing but cooperating processes.

Different algorithms are different granularities of GPI:

  • Policy Iteration: coarse-grained. Evaluate nearly completely, then improve.
  • Value Iteration: fine-grained. Each backup evaluates one step and improves via max.
  • Asynchronous DP: flexible. Evaluation/improvement backups applied to selected states, not all states uniformly.
  • Monte Carlo & TD control: sample-based GPI. Evaluation learned from experience; improvement performed gradually.

The two processes compete and cooperate: making the policy greedy with respect to the value function makes the value function incorrect for the new policy; making the value function consistent with the policy makes the policy no longer greedy. The textbook visualises this as two non-orthogonal constraint lines in a 2D space:

Key Idea: The competing-constraints view of GPI \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Constraint 1: } & V = V^\pi \quad \text{(value consistent with policy)} \\ \text{Constraint 2: } & \pi = \text{greedy}(V) \quad \text{(policy greedy w.r.t. value)} \end{aligned} \]

Each process drives toward one constraint line. Because the lines are not orthogonal, moving directly toward one goal causes some movement away from the other. Yet the joint process inevitably spirals toward the unique intersection — the optimal value function and optimal policy. This is the geometric essence of why GPI works: each step may not be monotonic in both dimensions, but the long-run trajectory converges.

As long as the value function becomes more accurate for the current policy and the policy becomes greedier with respect to the value function, the overall process follows GPI logic and converges (in finite MDPs) to optimality.

5.5.4 Asynchronous Dynamic Programming

Classical DP algorithms sweep every state in every iteration. Asynchronous DP updates states in some chosen order, using the most recent values available at the time of update. The Bellman backup itself is unchanged:

\[ V(s) \leftarrow \max_a \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a) \big[ r + \gamma V(s') \big] \]

Only the schedule of which states get updated when changes. This is useful in several situations:

  • Large tabular problems: full sweeps may be costly; updating only important states reduces computation.
  • Real-time planning: updates concentrated around states the agent currently visits.
  • Prioritized updates: states with large expected Bellman error are updated earlier.
  • Bridge to model-free methods: many sample-based RL algorithms can be viewed as partial, local, or sampled Bellman-style updates.

For finite MDPs, asynchronous DP converges when no relevant state is ignored forever.

5.5.5 Practical Issues in Applying DP

DP is exact and elegant, but it imposes strong assumptions. Understanding its limitations clarifies why later RL methods exist:

  1. Known model required. DP needs \( p(s', r \mid s, a) \) or equivalent. Without it, expected backups cannot be computed exactly — use Monte Carlo or TD instead.
  2. Finite, manageable state space. Tabular DP stores one value per state. Infeasible for large or continuous spaces — use function approximation.
  3. Discrete actions. DP requires enumerable actions to apply the \( \max \) operator. For continuous controls, use policy-gradient methods.
  4. Policy may stabilize before values converge. The greedy policy often stops changing before \( V \) is fully accurate. Monitor policy stability, not just value convergence.
  5. Extracting policy from \( V \) requires lookahead. To act from \( V \) only, you must use the model at decision time. Maintaining \( Q \)(s, a) avoids this repeated lookahead.

These issues are not flaws — they are what make DP the clear theoretical foundation and what motivate the rich family of approximate, sample-based methods that follow.

DP Efficiency: Why is DP still relevant?

DP is sometimes dismissed because of the curse of dimensionality (Bellman, 1957): the number of states grows exponentially with the number of state variables. A backgammon game, for example, has over \( 10^{20} \) states — infeasible for tabular DP even on modern hardware.

Yet in the problems where DP does apply (state spaces up to millions), it is remarkably efficient. The time to find an optimal policy is polynomial in the number of states and actions. Compare this to brute-force search over all deterministic policies, which would be \( m^n \) (\( n \) states, \( m \) actions) — exponential. DP is exponentially faster than direct policy-space search.

Moreover, asynchronous DP methods can focus computation on states that actually occur along near-optimal trajectories. If an MDP has \( 10^6 \) states but optimal paths visit only a few thousand, asynchronous updates that concentrate on visited states can find good policies without sweeping the entire state space. This idea of focusing computation on relevant states is a recurring theme in reinforcement learning, reappearing in sample-based methods (Monte Carlo, TD) and eventually in deep RL with experience replay.

5.6 Summary & Key Takeaways

Key Idea: What you should remember from Lecture 5
  1. Two value functions: \( V^\pi(s) \) = goodness of being in state \( s \); \( Q^\pi(s, a) \) = goodness of taking action \( a \) in state \( s \). Both are expectations of discounted return under \( \pi \).
  2. \( Q \) is preferred when actions are stochastic: it makes greedy action selection trivial and model-free.
  3. Bellman expectation equation: \( V^\pi(s) \) = \( \sum \)a \( \pi \)(a | s) \( \sum \)\( s' \), r p(\( s' \), r | s, a)[ r + \( \gamma \) \( V^\pi \)(\( s' \)) ]. Defines \( V^\pi \) recursively in terms of \( V^\pi \) of successor states.
  4. Bellman optimality equation: same structure, but with \( \max_a \) replacing \( \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \).
  5. Value Iteration: simplest algorithm using these ideas. Initialise values, repeatedly apply the Bellman optimality update, stop when changes drop below a threshold, then read off the greedy policy.
  6. Two sweep styles: synchronous (not-in-place) vs. asynchronous (in-place); the latter converges faster.

  1. Grid-world verification: Pick any other cell in the grid-world table (Section 6.2) and verify the Bellman expectation equation holds with \( \gamma = 0.9 \) under the equiprobable random policy. (Try cell (4, 3) which has value \( -0.4 \).)
  2. Optimal policy from \( V^* \): Suppose for some MDP you have: \( V \)^*(s_1) = 5 \), \( V \)^*(s_2) = 8 \), \( V \)^*(s_3) = 3 \), \( \gamma = 0.9 \). From state \( s_0 \), action \( a \) has the dynamics:
    • With prob. 0.6: reward 1, go to \( s_2 \).
    • With prob. 0.4: reward \( -2 \), go to \( s_3 \).
    Compute \( Q \)^*(s_0, a) \).
  3. Race-car continuation: Continue the race-car Value Iteration trace for two more iterations. Does the optimal action \( a \)t cool change? Does it change at warm?
  4. Reward design: Re-design the cart–pole reward so that the optimal policy is the same but the agent receives a small positive reward at every step instead of zero (for the continuing formulation). What is the effect of this on \( V^* \)?
