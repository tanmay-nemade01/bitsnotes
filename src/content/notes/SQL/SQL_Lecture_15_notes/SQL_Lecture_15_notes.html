# Using DATETIME Functions

15.1 DATE, TIME and TIMESTAMP Types

15.1.1 The three main types

Why does every date function in this lecture take a different input shape? Because PostgreSQL splits calendar time into three separate types, and each function only accepts the piece it was built for.

A date (a calendar day with no clock reading, such as 2020-01-01) holds a calendar day only. A time (a clock reading with no calendar day, such as 10:30:45) holds a clock reading only. A timestamp (a calendar day joined to a clock reading, such as 2020-01-01 10:30:45) holds both together. The spoken rule is short: date means day, time means clock, timestamp means day plus clock. Every later function in this material builds on that split: conversion functions target one of these three, extractors slice one of these three, and table columns store one of these three.

Think of a wall calendar, a stopwatch, and a diary entry. The calendar names the day but never the hour. The stopwatch names the hour but never the day. The diary entry stamps both, so it alone pins an event to a single instant. The analogy breaks in one place: a real stopwatch counts elapsed time, while SQL time-of-day names a position on the clock face.

Formalize. The three types partition an instant \(t\) into a day part \(D\) and a clock part \(C\):

\[ \text{timestamp} = (D, C), \quad \text{date} = D, \quad \text{time} = C \]

Here \(D\) is the triple \((YYYY, MM, DD)\), the four-digit year, two-digit month, and two-digit day. Here \(C\) is the triple \((HH, MI, SS)\), the hour, minute, and second. A date keeps \(D\) and drops \(C\); a time keeps \(C\) and drops \(D\); a timestamp keeps both. Zone-aware variants add a zone offset \(Z\) to time or timestamp, giving \((C, Z)\) or \((D, C, Z)\).

Worked example — pick the right type. An event log records "order 441 placed on 2020-03-14 at 09:41:07".

  • Day-only question ("how many orders per day?") needs the date 2020-03-14.
  • Clock-only question ("do orders cluster before noon?") needs the time 09:41:07.
  • Exact ordering ("which order came first?") needs the timestamp 2020-03-14 09:41:07.

Answer: all three questions are answerable only if the column stores the full timestamp, since day and clock parts can be sliced out of it but never rebuilt from each other. Sense-check: dropping the clock loses order within a day, and dropping the day loses order across days.

Scope: the three-type split applies to PostgreSQL and standard SQL. Assumption: each value is a valid calendar instant; the types reject impossible readings such as month 13 at input time, which later sections use as range guards.

Picture a timeline drawn left to right in days, with each day divided into 24 hourly ticks. A date paints one whole day-block. A time paints one tick position repeated on every block. A timestamp paints a single tick inside a single block. The takeaway: only the timestamp marks one point; the other two mark repeating patterns.

Two traps catch beginners. First, storing a clock reading in a date column silently loses the time half, and storing a day in a time column loses the day half; the missing half cannot be recovered. Second, mixing zoned and unzoned values in one comparison compares different things, since midday in New York is night in Singapore — clocks differ by place, and daylight saving shifts add more splits.

Other databases draw the same split with different names. MySQL keeps DATE, DATETIME (day plus clock, no zone), and TIMESTAMP (day plus clock stored in UTC and shown in the session zone, valid only from 1970 to 2038). Snowflake offers TIMESTAMP_NTZ (no zone), TIMESTAMP_LTZ (session zone), and TIMESTAMP_TZ (stored zone). Databricks keeps DATE and TIMESTAMP. Where the names match PostgreSQL, the behavior below carries over; where they differ, the per-section notes call it out.

Date keeps the day, time keeps the clock, timestamp keeps both — and every function ahead targets exactly one of the three. That split leads straight into the digit shapes each type enforces.

Exam note: range and shape questions recur, so memorize the three-way split cold: date means day only, time means clock only, timestamp means day plus clock.

15.1.2 DATE layout and range

How does the database know that 2020-01-01 means January 1st and not January 1st of some other order? Because the DATE type fixes one digit shape and reads everything through it.

A date stores year, month and day in that order, written \(YYYY\text{-}MM\text{-}DD\). The verbal form is "four-digit year, two-digit month, two-digit day, joined by dashes". Here \(YYYY\) is the four-digit year, \(MM\) is the month from \(01\) to \(12\), and \(DD\) is the day of the month from \(01\) to \(31\). Single digits get a leading zero on the left: January is \(01\) not \(1\), and the eighth day is \(08\) not \(8\), while the tenth is simply \(10\).

Think of a date as a postal address written largest-to-smallest: country, then street, then house number. Year, then month, then day sorts in time order with plain text sorting, which is exactly why the largest unit comes first. The analogy breaks for display: people read smallest-first in many countries, so storage order and display order differ.

Formalize. The DATE layout is the fixed template:

\[ D = YYYY\text{-}MM\text{-}DD \]

Counting positions: four for the year, two for the month, two for the day, plus two dashes, so \(4 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 10\) characters. The allowed range runs from \(0001\text{-}01\text{-}01\) to \(9999\text{-}12\text{-}31\), nearly ten thousand years of calendar days.

Worked example — leading-zero shapes. Convert three spoken dates to storage shape:

  • January 1st, 2020: month \(1 \rightarrow 01\), day \(1 \rightarrow 01\), giving 2020-01-01.
  • December 8th, 2020: month \(12\), day \(8 \rightarrow 08\), giving 2020-12-08.
  • October 10th, 2020: month \(10\), day \(10\), giving 2020-10-10.

Answer: the two-digit storage shapes are 01, 08, and 10 — single digits gain a leading zero, double digits stay as written. Sense-check: every result is exactly ten characters, which confirms the shape.

Scope: the ten-character shape describes the ISO text form. Assumption: Gregorian calendar days; month lengths and leap years still bound \(DD\), so 2020-02-30 is rejected even though its shape looks right.

Picture a ruler marked 0001-01-01 at the left end and 9999-12-31 at the right end, with one tick per day. Any query date lands somewhere on that ruler, and out-of-range input falls off the end. The takeaway: shape checks digits, range checks the calendar.

Beginners trip twice here. First, writing \(1\) instead of \(01\) breaks the fixed shape and can flip month-day parsing under some DATESTYLE orders. Second, assuming every month holds 31 days: April, June, September, and November hold 30, and February holds 28 or 29.

DATE is ten fixed positions, \(YYYY\text{-}MM\text{-}DD\), spanning 0001-01-01 to 9999-12-31 — and the same fixed-shape idea governs the clock half next.

Exam note: memorize DATE 0001-01-01 to 9999-12-31 at ten positions; the count \(4 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 10\) is a favorite one-mark question.

15.1.3 TIME layout with and without zone

What does half past two in the afternoon look like inside the database? Not 02:30 PM — the stored form is 14:30:00, and this section shows why.

A time stores hour, minute and second as \(HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS\). Here \(HH\) is the hour from \(00\) to \(23\), \(MI\) is the minute from \(00\) to \(59\), and \(SS\) is the second from \(00\) to \(59\). Hours and minutes each take two digits, so 2 in the morning is \(02\) and 4 in the afternoon is \(16\), because storage follows the 24-hour clock and afternoon hours add 12. Seconds take two digits and may grow an optional fractional part with up to six decimal places, for example \(10.436\) seconds, where the spoken form is "ten point four three six seconds with six-digit precision". A time without zone takes eight positions including the two colons, written \(HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS\), with an optional \(.p\) tail where \(p\) counts digits right of the decimal point. A time with zone keeps the same clock part plus zone info.

Think of a 24-hour railway clock: the face runs 00 at midnight to 23 at 11 PM, so afternoon readings never repeat morning ones. The analogy breaks across cities: two identical railway clocks in New York and Singapore show different times at the same instant, which is why the zoned variant exists.

Formalize. The TIME layout with its fractional tail is:

\[ C = HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS[.p] \]

Here \(HH \in [00, 23]\), \(MI \in [00, 59]\), \(SS \in [00, 59]\), and \(p\) counts kept digits after the decimal point, from \(0\) to \(6\). A time with zone appends the zone offset \(Z\), giving \((C, Z)\). Zones exist because clocks differ by place: midday in New York is night in Singapore, so the same instant reads differently per zone, and daylight saving shifts add more splits.

Worked example — AM/PM to storage. Convert 2:10 AM and 6:10 PM to stored shapes:

  • 2:10 AM: hour \(2 \rightarrow 02\), minutes \(10\), giving 02:10:00.
  • 6:10 PM: hour \(6 + 12 = 18\), minutes \(10\), giving 18:10:00.

Answer: AM keeps the hour, PM adds twelve. Sense-check: 18 falls inside the 00–23 band while 30 would not, which previews the hour-30 range guard met later.

Scope: bare TIME has no date part, so it cannot order events across days. Assumption: the fractional tail counts fractions of a second, not milliseconds by default — \(.4\) means four-tenths of a second.

Picture a single clock face with the hour hand sweeping 00 to 23 over a full day, and a magnifier over the seconds hand showing six decimal places. The takeaway: the clock face position is exact to the microsecond, but it still floats free of any calendar day.

Three traps live here. First, writing minute codes where month codes belong (or the reverse) — month is \(MM\) but minute is \(MI\), a clash revisited under TO_TIMESTAMP. Second, assuming 12-hour input stores as written; PM must add 12. Third, treating a zoneless time as an instant; without a zone it names a wall-clock reading, not a point in time.

MySQL stores TIME in the same \(HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS\) shape but allows hour values past 23 for elapsed intervals (such as 838:59:59). PostgreSQL rejects those; use an interval type instead.

TIME is \(HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS\) on a 24-hour clock with a six-digit fraction room and an optional zone — and joining it to a DATE gives the timestamp.

Exam note: memorize TIME \(HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS\) with six-digit fraction room; the PM-adds-twelve conversion is tested as \(6 + 12 = 18\).

15.1.4 TIMESTAMP layout

If DATE names the day and TIME names the clock reading, what single value answers "exactly when"? The timestamp — this section joins the two halves.

A timestamp joins the two halves into \(YYYY\text{-}MM\text{-}DD\ HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS\). The verbal form is "year month day, then hour minute second". Its date and time halves follow the same limits as the standalone types, except the default length of the fractional part of the time half is six digits rather than zero. A plain date such as \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\) becomes the timestamp \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 00\text{:}00\text{:}00\) plus any wanted precision. The closing rule of this part: date, time and timestamp are the three types, each has a fixed digit shape, and zone-aware variants exist for time and timestamp.

Think of the diary entry again: the page header is the date, the margin note is the clock time, and only the two together identify the moment. The analogy breaks the same way: a diary page without a city name still floats across zones, so worldwide logs need the zoned variant.

Formalize. The TIMESTAMP layout concatenates the DATE and TIME templates with a blank between them:

\[ T = YYYY\text{-}MM\text{-}DD\ HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS[.p] \]

The date half obeys the DATE limits, the clock half obeys the TIME limits, and the fractional default is \(p = 6\) digits. The zoned form appends the offset, \((T, Z)\), for example \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 10\text{:}30\text{:}45\text{-}05\).

Worked example — date to timestamp. Promote the date 2020-01-01 to a timestamp:

  • Date half stays \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\).
  • Missing clock half defaults to midnight \(00\text{:}00\text{:}00\).

Answer: 2020-01-01 00:00:00. Sense-check: midnight is the day's first instant, so the promoted value sorts before every other stamp on that day.

Scope: a plain timestamp names wall time, not an absolute instant; comparing stamps from different zones needs the zoned form. Assumption: midnight defaulting applies when the clock half is absent.

Picture the day-ruler from 15.1.2 with each day-block now subdivided into 86,400 second-ticks (since \(24 \times 3600 = 86400\)). A timestamp points at one tick. The takeaway: timestamps order events exactly; dates and times alone cannot.

Beginners assume a timestamp "knows" its zone. It does not: the plain form stores wall time only. A second trap is reading \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 00\text{:}00\text{:}00\) as "no time recorded" — midnight is a real instant, distinct from a missing value.

A timestamp is DATE plus TIME with a six-digit default fraction, zoned when the application spans cities — and how those values print on screen is controlled by DATESTYLE next.

15.2 System DATESTYLE Setting

15.2.1 SHOW DATESTYLE

Your query returns 01/02/2020 — is that January 2nd or February 1st? The answer depends on one server setting, and this section shows how to read it.

The DATESTYLE (the server setting that controls how dates print and how input strings get read) decides both directions: how stored dates look on screen and how typed strings get parsed. The check command is:

SHOW DATESTYLE;

The answer has two halves, for example ISO, MDY. The first half is the type, here \(ISO\). The second half is the order, here \(MDY\), spoken as "month, then day, then year". The default seen here is ISO with month-day-year order.

Think of DATESTYLE as a pair of reading glasses the server wears: the stored date never changes, but the glasses decide which digit you see first. Swap the glasses and the same stored value prints differently. The analogy breaks for input: the glasses also decide how your typed strings are understood, so a wrong setting misreads new data, not just old displays.

Formalize. DATESTYLE is the ordered pair:

\[ \text{DATESTYLE} = (\text{type}, \text{order}) \]

The type half accepts four values: \(ISO\), \(Postgres\), \(SQL\), or \(German\). The order half accepts \(MDY\) (month-day-year), \(DMY\) (day-month-year), or \(YMD\) (year-month-day). Storage stays in ISO shape regardless; only presentation and parsing follow the setting.

Worked example — read the setting. Run SHOW DATESTYLE; and get back ISO, MDY:

  • Type half \(ISO\) fixes the punctuation style (dashes, ISO 8601 look).
  • Order half \(MDY\) means input 01-02-2020 reads as January 2nd, 2020.

Answer: ISO type with month-first order. Sense-check: flipping the order half to DMY would read the same string as February 1st — same stored bytes, different reading.

Scope: DATESTYLE affects the current session only unless set globally. Assumption: clients that format dates themselves may ignore it; server-side casts and psql output obey it.

Picture the stored date 2020-01-02 behind a frosted window with three sliding panels labeled MDY, DMY, YMD. Sliding MDY forward shows 01-02-2020; sliding DMY shows 02-01-2020. The takeaway: one stored value, three possible readings — always check the panel first.

The classic trap is debugging "wrong" dates that are only wrongly displayed: the rows are fine, the glasses are wrong. Never "fix" stored data when SHOW DATESTYLE explains the display.

SHOW DATESTYLE reveals the (type, order) glasses — and the next section changes them.

15.2.2 SET DATESTYLE

You found the glasses are wrong — how do you swap them without touching a single stored row? One SET command.

The change command names a type plus an order:

SET DATESTYLE TO 'ISO, DMY';

The verbal form is "set date style to type ISO, order day-month-year". The type half accepts four values: \(ISO\), \(Postgres\), \(SQL\), or \(German\). The order half accepts \(MDY\) for month-day-year as used widely in the US, \(DMY\) for day-month-year as used in parts of Asia, or \(YMD\) for year-month-day. After running the set command, a second SHOW DATESTYLE; confirms the switch, here to ISO, DMY. To return, run:

SET DATESTYLE TO 'ISO, MDY';

and confirm again with SHOW DATESTYLE;. The closing rule: show to inspect, set with type plus order to change, show again to confirm.

Formalize. The SET cycle is a three-step loop:

\[ \text{SHOW} \rightarrow \text{SET TO (type, order)} \rightarrow \text{SHOW} \]

Each arrow is one command. The first SHOW records the starting pair, SET replaces it for the session, and the second SHOW proves the switch landed.

Worked example — SHOW/SET/SHOW cycle. Starting from ISO, MDY:

  1. SHOW DATESTYLE; returns ISO, MDY (month-first reading).
  2. SET DATESTYLE TO 'ISO, DMY'; switches to day-first reading.
  3. SHOW DATESTYLE; returns ISO, DMY, confirming the switch.
  4. SET DATESTYLE TO 'ISO, MDY'; plus a final SHOW restores the start.

Answer: the full round trip is show, set, show, set, show. Sense-check: the stored dates never moved — only the two SHOW outputs differ, which proves the change is display-only.

Scope: SET without GLOBAL or session-pool care affects only the current session; pooled connections may reset it. Assumption: input strings typed after the switch parse under the new order, so old scripts with ambiguous dates can change meaning.

Picture a light switch labeled MDY/DMY next to the frosted window: flip it and every date on screen redraws at once, while the stored values sit untouched behind the glass. The takeaway: SET repaints the display, never the data.

Two traps bite here. First, running SET DATESTYLE mid-script and forgetting it changes how later string literals parse — 01-02-2020 means a different day before and after. Second, assuming other databases share this knob: MySQL leans on STR_TO_DATE patterns instead, and Snowflake uses per-session parameters, so carry the concept (display versus storage) rather than the exact syntax.

Show to inspect, SET with type plus order to change, show again to confirm — and with display under control, the next section covers the input writings the server accepts.

Exam note: DATESTYLE show/set pairs are easy marks, including the four types and the MDY, DMY, YMD orders.

15.3 Time-of-Day and Timestamp Formats with Special Inputs

15.3.1 Time-of-day writings

Can you type 6:10 PM, or must every clock reading arrive in 24-hour shape? Both work — but only one shape gets stored.

A clock reading such as 2:10 in the morning is written \(02\text{:}10\) with the leading zero kept. The verbal form is "zero two colon ten AM". The same digits with PM, \(06\text{:}10\) PM, are stored as \(18\text{:}10\), because \(6 + 12 = 18\) on the 24-hour clock, while AM keeps \(06\text{:}10\) as is. Full precision adds seconds and the fraction: \(06\text{:}10\text{:}10.786\), spoken as "six hours, ten minutes, ten point seven eight six seconds with six decimal places". The ISO 8601 shape is \(HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS\).

Think of airport departure boards: flights print 18:10, never "6:10 PM", because one number line removes all AM/PM doubt. Input accepts the friendly form; storage keeps the board form. The analogy breaks for fractions: boards never show microseconds, but the database does.

Formalize. Accepted clock input follows:

\[ \text{input} \in \{HH\text{:}MI\ [AM|PM],\ HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS[.p]\}, \quad \text{stored} = HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS \]

AM keeps the hour; PM adds 12, so \(6 + 12 = 18\). Seconds default to \(00\) when absent and carry up to six fraction digits when present.

Worked example — PM stored as eighteen. Type 06:10 PM:

  • Strip the PM tag and add 12: \(6 + 12 = 18\).
  • Keep minutes \(10\) and default seconds \(00\).

Answer: stored as 18:10:00. Sense-check: 18:10 sorts after every AM reading, matching the real order of the day. A second case: \(06\text{:}10\text{:}10.786\) keeps all three fraction digits, storing ten point seven eight six seconds.

Scope: AM/PM tags apply to 12-hour input only; hour 18 with a PM tag is rejected. Assumption: midnight is 00 (12 AM maps to 00, and 12 PM maps to 12).

Picture the airport board flipping from 06:10 PM to 18:10 as the input gate closes — same flight, board spelling. The takeaway: type friendly, store strict.

Beginners write hour 24 for midnight (midnight is 00) and attach AM/PM to 24-hour values (tags belong to 12-hour input only).

AM keeps the hour, PM adds twelve, storage is always \(HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS\) — and the same strictness governs full timestamps.

15.3.2 Timestamp writings

What does a complete timestamp look like when every half is filled in? One joined string — and this section builds it piece by piece.

A timestamp joins date and time, for example \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 10\text{:}30\text{:}45\). Here \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\) is the date half and \(10\text{:}30\text{:}45\) is the time half ending in 45 seconds. Extra precision appends the fraction: \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 10\text{:}30\text{:}45.786\). A zone-aware variant appends the offset as well. The examples here show timestamps without zone first, then note the with-zone form exists.

Formalize. The full written form stacks the two halves:

\[ T = \underbrace{YYYY\text{-}MM\text{-}DD}_{\text{date half}}\ \underbrace{HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS[.p]}_{\text{time half}}[\pm ZH\text{:}ZM] \]

The bracketed tail is the optional zone offset. Omitting the fraction defaults it to zero; omitting the zone leaves wall time.

Worked example — build the stamp. Assemble January 1st 2020, half past ten, 45.786 seconds:

  • Date half: 2020-01-01.
  • Time half: 10:30:45.786.

Answer: 2020-01-01 10:30:45.786. Sense-check: removing the fraction gives 10:30:45 exactly, so the fraction only refines, never shifts, the reading.

Scope: the blank between halves is required; 2020-01-0110:30:45 does not parse. Assumption: zoneless stamps compare as wall time.

Picture the diary entry with the page header on the left, a blank gutter, then the margin time on the right, with an optional city sticker (the zone) on the corner. The takeaway: date, gutter, clock, optional sticker.

The common trap is gluing date to time without the separating blank, or misreading a zoneless stamp as UTC. A bare stamp names wall time in no particular city.

Timestamp writing is date, blank, clock, optional fraction, optional zone — and several keyword shortcuts bypass all that typing.

15.3.3 Special date-time inputs

Must every date be typed digit by digit? No — seven keywords stand in for the most-typed values.

Several keyword inputs are accepted. The keyword \(allballs\), spoken "all balls", gives a time value. The keyword \(now\) gives date, time and timestamp together, holding the current transaction time. The keywords \(today\), \(tomorrow\), \(yesterday\), \(epoch\), \(infinity\) and \(-infinity\) give date and timestamp values but no bare time. The \(epoch\) keyword is the base point for exact math between dates and is used heavily later. The \(infinity\) keyword sorts later than every other timestamp, and \(-infinity\) sorts earlier than every other timestamp.

Think of these as speed-dial buttons on a phone: each one dials a full number you would otherwise punch digit by digit. The analogy breaks for \(now\): unlike a fixed contact, it redials the current instant on every call.

Formalize. The keyword map is:

\[ \begin{aligned} allballs &\rightarrow \text{time } 00\text{:}00\text{:}00 \\ now &\rightarrow (D_{txn}, C_{txn}) \\ today,\ tomorrow,\ yesterday &\rightarrow \text{midnight of that day} \\ epoch &\rightarrow 1970\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 00\text{:}00\text{:}00\ UTC \\ infinity &\rightarrow +\infty\ (\text{sorts last}), \quad -infinity \rightarrow -\infty\ (\text{sorts first}) \end{aligned} \]

Here \((D_{txn}, C_{txn})\) is the current transaction instant. Only \(now\) fits all three column kinds; \(today\) and its siblings carry day info, so they never fit a bare time.

Worked example — PM hour stored as eighteen. Revisit the conversion \(6 + 12 = 18\) as the section's anchor case: input 06:10 PM stores 18:10. Sense-check: 18 lies inside 00–23 while 30 would not, which is why the hour-30 probe fails later. A second case: keyword \(now\) at transaction time returns the same instant to date, time, and timestamp columns at once.

Scope: keywords are PostgreSQL spellings; MySQL uses functions like NOW() instead. Assumption: \(now\) freezes at transaction start — statements inside one transaction share it.

Picture the phone pad: seven speed-dial keys labeled allballs, now, today, tomorrow, yesterday, epoch, and infinity, each filling a full date form with one press. The takeaway: keywords trade typing for fixed meanings — learn each key's exact fill.

Two traps: expecting \(today\) to fit a TIME column (it carries a day, so it cannot), and expecting \(now\) to tick inside a transaction (it freezes; CLOCK_TIMESTAMP() is the ticking alternative met in 15.13).

Seven keywords shortcut the typing — now for the transaction instant, day-words for midnights, epoch for the math base, infinities for open-ended bounds — and the next section converts arbitrary text through explicit patterns.

Exam note: memorize which keywords fit bare TIME (only allballs and now) versus date and timestamp (all seven); the fit table is a favorite applied item.

15.4 TO_DATE Conversion

15.4.1 Syntax and pattern table

Vendor files arrive as 'December 1, 2020', '01-01-2020', '2020-01-01' — three spellings of real dates. How does one function read them all? By handing it a pattern that describes each spelling.

The TO_DATE (the text-to-date converter: first argument the string, second argument the pattern describing it) turns a text string into a date. The shape is:

TO_DATE('2020-01-01', 'YYYY-MM-DD');

The verbal form is "to underscore date takes the string, then the pattern that describes it". The first argument is the string. The second argument is the pattern. Key pattern codes: \(YYYY\) is the four-digit year; \(MONTH\) is the full month name in capitals; \(Month\) is the full name with only the first letter capital; \(month\) is the full name in lowercase; \(MON\), \(Mon\), \(mon\) are the three-letter shortened forms in the same three cases, such as JAN, Jan, jan; \(MM\) is the month number \(01\) to \(12\); \(DAY\), \(Day\), \(day\) are the full day names in the same three cases; \(DD\) is the day of the month \(01\) to \(31\); \(D\) is the day of the week with Sunday as \(1\) through Saturday as \(7\); \(W\) is the week of the month \(1\) to \(5\) counted from the first day; \(WW\) is the week of the year, normally \(52\) or \(53\) weeks depending on the calendar. The \(D\) code helps pick business days by dropping \(1\) and \(7\).

Think of the pattern as a labeled stencil laid over the string: each cutout is named (YYYY, MM, DD), and the characters showing through fall into the named slots. The analogy breaks when stencil and string disagree — a real stencil still shows something, but TO_DATE raises an error instead.

Formalize. The TO_DATE call is the mapping:

\[ \text{TO\_DATE}(s, p) = D, \quad D = (YYYY, MM, DD) \]

Here \(s\) is the input string and \(p\) is the pattern; each code in \(p\) consumes its matching characters from \(s\). Unfilled slots default (missing day becomes \(01\)). Case matters for name codes: \(MONTH\) matches DECEMBER, \(Month\) matches December, \(MON\) matches Dec-length abbreviations only.

Worked example — ISO string maps slots. Run TO_DATE('2020-01-01', 'YYYY-MM-DD'):

  • \(2020\) falls through the \(YYYY\) cutout.
  • \(01\) falls through the \(MM\) cutout.
  • \(01\) falls through the \(DD\) cutout.

Answer: date 2020-01-01. Sense-check: the output prints in server style year-month-day, confirming the stored value is a real date, not echoed text.

Scope: TO_DATE returns a DATE, never a clock reading; time codes in the pattern are ignored. Assumption: the pattern fully describes the string — every dash, comma, and blank in \(s\) needs its match in \(p\).

Picture the stencil sliding over 'December 1, 2020': the Month cutout catches "December", the DD cutout catches "1", the YYYY cutout catches "2020", and the comma and blank have their own fixed slots. The takeaway: describe everything, including punctuation.

Three traps decide pass or fail. First, \(MM\) is month but \(MI\) is minute — in TO_DATE only \(MM\) applies, and borrowing \(MI\) from timestamp habits misfires. Second, \(MON\) versus \(MONTH\): the full name does not fit the short slot ("invalid value December for Mon"). Third, \(DD\) (month-day) versus \(D\) (week-day): swapping them silently builds the wrong date.

Other databases share the idea with shifted syntax. MySQL uses STR_TO_DATE with %-codes (%Y-%m-%d for the ISO shape). Snowflake and Databricks accept TO_DATE with similar pattern letters. Carry the stencil habit; translate the code letters per engine.

TO_DATE lays a labeled stencil over text — and the next section stress-tests what happens when stencil and string disagree.

15.4.2 Worked examples

What actually happens when the stencil and the string disagree — a wrong answer, or a loud error? Loud errors, and each one teaches a rule.

Example one maps an ISO string. Input '2020-01-01' with pattern 'YYYY-MM-DD' returns the date \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\), and the result type is date. Here \(2020\) maps to \(YYYY\), \(01\) maps to \(MM\), and \(01\) maps to \(DD\). Removing the dashes from the string while keeping the dashes in the pattern fails with "date out of range", because the input shape no longer matches the pattern shape. Dropping the dashes from both string and pattern succeeds. A string with only year and month gains day \(01\) by default.

Example two flips the order. Input '01-01-2020' with pattern 'DD-MM-YYYY' returns \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\). The stored output still follows the server style year-month-day even though the input arrived day-first. Reversing string and pattern so they disagree returns "date time value out of range", which is the signal that input and pattern are misaligned.

Example three reads a long date. Input 'December 1, 2020' with pattern 'Month DD, YYYY' returns \(2020\text{-}12\text{-}01\), where the full capitalized month name maps to \(Month\), \(1\) maps to \(DD\), and \(2020\) maps to \(YYYY\).

Example four reads a short date. Input 'Dec 01 2020' with pattern 'Month DD YYYY' fails with "invalid value December for Mon", because the full name does not fit the short-name slot. Switching the pattern code to \(Mon\) succeeds and returns the matching date.

Example five uses an ordinal tail. Input 'December 08 2020' with pattern 'Month DDTH YYYY' returns \(2020\text{-}12\text{-}08\). The \(TH\) tail accepts the ordinal ending so the eighth maps through \(DDTH\). Intake files arrive in mixed shapes, and matching each shape to its pattern is the daily chore.

Formalize. The mismatch rule is strict equality of shapes:

\[ \text{shape}(s) = \text{shape}(p) \quad \text{or the call fails} \]

Dashes, commas, blanks, and name lengths all count as shape. The \(TH\) suffix widens \(DD\) to accept ordinal tails (1st, 2nd, 8th) without changing the stored day.

Worked example — long December date. Run TO_DATE('December 1, 2020', 'Month DD, YYYY'):

  • "December" matches \(Month\) (full name, first letter capital).
  • "1" matches \(DD\) (day slots accept unpadded digits).
  • "2020" matches \(YYYY\); the comma and blank match literally.

Answer: 2020-12-01. Sense-check: December is month 12, so the output's middle slot reads 12. A companion case: 'Dec 01 2020' needs pattern code \(Mon\), and the full-name code fails loudly rather than guessing.

Scope: out-of-range errors name the failing field; read the message before retyping. Assumption: day \(01\) defaulting applies only when the pattern has no day code at all.

Picture five envelopes arriving in different handwriting — ISO digits, day-first digits, long names, short names, ordinals — and five matching stencils on the desk. Each envelope slides under its stencil; mismatched pairs jam loudly instead of forcing through. The takeaway: keep one stencil per spelling.

Beginners "fix" a mismatch by editing the string to fit a familiar pattern instead of writing the pattern that fits the string — that corrupts intake data. Describe the data as it arrives; never reshape arrivals to dodge pattern writing.

Match each spelling with its own stencil, read mismatch errors as alignment signals, and let missing days default to 01 — and the timestamp converter next adds clock codes to the same stencil habit.

Exam note: TO_DATE and TO_TIMESTAMP pattern traps decide pass or fail, above all \(MM\) for month versus \(MI\) for minute, \(MON\) versus \(MONTH\), \(DD\) versus \(D\), and matching dashes between string and pattern.

15.5 TO_TIMESTAMP Conversion

15.5.1 Syntax and extra codes

TO_DATE reads the day but drops the clock — what reads both? TO_TIMESTAMP, with a second row of pattern codes for hours, minutes, and seconds.

The TO_TIMESTAMP (the text-to-timestamp converter: first argument the full stamp string, second argument the pattern with date plus clock codes) turns a string into a timestamp. The shape is:

TO_TIMESTAMP('2020-01-01 10:30:20', 'YYYY-MM-DD HH24:MI:SS');

The verbal form is "to underscore timestamp takes the timestamp string, then the pattern". It differs from TO_DATE by taking a full timestamp string, not just a date. Date codes carry over, and time codes join them: \(HH24\) is the hour on the 24-hour clock, \(HH\) is the 12-hour form, \(MI\) is the minute, \(SS\) is the second from \(0\) to \(59\), \(MS\) is milliseconds, and \(US\) is microseconds. The clash to memorize: month is \(MM\) but minute is \(MI\), so writing \(MM\) where minutes belong raises a conflict that names the minute field.

Think of the stencil growing a second row: the top row still catches year, month, day, while the new bottom row catches hour, minute, second. The two rows share the letter M, so the stencil stamps them differently — MM upstairs, MI downstairs. The analogy breaks if you memorize letters without rows: MM and MI look alike only outside their rows.

Formalize. The TO_TIMESTAMP call extends the stencil:

\[ \text{TO\_TIMESTAMP}(s, p) = T, \quad T = (D, C) \]

The date half uses the TO_DATE codes; the clock half adds \(HH24 \in [00, 23]\), \(HH \in [01, 12]\), \(MI \in [00, 59]\), \(SS \in [00, 59]\), \(MS\) for milliseconds, \(US\) for microseconds. Omitted clock slots default to zero.

Worked example — slot map. Run the shape above against '2020-01-01 10:30:20':

  • \(2020 \rightarrow YYYY\), \(01 \rightarrow MM\), \(01 \rightarrow DD\).
  • \(10 \rightarrow HH24\), \(30 \rightarrow MI\), \(20 \rightarrow SS\).

Answer: timestamp 2020-01-01 10:30:20. Sense-check: hour 10 fits the 00–23 band and minute 30 fits 00–59, so every slot lands in range.

Scope: TO_TIMESTAMP returns wall timestamp (plus zone handling per session); it never returns a bare DATE. Assumption: \(HH\) without an AM/PM marker follows 12-hour reading and can mislabel afternoons — prefer \(HH24\).

Picture the two-row stencil over '2020-01-01 10:30:20': the top row swallows the date, the blank gutter aligns, the bottom row swallows the clock. The takeaway: one string, two rows, gutter between.

The \(MM\)/\(MI\) clash is the costliest typo in the lecture: writing \(MM\) in the clock row triggers a conflict error naming the minute field. Build the clock row as HH24, MI, SS in that fixed order and the clash never appears.

Snowflake's TO_TIMESTAMP accepts similar codes but auto-detects several common shapes, so explicit patterns matter less there. MySQL splits the job across STR_TO_DATE and UNIX_TIMESTAMP. Databricks follows the pattern-code habit closely.

TO_TIMESTAMP is TO_DATE plus a clock row — and the examples next show how strictly each slot is checked.

15.5.2 Worked examples

Garbage in, garbage out: the function checks ranges but cannot know intent. These examples show where the guardrails catch you and where they stay silent.

Example one maps every slot. The string '2020-01-01 10:30:20' against 'YYYY-MM-DD HH24:MI:SS' maps \(2020\) to \(YYYY\), \(01\) to \(MM\), \(01\) to \(DD\), \(10\) to \(HH24\), \(30\) to \(MI\), and \(20\) to \(SS\). The result is the timestamp \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 10\text{:}30\text{:}20\) with zone info attached. Leaving \(SS\) out of the pattern while the string still carries \(20\) drops the seconds to \(00\), so the output ends \(10\text{:}30\text{:}00\). Narrowing the pattern to the hour only returns the date with \(10\text{:}00\text{:}00\).

Example two shows weak checking. The string carries hour \(30\) against an \(HH24\) slot, and no 30th hour exists. The call fails with "date time field value out of range". Changing \(30\) to \(20\) succeeds. The lesson is phrased as garbage in, garbage out: the function checks ranges but cannot know intent, so the caller must know the input shape.

Example three reads fractions. Input day-month-year plus seconds and milliseconds against a pattern ending in \(SS\text{.}MS\) treats the tail as seconds after the decimal point. Seconds \(10\) with fraction \(4\) render as \(10.4\), which reads as four hundred milliseconds, and the empty slots fill as \(10.400\). Writing \(10\text{:}8\) in the tail renders \(10.8\). Writing \(876\) renders \(10.876\). Writing \(004\) renders \(.004\), which reads as four milliseconds. Tick-by-tick stock feeds and click streams need this millisecond reading, since events land fractions of a second apart.

Formalize. Omitted slots zero out; out-of-range slots fail:

\[ \text{omitted } SS \rightarrow 00, \qquad HH24 = 30 \rightarrow \text{error} \]

Fraction digits fill left to right after the point: \(.4 = .400\), \(.876 = .876\), \(.004 = .004\) seconds.

Worked example — fraction tails. Parse seconds tail \(10.4\) against \(SS\text{.}MS\):

  • Whole part \(10\) fills \(SS\).
  • Fraction \(4\) fills the first \(MS\) slot; empty slots pad right with zeros.

Answer: 10.400 seconds, read as four hundred milliseconds. Sense-check: \(.4 = .40 = .400\) of a second, and \(0.4 \times 1000 = 400\) ms. Companion cases: tail \(876\) gives 10.876, tail \(004\) gives .004 (four milliseconds).

Scope: range checks catch impossible slots (hour 30) but not plausible mistakes (hour 20 typed for 21). Assumption: the caller knows the input shape — the function verifies, never guesses.

Picture a bouncer at the slot door checking IDs: hour 30 is turned away loudly, but hour 20 with a straight face walks in even when you meant 21. The takeaway: the guard checks validity, not intent.

Garbage in, garbage out: a plausible-but-wrong hour passes silently and corrupts downstream math. Validate vendor shapes before converting, especially at intake boundaries.

Slots zero when omitted, fail when impossible, and pass silently when plausible-but-wrong — so know the input shape before converting, and use the reverse conversion function next to print results back out.

Exam note: the \(MM\)/\(MI\) clash and the hour-30 out-of-range error are paired favorites: one tests pattern spelling, the other tests range guards.

15.6 TO_CHAR Formatting

15.6.1 Syntax and direction

TO_DATE reads text into dates — what writes dates back into text for reports and screens? TO_CHAR, running the same stencil in reverse.

The TO_CHAR (the value-to-text formatting function: first argument the value, second argument the wanted look) runs the reverse trip: it turns a timestamp, interval, integer, double precision, or numeric value into text. The shape is:

TO_CHAR(CURRENT_TIMESTAMP, 'Month DD, YYYY');

The verbal form is "to underscore char takes the expression, then the pattern". The left half is the value, the right half is the wanted look. Numeric codes include \(9\) for a digit slot, \(D\) for the decimal point, \(S\) for the sign, and \(TH\) for ordinal endings such as \(1ST\), \(2ND\), and \(8TH\). Calling CURRENT_TIMESTAMP alone here returns something like \(2020\text{-}11\text{-}27\ 17\text{:}45\text{:}26.773665\text{-}05\), spoken as "twenty six point seven seven three six six five seconds with New York offset minus five". That same instant passed through pattern 'Month DD, YYYY' renders as text such as January 01 2020, with the month name and zero-kept day.

Think of a printing press with interchangeable type blocks: the value pours in as raw metal and the pattern blocks stamp it into readable lines. Swap the blocks and the same metal prints a different line. The analogy breaks on types: the press output is text, so it cannot feed date math downstream.

Formalize. The TO_CHAR call is the reverse mapping:

\[ \text{TO\_CHAR}(v, p) = \text{text}, \quad v \in \{\text{timestamp, interval, integer, double, numeric}\} \]

Date codes print names and numbers; numeric codes print \(9\) per digit slot, \(D\) for the point, \(S\) for the sign, \(TH\) for ordinals. The result is text — formatting is one-way.

Worked example — stamp to month text. Run TO_CHAR(CURRENT_TIMESTAMP, 'Month DD, YYYY') when now reads 2020-11-27 17:45:26.773665-05:

  • \(Month\) prints the full month name.
  • \(DD\) prints the zero-kept day; \(YYYY\) prints the year.

Answer: text like November 27, 2020. Sense-check: the output is text, so wrapping it in date math would fail — formatting is the last step, never an middle one.

Scope: TO_CHAR output is text; downstream sorting becomes alphabetical, not chronological. Assumption: the pattern letters match the value kind — date codes for stamps, numeric codes for numbers.

Picture the press: the timestamp ingot drops in the left hopper, the Month-DD-YYYY blocks clamp down, and a readable line slides out the right. The takeaway: values in, text out, one way only.

Beginners format first and compute later — sorting formatted month names alphabetically scrambles chronology (April before January). Compute on stamps, format only for display.

MySQL's DATE_FORMAT uses %-codes (%M %D, %Y) for the same job; Snowflake and Databricks keep TO_CHAR/TO_VARCHAR with near-identical letters. Same press, different block labels.

TO_CHAR prints values to text through a pattern — and zones plus fill mode refine the print.

15.6.2 Worked examples with zones and fill mode

Raw TO_CHAR output pads with blanks and zeros — fine for machines, ugly for humans. How do you print "January 1st, 2020" instead of "January 01, 2020"? The FM prefix.

Example one adds an explicit zone. Casting the string '2020-01-01 10:45:55-06:00' to timestamp with zone and formatting with year, month, day, hour, minute, second and zone codes returns the text plus a zone tag such as EST, shifted to local time, here showing \(11\text{:}01\text{:}55\) style output after conversion with daylight handling. The same clock time reads differently by place: late afternoon in New York is mid-afternoon in Chicago, and the conversion absorbs that gap.

Example two strips padding. The fill mode prefix \(FM\) drops padding blanks and leading zeros. Pattern 'FMMonth DDTH, YYYY' on the same value renders January 1st, 2020 with no leading space or zero, and the \(TH\) tail picks the right ending: 1st, 2nd with \(ND\), 8th with \(TH\). The text form suits reports and display output.

Example three formats a real table. Querying movie name plus release date shows plain \(YYYY\text{-}MM\text{-}DD\) values. Adding TO_CHAR(release_date, 'FMMonth DDTH, YYYY') adds a text column reading February 2nd, 1972 and 4th-style rows for other titles. Appending \(SS\) and zone codes appends the seconds plus tags such as EST or EDT. TO_CHAR is the display function for reports, exports, and on-screen dates.

Formalize. The fill-mode rule prefixes any code it cleans:

\[ FM + \text{code} \rightarrow \text{unpadded text}, \quad TH \rightarrow \{st, nd, rd, th\} \]

So \(Month\) prints "January " (padded) while \(FMMonth\) prints "January" (bare); \(DD\) prints "01" while \(FMDD\) prints "1"; \(DDTH\) picks 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th by value.

Worked example — clean report date. Format 2020-01-01 with 'FMMonth DDTH, YYYY':

  • \(FMMonth\) prints January with no trailing blanks.
  • \(DDTH\) prints 1st (day 1 takes "st"; day 2 takes "nd"; day 8 takes "th").

Answer: January 1st, 2020. Sense-check: the same value without FM prints padded blanks and "01" — compare both to prove FM did the cleaning. Table case: TO_CHAR(release_date, 'FMMonth DDTH, YYYY') turns 1972-02-02 into February 2nd, 1972.

Scope: FM affects only the code it prefixes; place it before each code needing cleanup. Assumption: zone tags print the session-zone abbreviation after conversion.

Picture two printed invitations side by side: one reads "January 01, 2020" with visible gaps, the other "January 1st, 2020" crisp and tight. The takeaway: FM is the typesetter's cleanup pass.

The trap is prefixing FM once and expecting it to clean the whole pattern — it cleans only its own code. Repeat FM before every padded code, or stray blanks survive into exports.

Zones convert, FM cleans, TH picks endings — TO_CHAR is the display endpoint, and the builders next construct intervals and stamps from parts.

Exam note: the FM prefix plus TH endings is a hands-on favorite: know that FM drops padding and TH selects st/nd/rd/th by value.

15.7 MAKE_INTERVAL Construction

15.7.1 Syntax and slot order

Date math needs durations — "3 months", "10 days" — as real values, not text. How do you build one from parts? MAKE_INTERVAL, with seven slots in a fixed order.

The MAKE_INTERVAL (the duration builder: seven positional slots that combine into one interval value) builds an interval from parts. The positional shape is:

MAKE_INTERVAL(2020, 01, 01, 01, 10, 30, 45);

The verbal form is "make underscore interval takes years, months, weeks, days, hours, minutes, seconds in that order". So the slots are \(years\), \(months\), \(weeks\), \(days\), \(hours\), \(minutes\), \(seconds\). The sample call passes years \(2020\), months \(01\), weeks \(01\), days \(01\), hours \(10\), minutes \(30\), seconds \(45\), and returns an interval reading 2020 years, 1 month, 8 days, \(10\text{:}30\text{:}45\). The 8 days arise because one week plus the one day combine.

Think of a seven-compartment spice box with fixed labels: years in slot one, months in slot two, all the way to seconds in slot seven. Scoop quantities into each compartment and the box blends one duration. The analogy breaks on weeks: no kitchen box merges two compartments, but this one folds weeks into days.

Formalize. The positional builder maps slots to an interval:

\[ \text{MAKE\_INTERVAL}(y, mo, w, d, h, mi, s) = y\ \text{years} + mo\ \text{months} + (7w + d)\ \text{days} + h\text{:}mi\text{:}s \]

Weeks fold into days through \(7w + d\), so weeks \(1\) plus days \(1\) gives \(7 + 1 = 8\) days. The sample yields 2020 years, 1 month, 8 days, \(10\text{:}30\text{:}45\).

Worked example — positional slots build the interval. Run MAKE_INTERVAL(2020, 01, 01, 01, 10, 30, 45):

  • Years \(2020\), months \(01\) pass through directly.
  • Weeks \(01\) plus days \(01\): \(7 \times 1 + 1 = 8\) days.
  • Clock \(10\text{:}30\text{:}45\) passes through directly.

Answer: 2020 years 1 month 8 days 10:30:45. Sense-check: the day count 8 exceeds either input (1 week, 1 day) alone, proving the fold happened.

Scope: positional order is fixed — years, months, weeks, days, hours, minutes, seconds. Assumption: slot values may exceed natural bounds (months 13 is accepted and normalized downstream).

Picture the spice box with seven labeled wells: pour 2020 into years, 01 into months, 01 into weeks, 01 into days, then watch the weeks well drain into the days well, leaving 8. The takeaway: seven slots in, one blended duration out.

The trap is misordering slots — swapping weeks and days silently builds a different duration with no error. Count the seven positions aloud when writing positional calls, or switch to the named form.

Seven slots, fixed order, weeks fold into days — and the week-folding ladder next makes that merge concrete.

15.7.2 Week-number effect and named form

Does adding one week always add exactly seven days? Almost — and the exception teaches how calendar math really works.

Holding every slot fixed except weeks shows the step. With weeks \(1\) the day count reads 8 days. With weeks \(2\) it reads 15 days. With weeks \(3\) it reads 22 days. With weeks \(4\) it reads 29 days. With weeks \(5\) it reads 36 days. Each extra week adds about seven days, though calendar and zone behavior can shift exact totals. A bare weeks-only call is cleaner: weeks \(2\) alone is \(14\) days, weeks \(3\) is \(21\) days, weeks \(4\) is \(28\) days, the plain \(7 \times n\) ladder, while the mixed call above reads 15 for its second week because month length and calendar math join in.

The named form passes slots by name and reads better: MAKE_INTERVAL(days => 10) returns 10 days, and MAKE_INTERVAL(months => 10, days => 2, mins => 35) returns 10 months, 2 days, \(00\text{:}35\text{:}00\). The positional form works, but the named form states intent per slot.

Formalize. The week ladder in the mixed call is:

\[ \text{days shown} = 7w + 1, \quad w = 1 \ldots 5 \rightarrow 8, 15, 22, 29, 36 \]

The bare-weeks ladder is the clean \(7 \times n\): weeks \(2 \rightarrow 14\), \(3 \rightarrow 21\), \(4 \rightarrow 28\). The mixed call's second week reads 15 rather than a plain 14 because month length and calendar normalization join the arithmetic.

Worked example — week ladder. Freeze all slots except weeks:

  • Weeks \(1\): \(7 \times 1 + 1 =\) 8 days.
  • Weeks \(2\): \(7 \times 2 + 1 =\) 15 days.
  • Weeks \(5\): \(7 \times 5 + 1 =\) 36 days.

Answer: each extra week adds seven days along 8, 15, 22, 29, 36. Sense-check: successive gaps are exactly 7 (15 − 8 = 7), confirming the step. Named-form case: MAKE_INTERVAL(days => 10) returns 10 days with zero ambiguity.

Scope: bare-weeks calls follow exact multiples; mixed calls normalize through the calendar. Assumption: named arguments use => and accept any subset of slots.

Picture a staircase where each tread is seven days wide but the whole flight sits on a calendar hillside — bare treads measure exactly 7, while the hillside tilts mixed totals by month length. The takeaway: weeks are exact alone, approximate inside mixed durations.

Beginners read "15 days" as "two weeks plus one" and hard-code \(2 \times 7 + 1\) downstream — calendar normalization can shift such totals. Treat mixed intervals as durations for math, not as exact day counts.

Snowflake builds intervals with INTERVAL literals and DATEADD instead of MAKE_INTERVAL; Databricks leans on INTERVAL strings plus make_interval with named arguments. The named-argument habit transfers everywhere.

Weeks climb a seven-day ladder, mixed calls normalize through the calendar, and the named form states intent — and the stamp builders next apply the same parts-to-value idea to timestamps.

Exam note: the slot order years-months-weeks-days-hours-minutes-seconds plus the \(7w + d\) fold is a recall favorite.

15.8 MAKE_TIMESTAMPTZ and MAKE_TIMESTAMP

15.8.1 Building a zoned timestamp

What if the parts arrive as separate numbers — year here, month there — with no string to parse? The MAKE builders assemble stamps straight from numbers.

The MAKE_TIMESTAMPTZ (the zoned stamp builder: year through second plus an optional zone, defaulting to the server zone) builds a timestamp with zone from parts. The shape is:

MAKE_TIMESTAMPTZ(2020, 02, 15, 10, 35, 15.35);

The verbal form is "make timestamp with zone takes year, month, day, hour, minute, second, plus an optional zone". The sample passes year \(2020\), month \(02\), day \(15\), hour \(10\), minute \(35\), second \(15.35\), and the result carries the server zone, here minus five for New York. Wrapping the call in PG_TYPEOF(...) confirms the type timestamp with time zone. With no zone argument the current server zone applies. The zone catalog lives in PG_TIMEZONE_NAMES with columns for name, shortened tag, offset from UTC, and a daylight-saving flag, plus PG_TIMEZONE_ABBREVS with the same shape keyed by tag. Passing an explicit zone such as US/Alaska at minus nine stamps that zone instead.

Think of an assembly line with six numbered bins — year, month, day, hour, minute, second — plus an optional city sticker at the end. Parts drop into bins, the line bolts them into a stamp, and the sticker (or the factory's default city) fixes the zone. The analogy breaks for bad parts: a real line jams, while this one rejects out-of-range numbers with an error.

Formalize. The zoned builder is:

\[ \text{MAKE\_TIMESTAMPTZ}(Y, M, D, h, mi, s[, Z]) = (T, Z), \quad Z = \text{given zone or server zone} \]

Six numeric parts form the wall stamp \(T\); the zone \(Z\) pins it to an absolute instant. PG_TYPEOF(...) proves the result kind is timestamp with time zone.

Worked example — zoned stamp from parts. Run MAKE_TIMESTAMPTZ(2020, 02, 15, 10, 35, 15.35) on a New York server:

  • Parts bolt into wall stamp 2020-02-15 10:35:15.35.
  • No zone argument, so the server zone -05 attaches.

Answer: 2020-02-15 10:35:15.35-05. Sense-check: PG_TYPEOF(...) returns timestamp with time zone, confirming the zone stuck. Override case: passing 'US/Alaska' stamps -09 instead.

Scope: numeric parts only — text month names do not fit these slots. Assumption: out-of-range parts (month 13, hour 30) fail rather than wrap.

Picture the six bins feeding a press, with a rack of city stickers (PG_TIMEZONE_NAMES: name, tag, UTC offset, saving flag) beside the line. No sticker picked means the factory-city sticker applies. The takeaway: numbers in, zoned stamp out.

Beginners pass month names or weekday strings into numeric slots — the builder takes numbers only. A second trap is assuming the server zone travels with exported rows; it does not, only the stored offset does.

Snowflake's TIMESTAMP_TZ_FROM_PARTS and Databricks' make_timestamp with a zone argument play the same role with shifted argument lists. MySQL has no direct twin; it concatenates parts into a string first.

Six numbers plus an optional zone build a pinned instant — and the same parts read differently under different stickers.

15.8.2 Zone tags shift the same parts

Same numbers, different city — same instant or different? Different readings, one instant, and the arithmetic is just offsets.

One parts row viewed under several tags shows the shift. The same \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\) parts render at minus five under EST, and under a far-ahead tag the same instant falls back to \(2019\text{-}12\text{-}31\), because the offset crosses midnight. Tags with daylight saving active adjust again. The night-here-versus-day-there gap is exactly this arithmetic.

Formalize. One instant, many readings:

\[ \text{instant} = T - Z_{offset}, \quad \text{reading}_{new} = \text{instant} + Z_{new} \]

Subtract the old offset to reach UTC, add the new offset to render locally. Crossing midnight moves the calendar day even though the instant never moved.

Worked example — midnight crossing. Take wall parts 2020-01-01 00:30 under EST (-05):

  • UTC instant: \(00\text{:}30 + 05\text{:}00 =\) 05:30 UTC on 2020-01-01.
  • Render at +14: \(05\text{:}30 + 14\text{:}00 =\) 19:30 on 2020-01-01; render westward symmetrically and early hours fall back to 2019-12-31.

Answer: the same instant lands on different calendar days by zone. Sense-check: the UTC instant is identical in every rendering — only the wall reading moves.

Scope: offset arithmetic is exact; daylight rules add calendar-dependent shifts on top. Assumption: tag offsets come from the zone catalog, not hand-typed numbers.

Picture one radio broadcast heard on kitchen radios in New York and Fiji: the signal is single, but each kitchen clock shows its own wall time, and the Fijian date can read a day ahead. The takeaway: store the signal, render per kitchen.

Hand-typing numeric offsets instead of named zones freezes today's offset and breaks when daylight rules change — the standing advice is named zones always.

One instant, many wall readings, midnight crossings included — and the zone-free twin next stores the wall reading alone.

15.8.3 The zone-free twin

What if you need the wall reading without pinning any instant — a shop's "opens at 10" regardless of city? The plain builder stores exactly that.

The MAKE_TIMESTAMP (the zone-free stamp builder: same six numbers, no zone slot, wall time only) takes the same year-through-second parts but stores no zone. The sample MAKE_TIMESTAMP(2020, 02, 15, 10, 35, 15.35) keeps \(10\) as hour, \(35\) as minute, \(15.35\) as second, with no minus-five tail, and PG_TYPEOF(...) confirms timestamp without zone. Handing it a zone tag fails, since the function has no zone slot; the zoned builder is the tool for that case. The closing rule: zoned builder defaults to the server zone and accepts an override tag, while the plain builder never stores a zone.

Formalize. The twin builders differ by one slot:

\[ \text{MAKE\_TIMESTAMP}(Y, M, D, h, mi, s) = T\ (\text{wall time}), \quad \text{MAKE\_TIMESTAMPTZ}(\ldots[, Z]) = (T, Z) \]

Same six numbers; the zoned twin adds \(Z\), the plain twin has nowhere to put one.

Worked example — twin outputs. Run both builders on (2020, 02, 15, 10, 35, 15.35):

  • MAKE_TIMESTAMP(...) returns 2020-02-15 10:35:15.35 with no tail.
  • MAKE_TIMESTAMPTZ(...) returns 2020-02-15 10:35:15.35-05 on the New York server.

Answer: identical wall readings, but only the twin carries -05. Sense-check: PG_TYPEOF on each confirms timestamp without versus with time zone — the types differ even when the digits match.

Scope: wall stamps suit local recurring times (shop hours); absolute event ordering needs zoned stamps. Assumption: a seventh zone argument to the plain builder is a hard error, not a silent ignore.

Picture two photo prints from one negative: one labeled with city and offset on the back, one bare. Same picture, different archival value. The takeaway: pick the twin that matches the question — wall habit or absolute instant.

The trap is storing worldwide events as wall stamps and comparing them directly — 10:35 in New York sorts beside 10:35 in Singapore as equals when they are hours apart. Store zoned, compare at UTC level.

Zoned builder pins instants, plain builder keeps wall readings — and the extractors next slice either kind back into parts.

15.9 EXTRACT Value Retrieval

15.9.1 Syntax of the two twins

Builders pack parts into stamps — what unpacks one part back out? Two twin functions with mirrored syntax, and exams love comparing them.

Two functions pull single parts out of dates. EXTRACT (the FROM-style part puller: field first, then FROM, then the source) writes the part first with FROM:

EXTRACT(DAY FROM CURRENT_TIMESTAMP);

DATE_PART (the comma-style part puller: field and source as a quoted pair) writes part and source as a pair:

DATE_PART('day', CURRENT_TIMESTAMP);

The verbal form is "extract names the field, then FROM names the source; date part names both separated by a comma". The field list covers century, decade, year, quarter, month, week, day, day of week, day of year, hour, minute, second, millisecond, microsecond, epoch, zone, zone hour, and zone minute. Ranges to memorize: day of month \(1\) to \(31\); hours \(0\) to \(23\); minutes and seconds \(0\) to \(59\); months \(1\) to \(12\); quarters \(1\) to \(4\). The epoch (the count of seconds since \(1970\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 00\text{:}00\text{:}00\) UTC, the exact-math base for gaps) is the count of seconds since \(1970\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 00\text{:}00\text{:}00\) UTC, written \(E = t - t_0\) where \(t\) is the instant and \(t_0\) is that base, and it is the exact-math path used later.

Think of a stamp as a labeled toolbox and each function as a different grip reaching for the same wrench: EXTRACT grips "DAY FROM box", DATE_PART grips "('day', box)". Same wrench, mirrored handles. The analogy breaks on spelling: one handle is a bare keyword, the other a quoted string — grips are not interchangeable.

Formalize. Both twins compute the same projection:

\[ \text{EXTRACT}(F\ \text{FROM}\ s) = \text{DATE\_PART}('f', s) = \pi_F(s) \]

Here \(F\) is the field, \(s\) the source, and \(\pi_F\) the projection onto that part. The epoch field is the special projection \(E = t - t_0\) with \(t_0 = 1970\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 00\text{:}00\text{:}00\) UTC.

Worked example — mirrored pulls. Against CURRENT_TIMESTAMP reading 2020-11-27 18:57:16:

  • EXTRACT(DAY FROM CURRENT_TIMESTAMP) returns 27.
  • DATE_PART('day', CURRENT_TIMESTAMP) returns 27.

Answer: identical results through mirrored syntax. Sense-check: month pulls give 11 and year pulls give 2020 under both spellings, proving the twins agree.

Scope: field names are fixed vocabulary — DAY works, DAYS fails. Assumption: EXTRACT takes a bare keyword field; DATE_PART takes a quoted string field.

Picture the toolbox with labeled drawers (century down to microsecond, plus epoch and zone drawers at the bottom). Either grip opens the same drawer. The takeaway: learn one drawer map, use either grip.

Two traps: quoting the EXTRACT field (EXTRACT('day' ...)) and unquoting the DATE_PART field (DATE_PART(day, ...)) — each twin rejects the other's spelling. A second trap is pulling day when you need day-of-week or day-of-year; three different drawers share the word "day".

MySQL offers EXTRACT plus YEAR()/MONTH()/DAY() shortcuts; Snowflake adds DATE_PART synonyms and DATETRUNC; Databricks uses year()/month()/day() functions heavily. The drawer map transfers; the grip spelling varies.

Two grips, one drawer map — EXTRACT names FROM, DATE_PART pairs with a comma — and the examples next put both grips to work.

15.9.2 Worked examples

Can extractors slice anything beyond plain stamps — intervals, quarters, centuries? Yes, and one division shows the full range.

With the server date at November 27th 2020, EXTRACT(DAY FROM CURRENT_TIMESTAMP) returns \(27\), the month form returns \(11\), and the year form returns \(2020\). Each call slices one part from the same instant. EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM ...) returns the large seconds count measured from the 1970 base. Mixing types also works: EXTRACT(CENTURY FROM INTERVAL '500 years 2 months 11 days') returns \(5\), since \(\frac{500}{100} = 5\). Subqueries and recursive queries slice year, month, or day parts for grouping and filtering instead of dragging whole timestamps along.

Formalize. Part pulls project; epoch subtracts; century divides:

\[ \pi_{day} = 27, \quad \pi_{month} = 11, \quad \pi_{year} = 2020, \quad \text{century} = \frac{500}{100} = 5 \]

Each projection reads one drawer. The century pull truncates toward the century index, so 500 years lands in century 5.

Worked example — day, month, year, century. From stamp 2020-11-27 and interval '500 years 2 months 11 days':

  • Day pull: 27. Month pull: 11. Year pull: 2020.
  • Century pull: \(500 \div 100 =\) 5.

Answer: 27, 11, 2020, and century 5. Sense-check: reassembling 2020-11-27 from the three pulls reproduces the source date, proving nothing was lost.

Scope: century and decade pulls suit archives; daily analytics leans on year, quarter, month, day. Assumption: epoch pulls return double precision seconds, not integers.

Picture three cookie cutters (day, month, year) punching the same dough stamp, plus a century press stamping "5" on a 500-year block. The takeaway: slice parts for grouping, pull epoch for exact math.

Beginners drag whole timestamps into GROUP BY when a year or month pull would bucket cleanly — grouping by full stamps creates one group per second instead of per month.

Slice one part per call, reassemble to verify, reach for epoch when gaps need exact seconds — and date math next adds and subtracts whole values.

Exam note: EXTRACT against DATE_PART syntax order is a classic compare item: FROM-style bare keyword versus comma-style quoted string.

15.10 Math Operators with Dates

15.10.1 Date plus days

What is ten days after New Year's Day — and does the database need a function for it, or just a plus sign? Just a plus sign, with one casting rule.

A date plus a plain number adds days:

DATE '2020-01-01' + 10;

The verbal form is "date literal plus ten means ten days later". The result is \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}11\), since \(1 + 10 = 11\). Adding \(20\) or \(30\) moves to the 21st or to \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}31\). An explicit cast does the same from a string: '2020-01-01'::DATE + 10 also lands on the 11th. Without the cast the string stays a string and the plus does not date-math, so the type tag must be stated.

Think of the calendar as a staircase with one step per day: adding \(n\) climbs \(n\) steps. The analogy breaks for bare strings: a painted staircase on the wall (an uncast string) cannot be climbed — only a real DATE staircase takes steps.

Formalize. Date-plus-number is day stepping:

\[ \text{DATE}\ 'Y\text{-}M\text{-}D' + n = D + n\ \text{days}, \quad 1 + 10 = 11 \]

The DATE keyword (or ::DATE cast) builds the staircase; the bare string without it stays text and the plus misfires.

Worked example — date literal plus ten. Run DATE '2020-01-01' + 10:

  • Day slot: \(1 + 10 =\) 11.
  • Month and year unchanged.

Answer: 2020-01-11. Sense-check: adding 30 instead lands on 2020-01-31 (since \(1 + 30 = 31\)), still inside January, confirming plain day stepping. Cast form '2020-01-01'::DATE + 10 agrees.

Scope: a bare number adds days only to DATE; adding numbers to timestamps needs intervals. Assumption: the left side is typed DATE — cast first, add second.

Picture the staircase starting at the 1st: ten climbs land on the 11th tread, twenty on the 21st, thirty on the 31st. The takeaway: plus means climb, but only on a typed staircase.

A string stays a string without a cast: '2020-01-01' + 10 never does date math. Tag the type with DATE or ::DATE before adding — this is the section's core warning.

Type the date, then add days — and clock readings need intervals instead of bare numbers.

15.10.2 Time plus interval

What is one second after 23:59:59 — an error, or midnight? Midnight, with a wrap the database handles for you.

A time plus an interval needs matching kinds on both sides of the plus:

TIME '23:59:59' + INTERVAL '1 second';

The verbal form is "time plus a one-second interval". The result wraps past midnight to \(00\text{:}00\text{:}01\). Adding \(10\) seconds or \(2\) seconds moves the tail the same way. A cast form such as the string cast to time behaves the same.

Formalize. Time-plus-interval wraps modulo one day:

\[ 23\text{:}59\text{:}59 + 1\ \text{second} = 00\text{:}00\text{:}00 + 1\ \text{second} = 00\text{:}00\text{:}01 \]

Crossing midnight rolls the clock without touching any date (a bare time has none).

Worked example — wrap past midnight. Run TIME '23:59:59' + INTERVAL '1 second':

  • Seconds: \(59 + 1 = 60 \rightarrow\) carry one minute, seconds reset to 00.
  • Minutes: \(59 + 1 = 60 \rightarrow\) carry one hour, minutes reset to 00.
  • Hours roll \(23 \rightarrow\) 00, landing just past midnight.

Answer per lecture: 00:00:01, just past midnight. Note on forms: strict clock arithmetic gives midnight sharp 00:00:00 for 23:59:59 plus one second; the lecture records the wrap landing one second past, which is the same wrap behavior either way. Sense-check: any post-midnight result confirms the wrap fired instead of erroring.

Scope: both sides must be time-kind and interval-kind; mismatched kinds fail. Assumption: wrap-around is by design, not overflow.

Picture the clock face with the second hand one tick from the top: one more tick sweeps past 12 and the face resets. The takeaway: clocks wrap, they never overflow.

Beginners expect an out-of-range error at midnight; instead the value wraps silently. When wrap is wrong (a shift must not cross midnight), check bounds explicitly after adding.

Time plus interval wraps past midnight by design — and full stamps combine every hand at once.

15.10.3 Timestamp, mixed, and interval math

Dates step by days, times wrap by day — what moves a full timestamp, and can dates and times mix? Four rules cover the whole zoo.

Adding a duration to the current timestamp moves every hand: CURRENT_TIMESTAMP + '01:01:01' turns \(18\text{:}39\text{:}28.50631\) into \(19\text{:}40\text{:}29.50631\), because \(18 + 1 = 19\), \(39 + 1 = 40\), and \(28 + 1 = 29\), with zone kept. Adding a time to a date builds a timestamp: DATE '2020-01-01' + TIME '10:25:10' gives \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 10\text{:}25\text{:}10\). Adding bare hour-minute-second parts to a plain date without the timestamp kind is ignored, so the timestamp kind must wrap the expression. A duration before a date moves back: DATE '2020-01-01' - INTERVAL '1 hour' gives \(2019\text{-}12\text{-}31\ 23\text{:}00\), since one hour before midnight steps into the prior day. Interval pairs combine directly: \(30\) minutes \(+\) \(30\) minutes \(= 01\text{:}00\text{:}00\); \(30\) minutes \(-\) \(15\) minutes \(= 00\text{:}15\text{:}00\), a quarter hour; \(30\) minutes \(+\) \(1\) day \(= 1\ \text{day}\ 00\text{:}30\text{:}00\); and \(\frac{02\text{:}00\text{:}00}{2} = 01\text{:}00\text{:}00\).

Formalize. The four mixed-math rules are:

\[ \begin{aligned} T + \text{duration} &\rightarrow T\ \text{shifted, zone kept} \\ \text{DATE} + \text{TIME} &\rightarrow \text{TIMESTAMP} \\ \text{DATE} - \text{INTERVAL} &\rightarrow \text{earlier stamp (may cross midnight back)} \\ I_1 \pm I_2,\ I / n &\rightarrow \text{interval arithmetic} \end{aligned} \]

Concretely: \(30\ \text{min} + 30\ \text{min} = 01\text{:}00\text{:}00\); \(30\ \text{min} - 15\ \text{min} = 00\text{:}15\text{:}00\); \(30\ \text{min} + 1\ \text{day} = 1\ \text{day}\ 00\text{:}30\text{:}00\); \(02\text{:}00\text{:}00 / 2 = 01\text{:}00\text{:}00\).

Worked example — every hand moves. Add '01:01:01' to stamp 18:39:28.50631:

  • Hours: \(18 + 1 =\) 19. Minutes: \(39 + 1 =\) 40. Seconds: \(28 + 1 =\) 29; fraction .50631 unchanged.

Answer: 19:40:29.50631, zone kept. Sense-check: each hand moved by exactly one with no carry, the cleanest possible confirmation. Backward case: DATE '2020-01-01' - INTERVAL '1 hour' = 2019-12-31 23:00, proving subtraction crosses midnight into the prior day.

Scope: bare clock parts added to a plain DATE without timestamp typing are ignored — wrap in timestamp kind first. Assumption: interval division and multiplication scale every field proportionally.

Picture a control panel with four levers: shift-stamp, fuse date+time, step backward, combine intervals. Each lever moves its gauge predictably, including across midnight in both directions. The takeaway: pick the lever matching the kinds on both sides.

Two traps: adding clock parts to a DATE without timestamp wrapping (silently ignored), and subtracting across month ends without checking the landing day. Match kinds first, verify landings second.

MySQL does date math with DATE_ADD/ADDDATE and interval keywords; Snowflake and Databricks use DATEADD/DATE_ADD functions. Operators differ, but the kind-matching rule (date+interval, time+interval, date+time) holds on every engine.

Shift stamps with durations, fuse dates and times into stamps, step backward across midnights, combine intervals directly — and overlap checks next compare whole ranges.

Exam note: out-of-range favorites include hour 30, November 31st, and mismatched pattern order; the kind-matching rule (string stays string without a cast) is tested alongside.

15.11 OVERLAPS Check

15.11.1 Syntax and range test

Two bookings cover date ranges — do they collide? OVERLAPS answers true or false in one operator.

The OVERLAPS (the range-collision test: two start-end pairs returning TRUE when they share any point) asks whether two date ranges share any point. The shape is:

(DATE '2020-01-01', DATE '2020-12-31') OVERLAPS (DATE '2020-10-12', DATE '2020-12-01');

The verbal form is "left range overlaps right range". The left half sets the window, here the whole year from January 1st to December 31st. The right half sets the probe, here October 12th to December 1st. The result is \(TRUE\) because both probe dates sit inside the year. Probes fully outside return \(FALSE\). A probe such as November 31st fails with "date time field value out of range", because November holds only 30 days; using November 28th runs fine.

Think of two highlighter strokes on one timeline: where both colors cover the same days, the ink turns dark — that dark stretch means TRUE. Strokes that never touch stay light: FALSE. The analogy breaks at endpoints: real highlighters bleed, but OVERLAPS uses exact endpoint rules.

Formalize. The overlap test on ranges \((a, b)\) and \((c, d)\) is:

\[ (a, b)\ \text{OVERLAPS}\ (c, d) \iff \max(a, c) \le \min(b, d) \]

The shared stretch runs from the later start to the earlier end; when the later start passes the earlier end, nothing is shared and the result is \(FALSE\).

Worked example — year window probe. Test window (2020-01-01, 2020-12-31) against probe (2020-10-12, 2020-12-01):

  • Later start: \(\max(01\text{-}01, 10\text{-}12) =\) 10-12.
  • Earlier end: \(\min(12\text{-}31, 12\text{-}01) =\) 12-01.
  • Since 10-12 \(\le\) 12-01, the shared stretch is Oct 12 – Dec 1.

Answer: TRUE. Sense-check: both probe dates sit inside the year window, so a shared stretch must exist. Range-guard case: November 31st fails (November holds 30 days); November 28th runs fine.

Scope: OVERLAPS compares start-end pairs of date, time, or timestamp kinds — both pairs must be comparable kinds. Assumption: endpoints are valid calendar dates; impossible days fail before any comparison.

Picture the year as a long bar highlit yellow January to December, with October 12 to December 1 overpainted blue — the doubly-painted stretch glows green for TRUE. A probe bar floating fully outside leaves no green. The takeaway: green stretch means collision.

Beginners test single dates with OVERLAPS instead of BETWEEN — ranges need two endpoints per side. A second trap is forgetting November has 30 days; impossible probe dates error out instead of returning FALSE.

Two ranges, later start versus earlier end, shared stretch means TRUE — and spans can replace endpoints.

15.11.2 Interval form

What if the window is "starting January 1st, lasting 100 days" instead of two fixed dates? The interval form handles exactly that.

The left half may state a start plus a span:

(DATE '2020-01-01', INTERVAL '100 days') OVERLAPS (DATE '2020-10-12', DATE '2020-12-01');

The verbal form is "starting at January 1st, span one hundred days, then test the probe". One hundred days runs about three months, so an October probe misses and returns \(FALSE\), while widening the span to \(500\) days covers the probe and returns \(TRUE\). Overlap tests catch duplicate coverage between two periods, such as double-booked ranges or repeated day pairs.

Formalize. The interval form converts start-plus-span to endpoints first:

\[ (a, a + I)\ \text{OVERLAPS}\ (c, d), \quad 2020\text{-}01\text{-}01 + 100\ \text{days} \approx \text{early April} \]

One hundred days from January 1st lands in early April, months before the October probe — so \(FALSE\). Five hundred days reaches into the following year, covering the probe — so \(TRUE\).

Worked example — span probes. Test start 2020-01-01 with two spans against probe (2020-10-12, 2020-12-01):

  • Span 100 days: window ends early April 2020; later start Oct 12 > earlier end April — FALSE.
  • Span 500 days: window ends mid-2021, covering October–December 2020 — TRUE.

Answer: 100 days misses, 500 days covers. Sense-check: 100 days is about three months (January through March), which cannot reach October from January — the FALSE is expected.

Scope: interval spans add calendar days from the start; month-length quirks apply as in 15.7. Assumption: the probe side still needs two endpoints (or its own start-plus-span).

Picture the yellow bar now drawn from a start pin with a 100-day measuring tape — too short to touch the blue October stretch — then remeasured with a 500-day tape that sails past it. The takeaway: span length decides reach.

The trap is eyeballing "100 days ≈ 3 months" and guessing coverage; count to April and the miss becomes plain. Booking systems automate this check precisely to dodge such eyeball errors.

Databricks and Snowflake lack a direct OVERLAPS keyword; the equivalent is an explicit start1 <= end2 AND start2 <= end1 predicate. MySQL likewise uses comparison predicates. Same later-start/earlier-end logic, spelled longhand.

Endpoints or start-plus-span, the rule never changes: shared stretch means TRUE — and the native clock readings next stamp the values such ranges are built from.

Exam note: OVERLAPS true/false probes with 100-day versus 500-day spans are applied favorites; November-31st range failure rides along.

15.12 Native Date-Time Functions and Precision

15.12.1 The five readings

No parsing, no patterns — what gives the current day and clock straight from the server? Five native readings, each with a fixed shape.

Five native readings come from the server. CURRENT_DATE gives the day as \(YYYY\text{-}MM\text{-}DD\), here \(2020\text{-}11\text{-}27\). CURRENT_TIME gives clock plus zone, here \(18\) hours, \(57\) minutes, \(16.681082\) seconds at offset \(-05\text{:}00\) for New York. CURRENT_TIMESTAMP adds the day to the same clock, so it reads date plus time plus zone. LOCALTIME gives the server local clock, and LOCALTIMESTAMP gives the local day plus clock. A long run can separate the transaction clock from the local clock, which is why both exist.

Think of five wall displays in a control room: a date board, a zoned clock, a combined stamp display, and two local-only screens without zone tags. Same room, five framings. The analogy breaks under long transactions: the stamp display can freeze while the local screens keep ticking.

Formalize. The five readings partition the instant and the zone:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{CURRENT\_DATE} &= D \\ \text{CURRENT\_TIME} &= (C, Z) \\ \text{CURRENT\_TIMESTAMP} &= (D, C, Z) \\ \text{LOCALTIME} &= C \\ \text{LOCALTIMESTAMP} &= (D, C) \end{aligned} \]

The CURRENT trio carries the transaction clock with zone info; the LOCAL pair reads the session-local clock with no zone tag.

Worked example — read all five. On 2020-11-27 at 18:57:16.681082-05:

  • CURRENT_DATE returns 2020-11-27.
  • CURRENT_TIME returns 18:57:16.681082-05:00.
  • CURRENT_TIMESTAMP returns 2020-11-27 18:57:16.681082-05.
  • LOCALTIME / LOCALTIMESTAMP return the same digits without the zone tail.

Answer: day, zoned clock, zoned stamp, local clock, local stamp. Sense-check: stripping the zone tail from the CURRENT readings reproduces the LOCAL readings, proving they share one instant.

Scope: standard SQL names — portable across PostgreSQL, MySQL, Snowflake, and Databricks. Assumption: CURRENT readings freeze at transaction start; LOCAL readings track the session clock.

Picture the control room: three framed displays tagged with -05, two bare local screens beside them. During a long batch the framed trio holds still while the bare pair advances. The takeaway: CURRENT freezes with the transaction, LOCAL follows the wall.

Beginners log with LOCALTIMESTAMP and lose the zone, making cross-city ordering impossible later. Default to the zoned CURRENT_TIMESTAMP for stored events; use LOCAL forms for wall-display only.

Five readings, one instant, zoned versus local framing — and precision controls how many digits each clock shows.

15.12.2 Precision control

Microsecond tails clutter screens but decide race orders — how do you keep exactly as many digits as you need? A single number in parentheses.

Precision \(p\) counts kept digits after the decimal point, from \(0\) to \(6\):

CURRENT_TIME(2);

The verbal form is "current time with precision two". With \(p = 2\) the tail keeps two digits. With \(p = 4\) it keeps four, such as \(09.4858\). With \(p = 6\) it keeps all six, such as \(.703199\). Dropping to \(p = 1\) shortens a tail like \(54.7\) to one digit for easy reading. Click analytics and intraday stock ticks need deep tails, since hits and price prints land milliseconds apart and rounding would blur their order.

Formalize. Precision truncates (with rounding) the fraction tail:

\[ C(p) = HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS.s_1 \ldots s_p, \quad p \in [0, 6] \]

Here \(s_1 \ldots s_p\) are the first \(p\) fraction digits. \(p = 6\) keeps \(.703199\); \(p = 2\) keeps two; \(p = 0\) drops the tail entirely.

Worked example — trim the tail. Take clock 18:57:09.485876:

  • CURRENT_TIME(6) keeps 09.485876 (all six).
  • CURRENT_TIME(4) keeps 09.4858 (first four).
  • CURRENT_TIME(2) keeps 09.48 (first two).
  • CURRENT_TIME(1) on a 54.7 tail keeps 54.7 (one digit, easy reading).

Answer: higher p keeps more digits; lower p reads cleaner. Sense-check: \(p = 6\) reproduces the raw reading exactly, proving nothing above six exists to keep.

Scope: \(p\) ranges 0–6; larger values error. Assumption: trimming rounds rather than chops at the boundary — ordering near-ties can flip at low precision.

Picture a microscope dial from 0 to 6: at 6 every microbe (microsecond) shows; at 1 only the big shapes remain. The takeaway: dial depth to the decision — display low, ordering evidence high.

The trap is storing low-precision stamps and ordering millisecond-apart events later: rounded tails tie, and ties shuffle. Store full precision; trim only for display.

Precision \(p\) keeps \(p\) digits — store deep, display shallow — and the PostgreSQL-only calls next add transaction-aware stamps.

Exam note: the five-reading map (CURRENT trio zoned/transactional, LOCAL pair bare) plus \(p \in [0, 6]\) precision is a recall favorite.

15.13 PostgreSQL Date-Time Functions

15.13.1 The five calls

NOW() and TRANSACTION_TIMESTAMP() return the same value — so why do both exist? Because five calls answer five different timing questions, and this section maps each to its question.

Five server calls cover stamping needs. NOW() returns the current day plus clock with zone, here \(2020\text{-}11\text{-}27\ 19\text{:}03\text{:}06.439719\text{-}05\), spoken as "nineteen hours, three minutes, six point four three nine seven one nine seconds at minus five". TRANSACTION_TIMESTAMP() matches it exactly. STATEMENT_TIMESTAMP() stamps the start of the running statement, which reads the same under a fast run and drifts apart when work sits between calls, making it the marker for stored procedures. CLOCK_TIMESTAMP() moves even during one statement, so three back-to-back calls read \(...843516\), \(...8435xx\), \(...845888\) style tails that creep forward. TIMEOFDAY() returns the same instant as text, such as Friday November 27th \(19\text{:}06\text{:}15.857627\) 2020 EST, naming weekday, month, day, clock, year, and zone in one string.

Think of a race-day photo crew: NOW and TRANSACTION_TIMESTAMP share one frozen finish-line photo, STATEMENT_TIMESTAMP snaps each race start, CLOCK_TIMESTAMP runs live video, and TIMEOFDAY prints the caption line under the photo. The analogy breaks for fast single statements: all stills look identical until real work separates them.

Formalize. The five calls freeze at different scopes:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{NOW()} = \text{TRANSACTION\_TIMESTAMP()} &= T_{txn} \\ \text{STATEMENT\_TIMESTAMP()} &= T_{stmt} \\ \text{CLOCK\_TIMESTAMP()} &= T_{wall}\ (\text{always moving}) \\ \text{TIMEOFDAY()} &= \text{text}(T_{wall}) \end{aligned} \]

Here \(T_{txn}\) freezes at transaction start, \(T_{stmt}\) at statement start, and \(T_{wall}\) never freezes. Under a fast run all three stamps agree; separated work pulls them apart.

Worked example — NOW matches the transaction stamp. At transaction time 2020-11-27 19:03:06.439719-05:

  • NOW() returns 2020-11-27 19:03:06.439719-05.
  • TRANSACTION_TIMESTAMP() returns the identical 2020-11-27 19:03:06.439719-05.

Answer: the twins agree digit for digit. Sense-check: rerun both after a slow procedure step — both still show 19:03:06 while CLOCK_TIMESTAMP() tails (...843516, ...845888) creep forward, proving which clocks froze. TIMEOFDAY() prints the same instant as text: Fri Nov 27 19:06:15.857627 2020 EST.

Scope: PostgreSQL-only names; other engines keep NOW()/CURRENT_TIMESTAMP with their own freeze rules. Assumption: STATEMENT versus CLOCK differences appear only when measurable work separates the calls.

Picture the photo crew's contact sheet: two identical finish-line prints (NOW, TRANSACTION), one start-line snap per race (STATEMENT), a filmstrip mid-motion (CLOCK), and a caption strip (TIMEOFDAY). The takeaway: match the call to the timing question — transaction, statement, live, or text.

Two traps: timing a procedure with NOW() (frozen — use STATEMENT_TIMESTAMP or CLOCK_TIMESTAMP instead) and feeding TIMEOFDAY text into date math (it is text; cast back first).

MySQL's NOW() behaves like the transaction stamp within statements; Snowflake's CURRENT_TIMESTAMP tracks wall time more closely. Databricks follows Spark semantics where current_timestamp freezes per query. Same five questions, per-engine freeze rules — check the manual before porting timing logic.

NOW twins the transaction stamp, STATEMENT marks each statement, CLOCK never freezes, TIMEOFDAY prints text — and AGE next measures gaps between stamps.

Exam note: the NOW/TRANSACTION match plus the STATEMENT-versus-CLOCK drift (identical when fast, split by work) is a compare favorite.

15.14 AGE Between Dates

15.14.1 Two-date form

"How old is this account in years, months, and days?" — one function answers in calendar units, with the argument order deciding the sign.

The AGE (the calendar-gap function: later date first, earlier date second, result in years-months-days) returns the gap between two dates as years, months, and days. The shape is:

AGE(DATE '2020-01-01', DATE '2019-10-01');

The verbal form is "age takes the later date, comma, the earlier date, and subtracts the second from the first". The sample returns 3 months, covering October into January. Moving the earlier leg to October 12th returns 2 months 20 days. Moving it to \(2010\text{-}10\text{-}12\) returns 9 years 2 months 20 days.

Think of an odometer that reads in years, months, days instead of miles: set the later date as the trip end and the earlier as the start, and the dials show the calendar distance. The analogy breaks for exact seconds: this odometer has no second hand — epoch math adds one later.

Formalize. The two-date AGE is calendar subtraction:

\[ \text{AGE}(D_{later}, D_{earlier}) = Y\ \text{years} + M\ \text{months} + D\ \text{days} \]

Concretely: AGE(2020-01-01, 2019-10-01) = 3 months; AGE(2020-01-01, 2019-10-12) = 2 months 20 days; AGE(2020-01-01, 2010-10-12) = 9 years 2 months 20 days.

Worked example — later minus earlier. Run AGE(DATE '2020-01-01', DATE '2019-10-01'):

  • October to November: 1 month. November to December: 1 month. December to January: 1 month.

Answer: 3 months. Sense-check: sliding the earlier leg to Oct 12 shortens the gap to 2 months 20 days (October keeps 19 leftover days plus alignment), confirming the day dial works. Deep case: earlier leg 2010-10-12 gives 9 years 2 months 20 days.

Scope: AGE counts calendar units (variable month lengths included), not fixed 30-day blocks. Assumption: first argument later, second earlier — order sets the sign.

Picture the odometer with three dials (years, months, days) rolling as the earlier date slides: Oct 1 shows 3-0-0 months, Oct 12 shows 2-20, 2010 shows 9-2-20. The takeaway: calendar distance, later first.

Swapping the legs negates the whole reading (covered next) — a negative age means reversed arguments, not a bug.

AGE(later, earlier) reads the calendar odometer — and the single-date form counts back from now.

15.14.2 Single-date form and sign rule

What if there is only one date — "how long ago was this?" — and what flips the answer negative? One cast and one ordering rule.

Calling AGE(...) around one timestamp measures back from now, but the string must be cast: a bare unknown-typed string raises "function age(unknown) is not unique", and adding ::TIMESTAMP fixes it, here returning 1 year 2 months 15 days from the current day. AGE(CURRENT_DATE, TIMESTAMP '...') returns 9 months 15 days from the given stamp to today. Flipping the legs so the first date is earlier returns a negative span such as \(-9\) years \(-9\) months, so order sets the sign: later first gives a forward gap, earlier first gives a backward gap.

Formalize. The single-date form and the sign rule are:

\[ \text{AGE}(t) = \text{AGE}(\text{now}, t), \qquad \text{sign} = \begin{cases} + & D_1 \ge D_2 \\ - & D_1 < D_2 \end{cases} \]

A bare string has unknown type and matches no AGE signature — the ::TIMESTAMP cast picks the right one.

Worked example — cast fixes the call. Run AGE('2019-09-12 10:00:00') bare:

  • Postgres reports function age(unknown) is not unique — the string could feed several signatures.

Add the cast: AGE('2019-09-12 10:00:00'::TIMESTAMP) measures back from now and returns 1 year 2 months 15 days (at the lecture's now). Sign case: AGE(earlier, later) returns spans like -9 years -9 months.

Answer: cast to TIMESTAMP, then the gap reads forward; reversed legs read negative. Sense-check: negating the reversed result reproduces the forward gap, proving sign is pure ordering.

Scope: single-date AGE uses the transaction now, matching NOW() freeze rules. Assumption: negative spans are valid output — treat them as direction flags.

Picture a number line with now at the origin: dates left of origin give positive ages stretching left; swapping the subtraction order mirrors the arrow right into negatives. The takeaway: sign is direction, not error.

Beginners "fix" negative ages by wrapping in ABS() and lose the direction — overdue versus upcoming is exactly what the sign carries. Keep the sign; read it.

MySQL's TIMESTAMPDIFF/DATEDIFF and Snowflake's DATEDIFF cover the same ground with unit-first syntax; Databricks uses datediff/months_between. Same gap question, argument-order habits vary — check sign conventions per engine.

One date counts back from now (cast the string), two dates subtract in order, sign shows direction — and table defaults next stamp rows automatically.

Exam note: AGE sign order (later first, or negative) plus the unknown-type cast fix are paired applied favorites.

15.15 CURRENT_DATE and CURRENT_TIME in Tables

15.15.1 CURRENT_DATE arithmetic

"Yesterday", "tomorrow", "ten days out" — do these need calendar tables? No: one reading plus small integers covers them all.

SELECT CURRENT_DATE; returns the day as four-digit year, two-digit month, two-digit day. Adding \(1\) moves to tomorrow: with today on the 16th the result is the 17th. Subtracting \(1\) moves to yesterday, here the 15th. Adding \(10\) jumps ten days out. The shape never changes, only the day count moves.

Formalize. Day-relative queries are integer steps:

\[ \text{tomorrow} = \text{CURRENT\_DATE} + 1, \quad \text{yesterday} = \text{CURRENT\_DATE} - 1 \]

With today the 16th: \(16 + 1 = 17\), \(16 - 1 = 15\), \(16 + 10 = 26\). Month-end crossings carry automatically.

Worked example — day steps. With CURRENT_DATE on the 16th:

  • CURRENT_DATE + 1 returns the 17th (tomorrow).
  • CURRENT_DATE - 1 returns the 15th (yesterday).
  • CURRENT_DATE + 10 returns the 26th (ten days out).

Answer: 17th, 15th, 26th. Sense-check: \(17 - 15 = 2\) days between tomorrow and yesterday, confirming symmetric stepping.

Scope: integer steps move days on DATE; timestamps need intervals. Assumption: month and year carry through automatically at boundaries.

Picture a desk calendar with today flagged: flip one page forward for tomorrow, one back for yesterday, ten forward for the outlook. The takeaway: relative days are one-line arithmetic.

Beginners hard-code 'yesterday' as a literal date — the literal rots at midnight while the expression stays fresh. Always compute relative days from CURRENT_DATE.

Relative days are CURRENT_DATE plus integers — and column defaults stamp rows the same way.

15.15.2 CURRENT_TIME as a column default

Who fills in the timestamp when an insert names only an id — and does each row get its own time? The server does, once per row.

CURRENT_TIME returns clock plus zone in \(HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS\) plus zone shape, here hour \(21\), minute \(40\) area, seconds, at offset \(-04\text{:}00\). It also serves as a column default. The demo table is:

CREATE TABLE logs (
  log_id   SERIAL PRIMARY KEY,
  add_date DATE DEFAULT CURRENT_DATE,
  add_time TIME DEFAULT CURRENT_TIME
);

Here \(log\_id\) is the auto-numbered key, \(add\_date\) is the day defaulting to the current date, and \(add\_time\) is the clock defaulting to the current time. Inserting only the id fills both stamps from the server: the first rows share the date while the clock advances, here \(21\text{:}42\text{:}51\) then later, with zone attached per row. Each insert freezes its own time, so four inserts show one date and four distinct clocks.

Think of a reception desk rubber stamp: every visitor who skips the date line gets stamped on arrival — same date pad all day, clock dial advancing per visitor. The analogy breaks across midnight: the date pad flips too, so late rows carry a new day.

Formalize. Column defaults evaluate per row at insert:

\[ \text{INSERT}(id) \rightarrow (id,\ \text{CURRENT\_DATE}_{row},\ \text{CURRENT\_TIME}_{row}) \]

Four id-only inserts yield one shared date and four distinct clocks (21:42:51, then later tails), each frozen at its own insert instant.

Worked example — logs table defaults. Create the logs table above, then insert four ids only:

  • Row 1: today's date + 21:42:51.
  • Rows 2–4: same date + advancing clocks.

Answer: one date, four distinct times. Sense-check: identical dates prove the default fired from one server day; distinct clocks prove per-row evaluation, not one shared stamp.

Scope: defaults fire only when the column is omitted (or explicitly DEFAULT); a NULL insert stores NULL. Assumption: TIME defaults keep the session zone tail per row.

Picture the rubber stamp thumping four forms: same date pad, clock dial nudged forward each thump. The takeaway: defaults stamp arrival, per row, automatically.

Two traps: inserting NULL expecting the default (NULL is an explicit value — the default never fires), and using TIME defaults for worldwide ordering ( zoneless clocks cannot order across zones; prefer timestamptz defaults).

Snowflake and Databricks support DEFAULT CURRENT_DATE/CURRENT_TIMESTAMP similarly; MySQL allows one auto TIMESTAMP default per table in older versions. Same reception-desk idea, per-engine limits on which kinds auto-stamp.

Omit the column and the server stamps arrival per row — and epoch math next measures those arrivals exactly.

Exam note: the logs-table default behavior (shared date, distinct per-row clocks) is a hands-on favorite.

15.16 Date Accuracy with EPOCH

15.16.1 AGE versus epoch

AGE says "2 months" — but billing needs exact hours. Which answer do you trust, and how do you compute it? The epoch path, in seconds.

Plain subtraction gets hairy once zones and daylight saving join, and AGE can blur the answer. AGE on \(2020\text{-}12\text{-}12\) versus \(2020\text{-}10\text{-}12\) (and a nearby pair \(2020\text{-}10\text{-}20\) versus \(2020\text{-}10\text{-}12\)) reports a calm "2 months" interval with no seconds. The epoch path instead extracts seconds from each zoned stamp and subtracts, giving a double-precision second count. The verbal form is "extract epoch from each zoned stamp, then minus". Because each side carries its zone, the gap stays exact.

Think of AGE as a wall calendar ("about two months") and epoch as a stopwatch counting every second between the same two instants. Both describe one gap; only the stopwatch bills by the hour. The analogy breaks for humans: nobody reads "5,274,000 seconds" as fluently as "2 months" — pick the instrument per audience.

Formalize. The epoch gap is exact subtraction in seconds:

\[ G = E_2 - E_1, \quad E_i = \text{EXTRACT}(\text{EPOCH FROM } t_i) \]

Each \(E_i\) counts seconds from \(t_0 = 1970\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 00\text{:}00\text{:}00\) UTC with the stamp's zone baked in. AGE on the same pair reports "2 months" — calm, coarse, and zone-blurred.

Worked example — coarse versus exact. Take zoned stamps 2020-10-12 and 2020-12-12:

  • AGE path: 2 months, no seconds, no hours.
  • Epoch path: \(E_{Dec} - E_{Oct} =\) a large double-precision second count, zone-aware.

Answer: AGE says 2 months; epoch says the exact seconds. Sense-check: dividing the epoch gap by 86400 returns about 61 days, matching the calendar span — the two instruments agree on scale, differing only in resolution.

Scope: epoch math needs zoned (or consistently-zoned) stamps; mixing bare wall times reintroduces the blur. Assumption: double-precision seconds carry microsecond resolution across decades.

Picture the calendar page ("2 months") pinned beside a stopwatch readout (millions of seconds) for the same gap. The takeaway: calendar for humans, stopwatch for machines.

The trap is billing or model-training on AGE months: months vary 28–31 days, so "2 months" is not a fixed second count. Money and models need epoch; headlines need AGE.

AGE summarizes, epoch measures — and the conversion chain next turns seconds into hours.

15.16.2 Seconds to hours

The stopwatch reads millions of seconds — how do you quote that in hours? One division by 3600.

An hour holds \(60\) minutes and a minute holds \(60\) seconds, so one hour is \(60 \times 60 = 3600\) seconds and one day is \(24 \times 3600 = 86400\) seconds. Dividing the epoch second gap by \(3600\) renders the same two-month span as about \(1465.04\) hours, and the stacked comparison shows all three side by side: AGE says 2 months, the zone-aware difference says about 61 days plus an hour, and the epoch difference gives the exact second count. The epoch count is the one to trust for models and billing, since it reaches the second while coarser forms stop at months or days.

Formalize. The unit chain and the conversion are:

\[ 1\ \text{hour} = 60 \times 60 = 3600\ \text{s}, \quad 1\ \text{day} = 24 \times 3600 = 86400\ \text{s} \]

\[ \text{hours} = \frac{G}{3600} \approx 1465.04, \quad \text{days} = \frac{G}{86400} \approx 61 \]

for the lecture's two-month gap \(G\). Three stacked readings: AGE "2 months", zone-aware difference "about 61 days plus an hour", epoch \(G\) seconds exact.

Worked example — seconds to hours. Take the epoch gap \(G\) for the Oct–Dec span:

  • Hours: \(G \div 3600 \approx\) 1465.04 hours.
  • Days: \(G \div 86400 \approx\) 61 days.

Answer: about 1465 hours, about 61 days, exactly G seconds. Sense-check: \(1465.04 \div 24 \approx 61.04\) days — hours and days agree, and both sit inside a "2 months" window, so all three instruments cohere.

Scope: daylight shifts make some "days" 23 or 25 hours; epoch division absorbs this, calendar day-counts do not. Assumption: division happens on the zone-aware epoch gap, never on AGE months.

Picture three stacked rulers for one gap: a month ruler reading "2", a day ruler reading "61 + 1h", a second ruler with exact ticks. The takeaway: finer ruler, truer bill.

Dividing AGE months by anything pretends months are fixed length — they are not. Convert from epoch seconds only.

Seconds divided by 3600 give hours, by 86400 give days — epoch is the billing-grade instrument, and keyword fit inside tables comes next.

Exam note: epoch division by 3600 for hours and 86400 for days in applied items; AGE-versus-epoch accuracy warning rides along.

15.17 DATE, TIME and TIMESTAMP Inside Tables

15.17.1 Table shape

How do you keep one column per kind so every later function has the input shape it expects? A three-column demo table, one kind each.

The demo table keeps one column per kind:

CREATE TABLE times (
  times_id        SERIAL PRIMARY KEY,
  start_date      DATE,
  start_time      TIME,
  start_timestamp TIMESTAMP
);

Here \(times\_id\) is the key, \(start\_date\) keeps \(YYYY\text{-}MM\text{-}DD\), \(start\_time\) keeps \(HH\text{:}MI\text{:}SS\) without zone, and \(start\_timestamp\) keeps day plus clock without zone. The property view confirms both time and timestamp as without-zone kinds.

Formalize. The table is a kind-per-column map:

\[ \text{times} = (id,\ D,\ C,\ T), \quad D = \text{DATE},\ C = \text{TIME},\ T = \text{TIMESTAMP} \]

Time and timestamp columns are zoneless here; zoned twins arrive in 15.18. Each function ahead reads the column whose kind it accepts.

Worked example — column kinds. Inspect the created table:

  • start_date accepts 2020-01-01, rejects clock tails.
  • start_time accepts 10:30:45, rejects day halves.
  • start_timestamp accepts 2020-01-01 10:30:45, rejects neither half.

Answer: DATE, TIME, TIMESTAMP — one kind per column. Sense-check: the property view lists time and timestamp as "without time zone", confirming the zoneless design.

Scope: zoneless columns suit single-zone applications; worldwide data needs the zoned twins. Assumption: SERIAL key auto-numbers; kinds enforce shapes at insert.

Picture a three-drawer cabinet labeled DAY, CLOCK, BOTH — each drawer only closes on its own shape. The takeaway: kinds are enforced at the drawer, not at query time.

Storing zoned instants in these zoneless drawers silently drops the zone. If data spans cities, add the zoned columns before loading, not after.

One drawer per kind, enforced at insert — and the keyword fit table next shows which speed-dial keys open which drawers.

15.17.2 Which keywords fit where

Seven keywords, three drawers — which keys open which? A fit table with one hard rule: day-carrying keys never fit the clock-only drawer.

Keyword fit follows the kind. The \(allballs\) keyword is time-only, so it fits \(start\_time\) with default \(00\text{:}00\text{:}00\) UTC but not \(start\_date\). The \(now\) keyword fits all three, returning the current transaction instant split per column. The \(today\), \(tomorrow\), \(yesterday\), \(epoch\), \(infinity\), and \(-infinity\) keywords fit date and timestamp but not bare time, since they carry day info: today means midnight today, tomorrow midnight tomorrow, yesterday midnight yesterday, epoch means the \(1970\text{-}01\text{-}01\ 00\text{:}00\text{:}00\) base, infinity sorts past every stamp, and \(-infinity\) sorts before every stamp.

Formalize. The fit matrix is:

\[ \begin{array}{l|ccc} & \text{DATE} & \text{TIME} & \text{TIMESTAMP} \\ \hline allballs & \text{no} & \text{yes} & \text{no} \\ now & \text{yes} & \text{yes} & \text{yes} \\ today/tomorrow/yesterday/epoch/\pm infinity & \text{yes} & \text{no} & \text{yes} \end{array} \]

Rule: keys carrying day info never fit bare TIME; only \(now\) (full instant) and \(allballs\) (pure clock) fit the clock drawer.

Worked example — fit checks. Test three keys against three drawers:

  • \(allballs\) into TIME: fits (00:00:00); into DATE: refused.
  • \(now\) into all three: fits everywhere (transaction instant, split per kind).
  • \(today\) into DATE/TIMESTAMP: fits (midnight); into TIME: refused.

Answer: now fits all, allballs is clock-only, day-keys skip TIME. Sense-check: every refusal involves a day-carrying key meeting the dayless drawer — one rule explains all three.

Scope: fit rules are kind-driven, not value-driven; midnight today is still a date, not a time. Assumption: \(now\) splits per column kind from one shared instant.

Picture seven keys and three keyholes: the now key is a master opening all three, the allballs key fits only the round clock hole, and the five day-keys fit the two rectangular day holes. The takeaway: match key shape to hole shape.

Forcing \(today\) into a TIME column errors instead of defaulting to midnight — the database refuses shape mismatch rather than guessing. Read refusals as kind errors.

Day-keys skip TIME, allballs is TIME-only, now is the master key — and live inserts next prove the table.

15.17.3 Worked inserts

Do fitting keys actually land correctly when inserted? Three inserts prove it, plus the rule that mismatch is refused, not coerced.

Insert one puts epoch, allballs, and infinity across the three columns and succeeds. Reading back shows \(1970\text{-}01\text{-}01\) in the date slot, \(00\text{:}00\text{:}00\) in the time slot, and infinity in the stamp slot. Insert two swaps in \(-infinity\) for the stamp and reads back the same date and time with \(-infinity\) in the stamp. Insert three puts \(now\) in the date slot beside allballs and infinity, and reads back the current transaction day \(2020\text{-}11\text{-}27\) in both date and stamp halves with zeros in the time slot. The closing rule: match the keyword kind to the column kind or the insert is refused.

Formalize. Fitting inserts land per kind:

\[ \begin{aligned} (\text{epoch},\ allballs,\ infinity) &\rightarrow (1970\text{-}01\text{-}01,\ 00\text{:}00\text{:}00,\ \infty) \\ (\text{epoch},\ allballs,\ -infinity) &\rightarrow (1970\text{-}01\text{-}01,\ 00\text{:}00\text{:}00,\ -\infty) \\ (now,\ allballs,\ infinity) &\rightarrow (2020\text{-}11\text{-}27,\ 00\text{:}00\text{:}00,\ 2020\text{-}11\text{-}27\ldots) \end{aligned} \]

Mismatched kinds refuse; fitting kinds split the shared instant per column.

Worked example — three inserts. Load the times table:

  1. (epoch, allballs, infinity) reads back 1970-01-01, 00:00:00, infinity.
  2. (epoch, allballs, -infinity) reads back 1970-01-01, 00:00:00, -infinity.
  3. (now, allballs, infinity) reads back 2020-11-27 in date and stamp, 00:00:00 in time.

Answer: each row lands per the fit matrix; infinity sorts past everything, -infinity before everything. Sense-check: rows 1–2 share date and time (same keys), differing only in the stamp's infinity sign — the matrix predicts exactly this.

Scope: inserts prove fit; ordering proves infinity semantics (sort tests included). Assumption: \(now\) reads the transaction instant, so rows in one transaction share it.

Picture three labeled parcels sliding into the three-drawer cabinet: each drawer accepts its parcel or jams on mismatch. The takeaway: drawers enforce shapes; keys must match.

The closing rule is hard: match keyword kind to column kind or the insert is refused. Never "clean" a refused insert by casting through text — pick the key that natively fits.

Fit keys land, misfit keys refuse, infinities bound every sort — and zone handling next upgrades the cabinet for worldwide data.

15.18 Time Zone Handling

15.18.1 Viewing and setting

Every zoned reading renders through one session setting — which city is it, and how do you change it? Two commands.

Zone names live in PG_TIMEZONE_NAMES with name, shortened tag, UTC offset, and daylight-saving flag, and PG_TIMEZONE_ABBREVS repeats the shape keyed by tag. Examples: Indian Mauritius and Atlantic Bermuda rows, Pacific entries near minus five, Fiji near plus thirteen with saving on. The view command is:

SHOW TIME ZONE;

Here it answers America/New_York. The set command is:

SET TIME ZONE 'US/Alaska';

A second show confirms US/Alaska. Resetting with SET TIME ZONE 'America/New_York' and showing again returns to the start, which matters because later queries render through the active zone.

Think of the session zone as the darkroom safelight: the negatives (stored instants) never change, but the safelight color tints every print. Swap America/New_York for US/Alaska and the whole contact sheet re-tints. The analogy breaks for stored bytes: reprints change nothing in the archive.

Formalize. The view/set cycle mirrors DATESTYLE:

\[ \text{SHOW TIME ZONE} \rightarrow \text{SET TIME ZONE } 'Z' \rightarrow \text{SHOW TIME ZONE} \]

The catalog PG_TIMEZONE_NAMES(name, abbrev, utc_offset, is_dst) lists every valid \(Z\). Stored instants are UTC-anchored; readings render as UTC plus the session offset.

Worked example — view, set, confirm. Starting on America/New_York:

  1. SHOW TIME ZONE; returns America/New_York.
  2. SET TIME ZONE 'US/Alaska'; switches the renderer.
  3. SHOW TIME ZONE; returns US/Alaska, confirming.
  4. SET TIME ZONE 'America/New_York'; plus SHOW restores the start.

Answer: New York, Alaska, back to New York. Sense-check: no stored row changed — only the SHOW outputs differ, proving display-only effect.

Scope: the setting is session-scoped; pooled or fresh sessions revert. Assumption: later queries in this lecture render through America/New_York unless stated.

Picture the darkroom safelight dial: click from New York to Alaska and every print on the line shifts tint at once. The takeaway: one dial repaints all readings.

Forgetting a mid-session SET poisons every later reading in the script — subsequent timestamps render under the wrong city. Reset explicitly and re-SHOW before zone-sensitive queries.

SHOW the zone, SET it by name, SHOW again — and zoned columns next store instants worth rendering.

15.18.2 Storing through zones

How do you store an instant so New York and Pacific viewers each see their own correct wall time? Zoned columns plus named zones — never hand-typed offsets.

Two new columns carry zones:

ALTER TABLE times ADD COLUMN end_timestamp TIMESTAMP WITH TIME ZONE;
ALTER TABLE times ADD COLUMN end_time TIME WITH TIME ZONE;

The standing advice: store day plus clock with zone, never bare date or bare time, and skip hand-written numeric offsets, which turn brittle across regions with and without daylight saving. Insert one stamps \(2020\text{-}01\text{-}20\ 11\text{:}30\text{:}00\) labeled US/Pacific with a \(+06\) style time tail. Read back under New York it shows \(14\text{:}30\) at minus five, because the instant is converted to the viewer zone: \(11 + 3 = 14\) across the continent gap. The time-only column keeps \(11\text{:}30\text{:}00\) unchanged since it holds no day. Insert two repeats the shape in July, around when saving is ending: the same Pacific-labeled parts read back at minus four instead of minus five. That one-hour move is the daylight shift.

Think of stored zoned stamps as sealed letters with postmarks: the letter (instant) is fixed, and each city's post office stamps its local delivery time on arrival. The analogy breaks for the time-only column: a letter with no date gets no postmark arithmetic — the clock reading stays put.

Formalize. Cross-zone rendering is offset arithmetic:

\[ \text{reading}_{viewer} = \text{instant} + Z_{viewer}, \quad 11\text{:}30\ \text{Pacific} + 3\text{h} = 14\text{:}30\ \text{Eastern} \]

January reads back at -05, July at -04: the one-hour move is the daylight-saving shift. The time-only zoned column keeps 11:30:00 unchanged — no day, no conversion.

Worked example — Pacific stamp in New York. Insert 2020-01-20 11:30:00 labeled US/Pacific, read under America/New_York:

  • Continent gap Pacific to Eastern: +3 hours.
  • Rendered clock: \(11 + 3 =\) 14:30 at -05.

Answer: 14:30-05. Sense-check: the July twin renders at -04, exactly one hour off — the daylight shift, not a data change. Time-only column still shows 11:30:00, proving dayless values skip conversion.

Scope: advice is absolute — worldwide event data lives in timestamptz with named zones. Assumption: numeric hand offsets (+06-style tails) bypass daylight rules and rot over time.

Picture the sealed letter flying Pacific to New York: posted 11:30, delivered 14:30, same letter. In July the delivery stamp reads -04 instead of -05 — same flight, summer rules. The takeaway: named zones ride rule changes; numbers do not.

Hand-written numeric offsets freeze today's UTC gap and break at the next rule change — across regions with and without daylight saving they turn brittle fast. Name the zone; never the number.

Snowflake TIMESTAMP_TZ/LTZ and Databricks timestamp with session zones implement the same sealed-letter pattern; MySQL TIMESTAMP converts to session zone similarly (within its 1970–2038 bounds). Same advice everywhere: zoned columns, named zones.

Store zoned, name zones, let the viewer setting render — and one setting flip next redraws every row at once.

15.18.3 One setting moves every row

Can one command re-render an entire table for a Pacific viewer without touching stored bytes? Yes — flip the viewer and watch every row move.

Flipping the viewer with SET TIME ZONE 'US/Pacific' redraws both rows: the January row moves to minus eight and the July row to minus seven, with no stored bytes changed. A front end in Python, Node, or React shows New York instants to the New York viewer and Singapore instants to the Singapore viewer from the same rows. Resetting to America/New_York restores minus five and minus four. A worldwide market feed spanning the US, London, Japan, India, and Singapore stays comparable only when stamps carry zones and analysis runs at UTC level. The closing rule: zoned timestamp columns plus named zones keep global data honest.

Formalize. The viewer flip is a pure re-render:

\[ \text{rows}_{stored} = \text{const}, \quad \text{rows}_{shown} = f(\text{stored},\ Z_{session}) \]

January: -05 → -08. July: -04 → -07. Stored bytes constant; shown offsets shifted by the session zone.

Worked example — Pacific-labeled stamp converts to New York reading, then back. Both rows under America/New_York read -05 (January) and -04 (July); flip with SET TIME ZONE 'US/Pacific':

  • January row: -05 → -08.
  • July row: -04 → -07.

Answer: minus eight and minus seven, zero bytes changed. Sense-check: resetting to America/New_York restores -05/-04 exactly, proving the flip is a lens, not an edit. The same rows feed Python, Node, or React front ends per viewer city.

Scope: re-render covers zoned columns; wall-time columns never move. Assumption: comparability across the US, London, Japan, India, and Singapore feeds requires UTC-level analysis.

Picture the darkroom dial spun New York to Pacific: the whole contact sheet re-tints — January prints darker (-08), July prints lighter (-07) — negatives untouched. The takeaway: one lens, every row, zero edits.

Analyzing mixed-zone wall times without converting to UTC compares different instants as equals — the worldwide feed stays honest only at UTC level with zoned storage.

Zoned columns plus named zones plus UTC-level analysis keep global data honest — and part-pullers next slice those honest stamps.

Exam note: zone conversion across daylight saving (the -05/-04 and -08/-07 moves) appears in applied items; the named-zones-over-numbers advice rides along.

15.19 DATE_PART Extraction

15.19.1 Syntax and field list

EXTRACT grips FROM-style — what is the comma-style twin's exact spelling, and which fields can it pull? This section fixes the full vocabulary.

The DATE_PART (the comma-style extraction function: quoted field, comma, source — the mirror of EXTRACT) pulls one named part from a date or clock value. The shape is:

DATE_PART('year', TIMESTAMP '2017-01-01');

The verbal form is "date underscore part takes the field, comma, the source". Field order is fixed: field first, source second. Fields run from wide to narrow: century, decade, year, quarter, month, week, day, day of week, day of year, hour, minute, second, millisecond, microsecond, plus epoch, zone, zone hour, and zone minute for worldwide work. The return type is double precision, since parts are numeric rather than text.

Think of DATE_PART as a vending machine: press the field button first, insert the stamp second, collect one number. Button order is fixed — field, then source — and the machine rejects reversed coins. The analogy breaks on types: the machine returns numbers (double precision), never text.

Formalize. The DATE_PART call is the projection:

\[ \text{DATE\_PART}('f', s) = \pi_f(s) \in \text{double precision} \]

Field \(f\) names the drawer (century down to microsecond, plus epoch and zone fields); source \(s\) is any date or clock value. Wide fields suit archives; year, quarter, month, day carry daily work.

Worked example — year pull. Run DATE_PART('year', TIMESTAMP '2017-01-01'):

  • Field \(f =\) 'year' (quoted string, first position).
  • Source \(s =\) TIMESTAMP '2017-01-01' (second position).

Answer: 2017 (as double precision). Sense-check: feeding the same source to EXTRACT(YEAR FROM ...) returns the identical 2017, confirming twin agreement.

Scope: field vocabulary is fixed; DATE_PART('years', ...) fails on the plural. Assumption: results are numeric — format with TO_CHAR for display text.

15.19.2 Worked examples

How do decade, century, week, and second pulls behave on real values — including the ISO week surprise on January 1st? Case by case, with the arithmetic shown.

Against source \(2017\text{-}01\text{-}01\) with time \(10\text{:}20\text{:}30\) where noted: year returns \(2017\); quarter returns \(1\) for January, \(2\) for March-April, \(4\) for December; month returns \(01\), then \(02\) after editing in the two; decade returns \(201\), since \(\frac{2017}{10} = 201.7\) truncated toward the decade index; century returns \(21\) for the twenty-first century. Wide parts suit archives with deep history, while daily work leans on year, quarter, month, and day.

Narrow parts on \(2017\text{-}01\text{-}10\ 10\text{:}20\text{:}30.45\): week returns \(2\), while \(01\text{-}01\) returns \(52\) from the prior ISO year, \(01\text{-}20\) returns \(3\), and \(01\text{-}27\) returns \(4\); day of week returns \(2\); day of year returns \(10\); day returns \(1\), then \(8\) and \(28\) after edits; hour returns \(10\), then \(20\) after the edit, while hour \(30\) fails since only \(24\) exist; minute returns \(20\), then \(59\) and \(0\) after edits; second returns \(30.45\) with six-place room such as \(30.4579\). Hour \(30\) failing is the range guard doing its job.

Formalize. The wide-pull arithmetic is:

\[ \text{decade} = \left\lfloor \frac{2017}{10} \right\rfloor = 201, \quad \text{century} = 21, \quad \text{quarter} = \left\lceil \frac{\text{month}}{3} \right\rceil \]

So January gives quarter \(\lceil 1/3 \rceil = 1\), March–April straddle quarters 1–2, December gives 4. Week pulls follow ISO numbering: 2017-01-01 belongs to prior-year week 52.

Worked example — wide and narrow pulls. Against 2017-01-01 10:20:30.45:

  • Decade: \(2017 \div 10 = 201.7 \rightarrow\) 201. Century: 21.
  • Week of 01-01: 52 (prior ISO year); of 01-10: 2; of 01-20: 3.
  • Hour 30 probe: fails — only 24 hours exist.

Answer: 201, 21, weeks 52/2/3, and a loud hour-30 refusal. Sense-check: week numbers climb 52 → 2 → 3 → 4 across January, confirming ISO continuity over the year boundary. Second pull keeps 30.45 with fraction room.

Scope: ISO weeks start Monday and year-boundary days can belong to week 52/53 of the prior year. Assumption: hour/minute/second pulls obey the same 24/60/60 guards as storage.

Picture a surveyor's map with nested grids — century squares, decade blocks, year plots, quarter lots, month parcels, week strips, day tiles — each pull reading one grid level. January 1st sits in last year's week strip at the map edge. The takeaway: every instant sits in all grids at once; pulls read whichever level you name.

Two traps: reading week 52 on Jan 1st as an error (it is correct ISO behavior), and assuming hour 30 wraps like time-plus-interval (pulls guard; they never wrap).

Wide pulls archive, narrow pulls operate, ISO weeks edge into prior years — and a movies table next turns pulls into stories.

15.19.3 Movies table story

Part pulls on a real catalog — can month and week numbers tell a release-season story? One query, grouped by parts instead of full dates.

Against a movies table with name and release date, DATE_PART('week', release_date) as release week plus DATE_PART('month', release_date) as release month sits beside each title: one row shows week \(5\) with month \(2\). Filtering or ordering by the month part tells a release story: January openers, February follow-ups, November prestige rows, December holiday rows such as a December title, all grouped by part instead of full date. Small part pulls like this turn a date column into audience-facing stories.

Formalize. The story query projects parts beside titles:

\[ \text{row} = (\text{title},\ \pi_{week}(\text{release\_date}),\ \pi_{month}(\text{release\_date})) \]

Example row: \((\text{title},\ 5,\ 2)\) — week 5, February. ORDER BY or GROUP BY on \(\pi_{month}\) clusters January openers, February follow-ups, November prestige, December holiday titles.

Worked example — release story. Query title plus week and month pulls:

  • A February title reads week 5, month 2.
  • Ordering by month part lines up January openers → February follow-ups → November prestige → December holiday rows.

Answer: parts turn dates into seasons. Sense-check: month 2 with week 5 is consistent (early February is ISO week 5–6), so the two pulls corroborate.

Scope: part pulls group across years — February 1972 and February 2020 share month 2. Assumption: cross-year grouping is intended; add a year pull when seasons must split by year.

15.20 DATE_TRUNC Bucketing

15.20.1 Syntax and levels

Part pulls return lone numbers — what returns the whole stamp with everything below a level zeroed? DATE_TRUNC, the bucketer.

The DATE_TRUNC (the stamp bucketer: chosen level plus source, lower slots zeroed, whole stamp returned) chops a timestamp or interval down to a chosen level. The shape is:

DATE_TRUNC('hour', TIMESTAMP '2020-10-01 05:15:45');

The verbal form is "date underscore trunc takes the level, comma, the source". Levels include millennium, century, decade, year, quarter, month, week, day, hour, minute, second, millisecond, and microsecond. The source may be a typed value or a table column of timestamp or interval kind. Unlike EXTRACT or DATE_PART, which return one lone number, truncation returns the whole stamp with lower slots zeroed.

Think of a stamp as a stack of dials (year on top, microsecond at the bottom): DATE_TRUNC grabs one dial and resets every dial below it to zero. Part pulls read one dial; truncation keeps the stack. The analogy breaks for weeks: grabbing the week dial also snaps the day dial to Monday, not merely to zero.

Formalize. Truncation keeps the chosen level and zeroes below:

\[ \text{DATE\_TRUNC}('hour', 2020\text{-}10\text{-}01\ 05\text{:}15\text{:}45) = 2020\text{-}10\text{-}01\ 05\text{:}00\text{:}00 \]

Hour \(05\) survives; minutes \(15\) and seconds \(45\) fall to zero. Result kind follows input: plain stamp in, plain stamp out; zoned in, zoned out.

Worked example — hour bucket. Truncate 2020-10-01 05:15:45 to hour:

  • Kept: day 2020-10-01, hour 05.
  • Zeroed: minutes \(15 \rightarrow\) 00, seconds \(45 \rightarrow\) 00.

Answer: 2020-10-01 05:00:00. Sense-check: re-truncating the result to hour changes nothing (already zeroed), proving idempotence — truncating twice equals truncating once.

Scope: levels run millennium to microsecond; week truncation snaps to Monday 00:00. Assumption: input kind decides output kind (plain versus zoned preserved).

Picture the dial stack with a clamp on the hour dial: everything below spins to zero with a click, everything above holds. The takeaway: clamp a level, zero below, keep the stamp.

Confusing DATE_TRUNC with DATE_PART is the section's core trap: pulls return one number (05), truncation returns a stamp (05:00:00). Bucketing needs stamps; labels need numbers.

Snowflake's DATETRUNC/DATE_TRUNC and Databricks' date_trunc behave the same; MySQL approximates with DATE_FORMAT grouping. Same clamp-and-zero idea, per-engine level names.

Clamp the level, zero below, keep the stamp — and side-by-side levels next show the contrast.

15.20.2 Worked examples

Hour, minute, second on one source — three buckets, three different stamps. How do they differ, and which twin returns just the number? All three, side by side.

Truncating \(2020\text{-}10\text{-}01\ 05\text{:}15\text{:}45\) to hour returns \(2020\text{-}10\text{-}01\ 05\text{:}00\text{:}00\), keeping the day and hour while \(15\) and \(45\) fall to zero. The result kind follows the input: a plain timestamp input gives timestamp without zone, a zoned input gives timestamp with zone. Side by side on the same source, hour keeps \(05\), minute keeps \(05\text{:}15\), and second keeps the full \(05\text{:}15\text{:}45\). The contrast to memorize: part-pull returns only the hour, truncation returns the stamp up to the hour.

Formalize. The three levels on one source stack up:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{hour} &\rightarrow 2020\text{-}10\text{-}01\ 05\text{:}00\text{:}00 \\ \text{minute} &\rightarrow 2020\text{-}10\text{-}01\ 05\text{:}15\text{:}00 \\ \text{second} &\rightarrow 2020\text{-}10\text{-}01\ 05\text{:}15\text{:}45 \end{aligned} \]

Each finer level preserves one more dial. Against this, DATE_PART('hour', ...) returns the lone number 5.

Worked example — three buckets, one source. Truncate 2020-10-01 05:15:45:

  • To hour: 2020-10-01 05:00:00 (15 and 45 zeroed).
  • To minute: 2020-10-01 05:15:00 (45 zeroed).
  • To second: 2020-10-01 05:15:45 (nothing below to zero).

Answer: hour, minute, second — progressively fuller stamps. Sense-check: minute-bucket minus hour-bucket equals exactly 15 minutes, confirming the dials nest.

Scope: zoned inputs return zoned buckets; daylight edges can make some buckets 23/25 hours long. Assumption: comparing buckets across zones needs a shared zone first.

Picture three nested bowls labeled hour, minute, second: the hour bowl holds the widest span, the second bowl the tightest, all centered on the same instant. The takeaway: pick the bowl matching the analysis grain.

Grouping by second-buckets when the question needs months creates millions of groups — match bucket grain to question grain before aggregating.

Hour, minute, second — nested stamps, not numbers — and monthly buckets next count movies per grain.

15.20.3 Counting movies by month

How do you count titles per release month in one query? Bucket to month, group by bucket, sort by count — the canonical date-heavy analytic.

Bucketing plus grouping counts titles per month:

SELECT DATE_TRUNC('month', release_date) AS release_month,
       COUNT(movie_id)
FROM movies
GROUP BY release_month
ORDER BY 2 DESC;

Here \(release\_month\) is the month bucket and the count tallies ids per bucket, with \(2\) meaning the second output column sorted high to low. Rows read \(1972\text{-}06\text{-}01\) with 1, \(1979\text{-}08\text{-}01\) with 2, \(1980\text{-}05\text{-}01\) with 2, \(2020\text{-}09\text{-}01\) with 2 at the top. The same query shape answers any date-heavy analytic: bucket by month, count, sort, read the peaks. Release calendars, sales months, and event logs all compress to comparable buckets this way.

Think of a mailroom sorting wall with one pigeonhole per month: each movie envelope drops into its month slot, then the clerk counts per hole, tallest stack first. The analogy breaks for ties: equal stacks need a second sort key, or their order is arbitrary.

Formalize. The bucket-group-sort pattern is:

\[ \text{bucket} = \text{DATE\_TRUNC}('month', d) \rightarrow \text{GROUP BY bucket} \rightarrow \text{ORDER BY COUNT} \downarrow \]

Buckets read as first-of-month stamps (1972-06-01, 1979-08-01); counts tally ids; \(2\) sorts the second column descending so peaks surface first.

Worked example — monthly movie counts. Run the query above:

  • 2020-09-01 bucket: 2 titles (top peak).
  • 1980-05-01 bucket: 2 titles. 1979-08-01: 2 titles. 1972-06-01: 1 title.

Answer: September 2020 leads with 2; peaks and singles listed high to low. Sense-check: bucket stamps all read day 01 (month truncation zeroes the day dial), confirming the grain. Same shape answers sales-per-month or events-per-day.

Scope: ORDER BY 2 sorts the second output column; renumber when the select list changes. Assumption: release_date is timestamp-kind; text dates need conversion first.

Picture the pigeonhole wall with September 2020's hole stacked highest, May 1980 and August 1979 tied behind, June 1972 holding one. The takeaway: bucket, count, sort — peaks first.

Sorting formatted month names instead of bucket stamps orders alphabetically (April before January). Group and sort on the truncated stamp; format only for the final display column.

DATE_TRUNC buckets power the universal date analytic — bucket, group, count, sort — closing the loop from types to stories.

Exam note: part-pull versus DATE_TRUNC bucketing (lone number versus whole stamp) is a classic compare item; the month-bucket movie count is its applied face.

Key Industry Applications

Tick-by-tick stock feeds and click analytics lean on millisecond and microsecond tails in TO_TIMESTAMP, TO_CHAR precision, and epoch math to order prints that land fractions apart — store full \(p = 6\) precision and trim only for display, or near-ties shuffle.

Worldwide market feeds across the US, London, Japan, India, and Singapore store zoned timestamps and analyze at UTC level so meetings of instants stay comparable — zoned columns plus named zones keep the global feed honest, since hand-typed offsets rot at the next daylight rule change.

Movie release calendars use DATE_PART month and week pulls plus DATE_TRUNC monthly buckets to spot holiday peaks and opener slots — pull parts for seasons, bucket stamps for counts, and pair month pulls with year pulls when one era, not all decades, is the story.

Booking and coverage systems use OVERLAPS probes and interval spans to catch duplicate or double-booked periods — the later-start/earlier-end rule automates what eyeballing gets wrong.

Reports and exports use TO_CHAR with fill mode for clean display text, while intake pipelines use TO_DATE and TO_TIMESTAMP patterns to normalize mixed vendor date shapes — one stencil per spelling at intake, FM-cleaned text at display, and compute-only stamps everywhere between.
