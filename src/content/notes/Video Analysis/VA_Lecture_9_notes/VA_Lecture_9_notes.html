# Action Recognition and Video Transformers

9.1 Action Recognition on Trimmed Clips

9.1.1 What the task asks for

We move now from feature extraction and the basics of video to the analysis part of video. Everything from here builds on top of what came before. There is a high dependence on earlier ideas. It is not a case of learning a topic, giving a test on it, and dropping it. The earlier ideas stay in use.

The first analysis task is action recognition. Later comes temporal action recognition, also called detection. Action recognition means this. We get a video. We identify what is happening in it. We name the action.

An action label (the single name given to what a person does in a clip), written \(y\), might be \(y = \text{running}\). A trimmed clip (a short video cut so it holds only one action), written \(C\), might span 10 seconds at 30 FPS, so about 300 frames.

Why start here? A full video can run for minutes and hold many actions, cuts, and dead time. That full problem mixes finding where actions start and end with naming them. The trimmed setup removes the finding part. Each input is already cut to one action. The model faces only the naming part. That makes trimmed clips the smallest test of whether a model reads motion at all. Action recognition names one action in trimmed clips.

The simplest form names only one action in the clip. Swimming, jogging, or running are typical examples. The clips are very short, around 10 seconds or 30 seconds. Each clip holds only one action. Such clips are called trimmed videos. They are trimmed down to a single action. There are no multiple actions in them.

A point of naming matters here. From here on the input is called a clip, not a video. A clip is shorter than a full video. It is specific to one action. When the action changes, the clip changes. The input changes with it. Each trimmed clip is taken to have a fixed length and one action.

A clip-level feature (one vector that sums up a whole clip), written \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\), holds \(d\) numbers, for example \(d = 512\). A class label (which action the clip shows), written \(y\), takes one of \(K\) values, for example \(y = \text{running}\) with \(K = 101\) on UCF 101. A confidence score (how sure the model is), written \(c \in [0,1]\), might be \(c = 0.91\).

The wanted output is a class label with a confidence score. Say the input clip shows running. The output could be running with a confidence of 0.91. Here the label \(y\) names the action, for example \(y = \text{running}\). The confidence \(c \in [0,1]\) states how sure the model is, for example \(c = 0.91\). The words in the room were that given a trimmed clip we find the action plus its confidence.

Worked example — running label with confidence 0.91 output. Setup: a trimmed clip of a person running, \(K = 3\) classes \(\text{swimming, jogging, running}\). The model gives raw scores \([1.0, 2.0, 4.5]\). Softmax turns them into \([0.03, 0.07, 0.90]\). The top label is \(y = \text{running}\) with confidence \(c = 0.90\), about 0.91 in the spoken case. Sense-check: the three numbers sum to 1.00, and the running slot is the largest, so the pick matches the scores.

9.1.2 Why the task is hard

Telling swimming apart from running is fairly direct. Swimming shows water bodies. What we see is mostly above the water. Hand movement is what we see. Running shows more leg movement. The setting is land, not water. Almost everything in the scene differs.

Think of swimming against running as sorting mail by country. The stamps, colors, and shapes all differ at once. One look at the pool against the track settles it. A model can lean on still cues alone: blue water, splashes, horizontal body pose against track, upright pose, leg stride. No fine time reading is needed.

Running against jogging is much harder. Both show a similar setting and a similar action. The cue that splits them is speed. Motion is what tells jogging from running. The actions look alike frame by frame. Only the rate of movement differs. Speed cue splits jogging from running.

Picture two short clips of the same runner on the same road. One frame from each looks the same: same road, same clothes, same pose mid-stride. Only the frame-to-frame shift differs. In jogging the body moves forward a short step per frame. In running the same pose covers more ground per frame, the arms pump faster, and the flight phase lasts longer. A model that reads single frames must guess. Only a model that reads change across frames gets it right.

A second challenge is viewpoint. The same action can be shot from many angles. An aerial view of running looks different from a straight view of the upper body of the athlete. A ground-level view looks different again. When the viewpoint shifts, scale shifts too. Resolution shifts too. Backgrounds change. Clip lengths change. The method must cope with all of this.

Think of it like this. Small spread means darts cluster tightly. Big spread means darts scatter. In the same way, small viewpoint change means frames look alike. Big viewpoint change means frames scatter widely. The action stays the same while its look changes a lot.

A camera high above turns a runner into a small dot with a moving shadow. A side camera at knee height turns the same run into big swinging legs. The pixel patterns share almost nothing, yet the label stays running. That is why the task needs features that hold up when angle, size, light, and background all shift at once.

Scope: the trimmed setup assumes one fixed-length clip holds exactly one action with no cuts. When a clip holds two actions, long dead time, or a cut in the middle, this setup breaks. The fix belongs to detection, which also finds start and end times.

Pitfalls: do not trust background alone — a pool edge behind a runner does not make swimming. Do not trust one frame for jogging against running — single frames of the two look the same. Do not assume a fixed camera — scale and angle shift the same action into very different pixels.

9.1.3 Datasets and the naive baseline

The field uses standard sets of labeled actions. The most used set is UCF 101, with around 101 classes of actions. UCF 101 is the common baseline for trimmed-clip action recognition. For a larger set, Kinetics is used, with 500 plus classes. Kinetics is the large-scale set used for pre-training. For a smaller set, HMDB51 is used, with 51 classes. HMDB51 is the small baseline set. Each set gives short labeled clips, one action per clip.

UCF 101 holds about 13,000 clips across 101 sport and daily actions, each a few seconds long. HMDB51 holds about 7,000 clips across 51 actions, many cut from films, so camera motion is strong. Kinetics holds hundreds of thousands of 10-second YouTube clips across 400 to 700 classes. In practice teams pre-train on Kinetics, then fine-tune on UCF 101 or HMDB51. That order matters: the big set teaches motion, the small sets test transfer.

A spatiotemporal feature (numbers that hold shape plus change over time), written \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\), is built by a 3D CNN or an R2 plus 1D net from a clip. A softmax classifier (a layer that turns raw scores into shares that sum to 1), written \(\text{softmax}\), maps \(WC + b\) to \(p(y \mid C) \in [0,1]^{K}\).

The starting point for modeling uses spatiotemporal features. A clip goes into a 3D CNN or an R2 plus 1D network. Out comes a feature vector \(C\). Here \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the clip-level spatiotemporal feature, with \(d\) the feature size. Then a softmax layer sits on top of \(C\) and picks between classes. The words in the room were that we attach a softmax layer onto \(C\), which separates the classes. In symbols, with \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times d}\) a weight matrix for \(K\) classes and \(b \in \mathbb{R}^{K}\) a bias vector, the class scores are:

\[p(y \mid C) = \text{softmax}(WC + b)\]

where \(p(y \mid C) \in [0,1]^{K}\) holds one confidence per class and sums to 1. This is a very naive approach. Softmax over feature vector \(C\) gives class scores.

Step by step, the path is: clip frames form a \(T \times H \times W \times 3\) block, the 3D net pools it to \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\), the matrix \(W\) maps \(d\) numbers to \(K\) raw scores, the bias \(b\) shifts each class, and softmax turns the \(K\) scores into shares. With \(K = 101\) and \(d = 512\), \(W\) holds \(101 \times 512 = 51,712\) weights plus 101 bias terms.

It does not scale well. A 3D CNN or R2 plus 1D net has very many parameters. A baseline of 3D CNN plus softmax did not scale to Kinetics with its 500 classes. A second gap is speed. Running in slow motion against fast movement is not caught well by a 3D CNN or R2 plus 1D net. The sampling rate changes and the fixed network misses it.

Picture the cost curve with axes for class count \(K\) on the horizontal and error plus training cost on the vertical. The 3D CNN line rises steeply past 100 classes toward 500. The fixed-kernel line stays flat across frame rates and misses both slow and fast ends. Takeaway: more classes and more rates break the naive net at once.

Scope: the softmax baseline assumes every clip has the same rate and the class set stays small. When rates vary or \(K\) grows past a few hundred, the fixed 3D kernels and the single linear head run out of reach.

Four lines of attack grew out of these gaps. First a two stream network. Second I3D. Third a slow fast network. Fourth transformers. Each one fixes one challenge of the one before. Two stream fixed scaling with pre-trained 2D nets. I3D brought back learned motion with fewer new parameters. Slow fast fixed multi-rate movement. Then video transformers changed the whole way of working with video.

Exam note: expect questions that ask which method fixes which gap, in this order — two-stream for trained-2D reuse, I3D for learned motion by inflation, SlowFast for multi-rate movement, transformers for learned frame weighting. Recap: a trimmed clip names one action with a confidence, and the naive 3D plus softmax path breaks on scale and rate, which sets up the four fixes ahead.

9.2 Two-Stream Networks

9.2.1 Spatial stream and flow stream

Why two paths at all? One frame tells what is present. Only change across frames tells how it moves. A single CNN path must do both jobs at once, so it often leans on still cues and misses motion. Splitting the jobs lets each path focus.

A two-stream design splits one clip into two 2D CNN paths. Both paths see the same clip. The first path takes spatial information with no motion. It takes each RGB frame and processes it. It reports objects, scenes, and poses in the frame, based on how it was trained. This is the spatial stream. It holds appearance. Two streams split spatial and flow paths.

An appearance cue (what a still frame shows), for example pool water or track ground, is read by the spatial stream from RGB values. A motion cue (how pixels shift from frame to frame), for example arm pull or leg stride, is read by the flow stream from optical-flow fields. Think of a photo against a flip-book: the photo shows the swimmer and the pool, while the flip-book shows the stroke direction. One still image cannot show direction on its own.

The second path is also a 2D CNN. It looks at the same clip from the view of flow. It works out optical flow from the frames. Movement is what gets caught here. That is the temporal side. So one stream holds what things look like. The other holds how they move.

A spatial stream (a 2D CNN on RGB frames), written with input \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}\), outputs appearance scores per class. A flow stream (a 2D CNN on stacked optical-flow frames), written with input \(F \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 2L}\) for \(L\) flow frames with horizontal and vertical parts, outputs motion scores per class.

In practice each stream samples a few frames: one RGB frame for the spatial side and a stack of about 10 flow frames for the motion side. At test time many such snapshots are drawn across the clip and their predictions are averaged. That keeps training cheap because both CNNs start from image-trained weights.

9.2.2 Late fusion and its trade-offs

The two streams must join at the end. The normal join is late fusion by averaging softmax scores. Each stream gives its own score per class. We average the two scores. Then a classifier, a shallow neural net or an SVM, gives the labels. In symbols, with \(s_{\text{spatial}} \in [0,1]^{K}\) the spatial scores and \(s_{\text{flow}} \in [0,1]^{K}\) the flow scores for \(K\) classes:

\[s_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{2}(s_{\text{spatial}} + s_{\text{flow}})\]

The words in the room were that we average the softmax scores and then apply a classifier. This is pretty direct. Average of spatial and flow scores gives late fusion.

A small number pass shows the idea. Say \(K = 3\) with classes \(\text{swimming, jogging, running}\). The spatial scores are \([0.70, 0.15, 0.15]\) since water is present. The flow scores are \([0.20, 0.30, 0.50]\) since leg drive is strong. The average is \([0.45, 0.225, 0.325]\), so swimming still wins. When flow is sharper, for example \([0.10, 0.20, 0.70]\), the average turns to \([0.40, 0.175, 0.425]\) and running wins. The join lets motion overrule still cues when motion is strong.

The plus point is reuse of trained nets. The spatial side can use a ResNet or similar net that is already trained on images. ResNet and EfficientNet style backbones are reused as pre-trained spatial nets. Flow can also use a trained net. So two trained nets do the heavy lifting. The join is cheap.

Scope: late averaging assumes the two streams make errors on different clips, so their mean is safer than either alone. When both streams lean on the same still cue, for example crowd plus stadium, averaging cannot fix the shared bias.

The weak points sit in the flow part and in the split itself. The flow used here is pre-computed flow. It is not learned with the task. So flow results stay weak. Pre-computed flow stays weak without learning. Also there is no joint spatiotemporal view. Each stream sees only its half. This setup splits swimming from running well, and swimming from jogging well. It fails on jogging against running. Same action at a different speed needs joint motion learning, which this split lacks.

Why does jogging against running fail here? Both actions share the road, the clothes, and the pose set. The spatial stream gives near-tied scores. The flow stream sees motion but through fixed, hand-built flow fields that were never tuned for fine speed gaps, and the two halves never meet until the final mean. No layer ever sees shape and speed together, so the small rate gap gets washed out.

Pitfalls: do not treat flow as learned motion — here flow is fixed input, so errors in the flow step carry straight through. Do not expect rate gaps from this split — jogging against running needs joint space-time weights, not a late mean.

Recap: two streams split appearance from motion and rejoin by a late mean, which reuses trained 2D nets but leaves motion fixed and split. Bridge: the next step keeps the trained weights yet learns motion inside the net by growing 2D kernels into time.

9.3 Inflated 3D ConvNets

9.3.1 Inflation of 2D kernels

What makes this different from the plain 3D CNN tried earlier? A plain 3D net starts from random weights and must learn shape and motion from small video sets, so it overfits. Inflation starts from a strong image net and grows it into time, so shape knowledge carries over from day one.

I3D starts from the same wish as before but uses a 3D network instead of only 2D nets. The natural doubt is what makes this different from the plain 3D CNN tried earlier. The answer is inflation. Inflation grows 2D kernels into time.

Inflation takes a trained 2D kernel and grows it in time. Each 2D filter is copied along the time axis and then scaled down. The words in the room were that we replicate the kernel and divide by the K factor. In symbols, with \(W_{2D} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}\) one 2D filter of height \(H\) and width \(W\), and \(K\) the number of time copies:

\[W_{3D}(t, h, w) = \frac{1}{K} W_{2D}(h, w), \quad t = 1, \dots, K\]

where \(W_{3D} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times H \times W}\) is the inflated 3D filter and \(t\) indexes time. Copying keeps the image knowledge. Dividing by \(K\) keeps the response size steady. Inflation replicates kernel then divides by K factor.

Why divide by \(K\)? Take a still clip where all \(K\) frames match. The 3D response sums \(K\) copies of the same 2D response. Without scaling the sum is \(K\) times too large. Dividing each copy by \(K\) brings the still-clip response back to the 2D response. So a photo repeated in time gives the same answer as the photo alone, and training starts from the image answer, not from noise.

An inflated filter (a 2D image filter copied along time), written \(W_{3D} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times H \times W}\), is built from \(W_{2D} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}\) by copying \(K\) times and scaling by \(1/K\). A boring video (the same image repeated in time), for example one frame copied 64 times, gives the same pooled response as the single image.

The gain is that an ImageNet-trained 2D CNN stays in use, but its core now spans time. Take Google Inception as the worked case. Google Inception is the worked backbone for inflation. Each clip goes to inflated convolution layers. As many Inception blocks as wanted get inflated. Then 3D global average pooling shrinks the map. A fully connected layer gives the output. The parameter count stays in check. At the same time motion gets learned, which the two-stream split did not do.

Worked example — inflate one filter. Setup: a \(3 \times 3\) edge filter \(W_{2D}\) with center row \([-1, 0, 1]\), and \(K = 3\) time copies. Each time slice is \(\frac{1}{3} W_{2D}\). On a still patch the three slices sum to \(3 \times \frac{1}{3} = 1\) times the 2D answer, so output matches the 2D output exactly. Sense-check: the net starts as an image net on still video, then learns time structure as weights move.

Picture the growth with axes for network depth on the horizontal and receptive size in time on the vertical. Early layers span a few frames, deeper layers span tens of frames, since pooling in time is held back in the first two pools and then made even across space and time. Takeaway: the net sees short strokes early and full strides late.

Scope: inflation assumes image features transfer to video frames, which holds for RGB but is weaker when flow fields look nothing like photos. When the input domain shifts far from images, the warm start helps less and longer video training is needed.

9.3.2 Two-stream I3D and its limits

The next step joins the two ideas. Two-stream thinking plus I3D thinking go into one model. Based on the input, the two branches get named RGB I3D and flow I3D. The RGB branch gives spatial data. The flow branch gives flow data. Both branches learn now, unlike the fixed flow before. Each branch gives class scores. The scores join late by averaging, the same late fusion as before:

\[s_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{2}(s_{\text{RGB-I3D}} + s_{\text{flow-I3D}})\]

Unlike the old split, both branches are 3D nets trained end to end, with about 25 million weights in the two-stream form on 64-frame snippets. RGB I3D learns shape plus motion from pixels. Flow I3D learns smoother motion from flow fields. Their mean beats either alone on UCF 101, HMDB51, and Kinetics.

The cost stays heavy. Inflation is still a form of 3D convolution. Compute load and memory load stay high. That part still needs work. A 3D kernel touches \(K\) times more values than its 2D parent per step, and the feature maps gain a time axis throughout, so memory and multiply count both climb.

The deeper gap is frame rate. This model locks to one frame rate. Say it trains at 30 FPS throughout. Then it wants 30 FPS at test time too. At 120 FPS or at reduced rates it breaks down. When clips carry multiple frame rates, one fixed rate cannot fit them all. That gap leads to slow fast networks.

Pitfalls: do not feed a new rate and expect the same scores — a stride learned at 30 FPS reads 120 FPS motion as near-still jumps. Do not read I3D as cheap — deep 3D stacks still need far more memory than two 2D nets.

Exam note: a likely question asks why I3D still fails on slow against fast movement, and the answer is the single fixed frame rate. Recap: inflation reuses image weights to learn motion, and the two-stream I3D form learns both branches, yet one locked rate cannot span slow and fast clips, which calls for two rates side by side.

9.4 SlowFast Networks

9.4.1 Slow pathway and fast pathway

Why two rates instead of appearance against flow? The hard leftover gap is speed: jogging against running, slow swim against fast swim. Appearance plus fixed flow never saw shape and rate together. Two frame rates see the same clip sparsely and densely, so rate itself becomes a feature.

A slow fast network keeps two pathways again, but for a new reason. It takes two frame rates, not appearance against flow. One pathway is slow. One pathway is fast. It uses no optical flow at all. Motion gets learned by the two rates together. Slow pathway takes sparse frames.

The slow pathway takes few frames over a span. The words in the room gave one frame in every two seconds as the picture to hold in mind. The fast pathway takes many frames over the same span. Its frames are dense. So the slow side holds sparse frames. The fast side holds dense frames. The count of frames itself hints at speed.

Think of flip-books: the slow book keeps every eighth page and shows pose and scene, while the fast book keeps almost every page and shows how fast limbs swing. The slow book is wide and rich per page. The fast book is thin per page but rich in change. Together they name both what moves and how fast.

Two knobs set the rates. They are called alpha and beta. Alpha sets how the slow rate relates to the fast rate. Beta sets channel width for the fast pathway. These two need tuning. The words in the room were that tuning alpha and beta well is what makes the model learn well. Tuning alpha and beta decides learning quality. Hyperparameter tuning is the main challenge here.

A rate ratio (how many more frames the fast path sees), written \(\alpha\), is 8 in the worked setting, so the fast path sees 8 times more frames. A width ratio (how much thinner fast channels run), written \(\beta\), is \(\frac{1}{8}\) in the worked setting, so each fast layer holds \(\frac{1}{8}\) the channels.

A concrete pass uses 64 raw frames fed to both pathways. With alpha set to 8 and beta to 1 by 8, the slow side sees only 4 frames of that span while the fast side sees 32 frames of the same span. In symbols for this worked setting, with \(T = 64\) total raw frames:

\[T_{\text{slow}} = 4, \quad T_{\text{fast}} = 32 = 8 \times T_{\text{slow}}\]

The words in the room were that the slow net looks at only 4 frames while the fast pathway looks at 32 frames. Slow fast frame counts are 4 and 32. The two sides then give two sets of spatiotemporal features. The join is mostly concatenation. The joined vector goes to a fully connected layer that names the label.

A second pass in the room stated the same counts through a stride value tau. With tau at 16, the slow count is total frames by tau, and the fast count is alpha times the slow count. In symbols:

\[T_{\text{slow}} = \frac{T}{\tau} = \frac{64}{16} = 4, \quad T_{\text{fast}} = \alpha \times T_{\text{slow}} = 8 \times 4 = 32\]

where \(T\) is total raw frames, \(\tau = 16\) is the slow stride, and \(\alpha = 8\) is the rate ratio. The two passes agree on the end counts even though the middle words differ. The stride \(\tau = 16\) picks 1 frame out of each 16, so 64 frames give 4 slow frames. Multiplying by \(\alpha = 8\) gives 32 fast frames. Keep the end counts 4 and 32.

Scope: the two-rate trick assumes both paths span the same time window at different densities. When clips are cut very short or rates jitter frame to frame, the fixed strides \(\tau\) and \(\alpha\) no longer line up, and the rate cue weakens.

9.4.2 Lateral links, backbones, and thin fast layers

After each stage, based on depth, a lateral link runs between the pathways. Fast features feed into the slow side. The direction is always fast into slow. The reason is frame count. The fast side sees 32 frames in one go. The slow side sees only 4. Feeding what the fast side learned helps the slow side learn better.

Each pathway can use a direct backbone. A ResNet or an EfficientNet works. ResNet and EfficientNet are the backbones named for both pathways. Keep in mind this is spatiotemporal, so the net is a 3D net. The joined data is concatenated, sent to a fully connected layer, and labeled.

The plus point is that no flow net is used directly. The fast plus slow pathways learn movement together. The only part to watch is tuning alpha and beta so learning goes well.

Beta acts as channel width, not as frame count. A common mix-up is to read beta as a frame knob. The key correction is that beta thins the fast layers. Beta thins fast channels. That is, each fast layer handles fewer channels. If a slow layer uses \(C\) channels, the matching fast layer uses \(C\) times beta. With \(\beta = \frac{1}{8}\):

\[C_{\text{fast}} = \beta \times C = \frac{C}{8}\]

The words in the room were that the channel width kind of increases in frame terms but each layer is made thin by the beta value. Since fast frames are 8 times more, channels per layer drop to 1 by 8. That keeps total compute in check. Beta channel thinning cuts fast width by 8.

A natural doubt is whether 1 by 8 of a channel even makes sense for RGB input with 3 channels. The answer is that beta does not cut the raw 3 input channels into fractions. Nothing ends in a fraction. Everything stays an integer. Beta applies to the designed width between layers. Say layer one is the input with 3 channels. Layer two is designed with 8 channels. Layer three is designed with 64 channels. The \(K \times K \times C\) kernel spec is where the width lives. Fast layers use 1 by 8 of those designed widths, rounded to whole channels in the built net. Built nets only allow whole channels, with a floor of 1, so a designed width of 8 maps to 1, a width of 64 maps to 8, and the raw 3 input channels stay 3. Odd widths round to the nearest whole number with a floor of 1, which keeps every shape an integer.

The compute picture was walked through with those widths. On the slow side, moving from layer one to layer two costs frames times input channels times output width. With 4 frames, 3 input channels, and width 8, that step costs \(4 \times 3 \times 8 = 96\). Moving from layer two to layer three, with 4 frames, 8 input channels, and width 64, that step costs \(4 \times 8 \times 64 = 2048\). The stated slow total is \(96 + 2048 = 2144\). Slow compute total is 2144 steps. On the fast side the same two steps use thinned widths 1 and 8. Step one uses 32 frames, 3 input channels, and width 1, which costs \(32 \times 3 \times 1 = 96\). Step two uses 32 frames, 1 input channel, and width 8, which costs \(32 \times 1 \times 8 = 256\). The fast total is \(96 + 256 = 352\). Fast compute total is 352 steps. The takeaway stands: the slow side needs far more compute than the fast side, even though the fast side sees more frames. The fast side stays cheap because its layers are thin.

Pitfalls: do not read beta as a frame knob — alpha sets frames, beta sets width. Do not split raw RGB 3 into fractions — beta touches designed widths only. Do not flip the lateral link — fast feeds slow, not the reverse, since the slow side needs motion help.

9.4.3 Worked Examples

Worked example 1 — slow against fast frame counts. Setup: 64 raw frames, alpha 8, beta 1 by 8. Slow frames: total frames scaled down to 4 frames per span since \(64/16 = 4\). Fast frames: 8 times the slow count, which is 32 frames since \(8 \times 4 = 32\). Answer: slow 4, fast 32. Sense-check: the two rates span the same clip at different densities, so speed change gets caught.

Worked example 2 — channel thinning with beta. Setup: slow layer width \(C\), beta 1 by 8. Fast width: \(C\) by 8. With \(C = 8\), fast width is 1. Sense-check: more frames but fewer channels per layer, so compute stays low.

Worked example 3 — slow compute total. Setup: 4 frames, widths 3 to 8 to 64. Step one: \(4 \times 3 \times 8 = 96\). Step two: \(4 \times 8 \times 64 = 2048\). Total: \(96 + 2048 = 2144\). Sense-check: slow layers are wide and costly, so the total runs into the thousands.

Worked example 4 — fast compute total as stated. Setup: 32 frames, thinned widths 1 and 8. Step one: \(32 \times 3 \times 1 = 96\). Step two: \(32 \times 1 \times 8 = 256\). Total: \(96 + 256 = 352\). Sense-check: compare 352 with the slow total 2144 — the fast side uses only a small slice of the compute since the slow net processes a lot of width while the fast one needs only this much compute.

9.5 Attention and Transformers for Video

9.5.1 Why attention fits video

Can the net itself learn which frames matter, give those frames more weight, and scale to many action types? Fixed kernels give every frame the same reach. Attention lets the data pick the weights per clip, so onset frames can count more than neutral ones.

Even with CNNs and flow nets in hand, flow computation stays hard. Transformers changed the whole way of working with video. They came from text, but their core skill fits video. In text like this apple is red, the meaning of each word rests on which words came at which times. That context from order is temporal data. Video also lives in order. Which frame comes at which time matters a lot. None of the three CNN lines caught that context as well as transformers do.

A second push is cost against reach. Two stream nets are cheap because they reuse trained 2D CNNs. I3D learns motion but 3D cost shoots up. Kernel size also stays fixed in CNNs. Attention asks a new question. Can the net itself learn which frames matter, give those frames more weight, and scale to many action types? That adaptive weighting is the draw.

A running onset makes the idea concrete. Picture standing still, then breaking into a run within a few frames. The onset frames where stillness turns into running carry the signal. Running onset frames matter while neutral standing frames do not. The plain standing frames carry no action. They are neutral. Now picture the same start but breaking into a jog. The onset looks different. A good net should learn on its own that the onset frames matter and the neutral frames do not. No hand-set score should pick them. Relevance must be learned.

A query (what one frame asks for), written \(Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{k}}\), a key (what each frame offers), written \(K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{k}}\), and a value (the content each frame carries), written \(V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{v}}\), meet in one rule: compare queries with keys, turn the scores into shares, then average values by those shares.

Relevance is learned by similarity. Each frame is compared with the rest. The scores are normalized. Then they scale a weight value. The words in the room were that we compute the similarity, normalize it, and multiply by the weight given by V, then take the softmax. In symbols, with \(Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{k}}\) queries, \(K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{k}}\) keys, \(V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{v}}\) values for \(n\) frames:

\[\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V\]

where \(d_{k}\) is the key size, \(QK^\top\) holds pairwise frame similarity, softmax turns each row into weights that sum to 1, and \(V\) supplies the content to average. Attention similarity softmax value weights with V. A worked attention arithmetic case was shown for completion and left for free-time study. The words in the room were that the case is only for attention computation and shows where the importance comes from.

Why divide by \(\sqrt{d_{k}}\)? Raw dot products grow with \(d_{k}\), which pushes softmax into sharp corners where learning stalls. Scaling by \(\sqrt{d_{k}}\) keeps the inputs near 1 in size, so shares stay soft early in training. With \(d_{k} = 64\), the scale is \(\sqrt{64} = 8\). A raw score of 16 turns into 2 after scaling, which softmax can still shape.

Scope: full attention assumes the clip fits in memory as an \(n \times n\) score table. When \(n\) grows into tens of thousands of tokens, that table turns too large, which is why later sections split space from time.

9.5.2 Encoder blocks and multi-head attention

Attention is used as multi-head self-attention. Start with input tokens. In text the input is a one-dimensional set of tokens. Each word or part gets an embedding. Think positional embedding plus text embedding added together. What goes in is not raw text. It is an embedding. What comes out is also a token-like vector, not the final class. It is an output vector or feature map that later steps process for the task at hand.

An encoder block (attention plus feed-forward with skips), written as MHA then AddNorm then feed-forward then AddNorm, maps input tokens to output vectors of the same shape. A classification head (one thin layer on the last output), written with \(W_{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times d}\) and \(b_{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{K}\), maps \(z \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) to class scores.

The change from input to output happens in a transformer block, mostly an encoder block. Each encoder block holds multi-head attention, called MHA, then AddNorm. AddNorm means add the residual link to the MHA output and then normalize. That result goes to a feed forward net, mostly a two-layer feed forward net. Then a second AddNorm adds and normalizes again. Out comes the block output vector for later steps.

One block repeats \(n\) times. Block one output feeds block two. Block two repeats MHA, AddNorm, feed forward, AddNorm. Then block three, and so on. GPT-2 and GPT-3 stack 64 or 128 such blocks in a row. GPT-2 and GPT-3 are the named cases of very deep stacks. A video transformer starts at a minimum of 12 blocks and can go up to 24 blocks. So 12 to 24 blocks process each input embedding.

MHA means the input embedding fans out to many self-attention heads. Each head is one self-attention run. Each head watches one aspect of the input. In text the aspects might be grammar roles. In video the aspects are video roles. Head one might watch pose. Head two might watch movement type. Head three might watch background or color change. Head four might watch long-term links, like frame 30 matching frame 50 when a runner circles a track and returns to the same spot. The head count sets how many aspects get learned side by side. The default guide value is 6 heads. Raise or lower it based on the data. All heads join by concatenation and move to the next net. This is a very large net, not a small one.

Put together, a video frame turns into an embedding. The embedding feeds block one with its many attention layers. Their outputs join, AddNorm runs, the feed forward net runs, AddNorm runs again. Then block two repeats it. Then blocks three and four. The last output goes to a classification head. That head only classifies. It can be a shallow net or just one layer. It gives the class label \(Y\). In symbols, with \(z \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) the last block output, \(W_{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times d}\) a class matrix, and \(b_{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{K}\) a bias:

\[Y = \text{classify}(W_{c} z + b_{c})\]

Worked example — tiny attention shares. Setup: \(n = 3\) frames, \(d_{k} = 1\), queries and keys \([0, 1, 2]\). Raw scores for frame 3 are \([0, 2, 4]\). Scale leaves them as is. Softmax gives about \([0.02, 0.12, 0.86]\). Answer: frame 3 leans 86 percent on itself. Sense-check: shares sum to 1 and favor the closest match, so relevance is learned, not fixed.

Pitfalls: do not drop positional codes — without them the net sees a bag of frames and loses order. Do not set too many heads on small sets — each head adds weights, so 6 heads is the guide and larger counts need more data.

9.5.4 Self, masked, and cross attention in video terms

Self-attention over a clip works like this. Take a 100-frame subset of a clip. A 10 second clip at 30 FPS would hold 300 frames, since \(10 \times 30 = 300\), and 100 of them form the study window. The early spoken count of 900 was a slip and was fixed to 300 in the room. Since labels exist for all frames, each frame can look both back and ahead. Frame 50 looks at past frames and next frames. It finds similar ones. Context gets learned on its own.

Frames next to each other match by default. Frames are highly linked in time. So frame 50 matches frames 48 and 49 behind it. It matches frames 51 and 52 ahead of it. But repeats at a distance match too. A runner returns to the same pose after some frames. The words in the room put the repeat at about every 10th frame for a stride and about 30 frames to circle near the start pose. Each head can catch a different repeat. One head holds pose. One holds movement. One holds color or background change. One holds long links like frame 30 with frame 50 on an elliptical track. That is how each head learns a different aspect of the same clip.

Masked attention hides the future. Each frame looks only at past frames. Frame 50 sees frames 1 to 49 only. Frames past 50 are masked out. Masking uses a value of negative infinity at those spots. Softmax on negative infinity gives 0. In symbols for the score matrix \(S \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) with \(n\) frames, the masked score from frame \(i\) to frame \(j\) is:

\[S_{i,j} = -\infty, \quad j > i\]

Masked future scores set to negative infinity. The words in the room were that the future spot gets negative infinity and softmax makes it 0. The plus point is better learning, since the model cannot peek ahead. This masked form is mostly used on the decoder side, not the encoder side. Cross attention is the third form. It lines up two streams, like video with text or video with audio. It needs both an encoder and a decoder model. Single attention is the default building block. Multi-head attention is many single runs side by side.

Exam note: if a question asks when to use masked form, answer past-only with negative infinity masks, decoder side. Write the attention, inflation, fusion, and token-count forms cleanly since mixed equations lost marks. Recap: attention learns onset weighting by similarity, stacks 12 to 24 encoder blocks with about 6 heads, and splits into self, masked past-only, and cross forms.

9.6 Image and Video Tokenization with Tubelets

9.6.1 From one frame to patch tokens

Why cut images into patches? Transformers take lists of tokens, not grids of pixels. Cutting turns a still frame into a word-like list, so the same attention code that reads sentences can read pictures.

History helps here. Transformers started in text. Then came vision transformers on images. Then video transformers on clips. The same path is followed here. First images, then video. Frame cut into patch tokens.

One frame alone has no time data. Time data is zero. Yet it holds a lot. Object type, pose, background, color spread, light, all sit in that still frame. The fix that stuck is to cut the image into patches. Take a 224 by 224 frame. Cut a grid of 16 by 16 pixel patches. Each patch is one tile. Tiles do not overlap. Each patch acts as one token, just as one word acts as one token in text. Each patch also gets an embedding.

A patch token (one small tile treated as one word), written with patch size \(16 \times 16 \times 3\), flattens to 768 numbers. An embedding size (the learned width per token), written \(D\), is 128 for frames here, so each patch maps from 768 numbers to a 128-size vector.

The counts are fixed and worth memorizing. A 224 by 224 frame split into 16 by 16 tiles gives 14 tiles per side. So \(14 \times 14 = 196\) patches, which means 196 tokens. Patch count is 196 tokens per 224 frame. Each token keeps its color depth. One token spans \(16 \times 16 \times 3\) values for RGB. Flattened, that is \(16 \times 16 \times 3 = 768\) numbers per token. Each pack size is fixed at 768, since \(16 \times 16 = 256\) and \(256 \times 3 = 768\). For a frame with \(n\) patches, the raw stack is \(n \times 768\). That stack is then mapped to a smaller learned embedding of size \(D\). With \(D = 128\), each token turns into a 128-size vector. For the whole frame the shape turns from \(n \times 768\) into \(n \times D\). The words in the room were that we learn the embedding that turns the flat patch into a token.

Height width channels turn into \(n\) patches this way. The patch size is fixed by default. The token count \(n\) shifts with frame size. Token RGB stays packed in one box. Channels are not split apart.

Worked example — patch grid. Setup: \(224 \times 224\) frame, \(16 \times 16\) tiles. Tiles per side: \(224/16 = 14\). Total: \(14 \times 14 = 196\) patches, so 196 tokens. Sense-check: 14 tiles of 16 pixels rebuild \(14 \times 16 = 224\) pixels per side, so nothing is left over.

Scope: patching assumes tiles do not overlap and cover the frame exactly. When frame sides are not multiples of 16, edge crops or pads are needed, and the clean 196 count no longer holds.

9.6.2 From frames to tubelets

Video input is four-dimensional. Width, height, channels, plus time \(T\). A 10 second clip at 30 FPS holds 300 frames. The tensor order used here is \(T \times H \times W \times C\), stated as THWC. With \(T = 300\), \(H = 224\), \(W = 224\), \(C = 3\), the clip tensor is \(300 \times 224 \times 224 \times 3\). Clip tensor shape is 300 frames THWC. The challenge is turning that block into tokens a transformer can take.

Frame-wise patching alone blows up. One frame gives 196 tokens. So 300 frames give \(300 \times 196\) tokens before any depth is added. With depth \(D = 128\) the input block is \(300 \times 196 \times 128\) in token terms. The count of things to process explodes. That is \(58,800\) tokens per clip, each 128 wide, before any attention table is even built.

The way out uses time links. Frames next to each other are highly linked. The safe claim for a trimmed clip is one scene per clip. The 10 seconds hold only jogging or only swimming. No cut. No abrupt change. So frame \(T\) matches \(T+1\), \(T-1\), and \(T-2\) well. Tubelets group linked frames. The link only breaks at a cut, which this clip type has none of. So groups of 4 or 5 frames can be handled together.

A tubelet (a small space-time block treated as one token), written with size \(2 \times 16 \times 16 \times 3\) here, flattens to 1536 numbers and maps to a \(D = 256\) vector. A time step (one tubelet group along time), written \(T/t\), is \(300/2 = 150\) here.

That grouped patch is called a tubelet. Instead of a flat 2D patch, the unit turns 3D. The patch size \(16 \times 16 \times 3\) stays. But now two frames go together, with tubelet time \(t = 2\). The tubelet size is \(2 \times 16 \times 16 \times 3\). Flattened, that is \(2 \times 16 \times 16 \times 3 = 1536\) numbers. The spoken digit string heard as one five three six is 1536, since \(2 \times 768 = 1536\). That flat vector is then projected to a \(D\) vector. In the worked case \(D = 256\).

The tubelet count follows at once. Frames \(T = 300\) grouped by \(t = 2\) give \(300 / 2 = 150\) time steps. Each step holds 196 patch slots. So the clip gives \(150 \times 196 = 29400\) tokens. Tubelet token total is 29400 for grouped clip. In symbols, with \(t\) tubelet time, \(P = 196\) patches per frame:

\[N_{\text{tokens}} = \frac{T}{t} \times \frac{H}{16} \times \frac{W}{16} = 150 \times 196 = 29400\]

The words in the room were to remember 29400 because later tasks reuse it. An early pass said 900 frames and 900 by 2 groupings. That was a slip. The 900 frame slip fixed to 300 frames. The fixed count is 300 frames, not 900, since 10 times 30 is 300. The same fix turns 900 times 196 into 150 times 196 after grouping. Keep the fixed numbers. Flat patch embedding 768 mapped to 128.

Pitfalls: do not mix patch flat size with tubelet flat size — one frame tile is 768, two-frame tube is 1536. Do not keep the 900 count — \(10 \times 30 = 300\), and \(300/2 = 150\) time steps, not 450.

Worked example 2 — flat patch size and embedding. Setup: patch \(16 \times 16 \times 3\). Flat size: \(16 \times 16 \times 3 = 768\). Map to \(D = 128\). Frame with \(n\) patches moves from \(n \times 768\) to \(n \times D\). Sense-check: the map is learned, not a fixed kernel.

Worked example 4 — tubelet count. Setup: \(T = 300\), tubelet time \(t = 2\), patches per frame 196. Time steps: \(300 / 2 = 150\). Tokens: \(150 \times 196 = 29400\). Tubelet shape: \(2 \times 16 \times 16 \times 3 = 1536\), projected to \(D = 256\). Sense-check: grouping linked frames cuts the token load while keeping motion together.

9.7 Space-Time Attention Variants and TimeSformer

9.7.1 Joint space-time attention

Why start with the heaviest form? Joint attention is the gold reference: every token reads every other token, so no link is missed. Its cost then motivates every lighter split that follows.

Plain self-attention over all tubelets is called joint space-time attention. It is used with video only. Joint attention faces all tokens. Every token looks at every tubelet spot. Token one looks at all 29400 tokens. Token two looks at token one plus all the rest. The last token looks at all past tokens. There is no split into space and time.

Joint space-time attention (all tubelets attend to all tubelets), written with \(N = 29400\) tokens, builds one \(N \times N\) score table. Its cost order with time steps \(T\) and patch grid \(H \times W\) is \(O((THW)^{2})\).

The plus point is full reach. All links get caught, however tangled. The price is compute and memory. With \(N = 29400\) tokens, the pairwise work scales like \(N^{2}\), which is \(29400^{2}\) operations. Joint attention cost grows with square of tokens. In symbols for time steps \(T\) and patch grid \(H \times W\), the stated order with time added is:

\[O((THW)^{2})\]

The words in the room were that with \(W\) and \(HW\) terms plus \(T\), the cost turns into order \(T\) \(HW\) squared. Concretely \(29400^{2} = 864,360,000\), about \(8.6 \times 10^{8}\) pairs per head before the per-pair width cost. Per-head width \(d\) adds a constant factor, so the full cost is \(O(N^{2}d)\), and head count scales it further. This full form fits very short clips well. With only 8 frames in total, joint attention is fine. For long inputs it turns too costly.

Worked example — joint cost at two sizes. Setup: 8 frames with 196 patches gives \(8 \times 196 = 1568\) tokens, so pairs are \(1568^{2} \approx 2.5\) million. Full 150 steps give \(29400^{2} \approx 864\) million pairs. Answer: short clips stay small, long clips blow up by about 350 times. Sense-check: the square turns a 19 times token rise into a 350 times cost rise.

Scope: joint form assumes the whole \(N \times N\) table fits in memory. When \(N\) passes tens of thousands, the table alone needs gigabytes, so short clips with about 8 frames can stay joint while longer ones must split.

9.7.2 Divided space-time attention

The split form is called divided space-time attention. It cuts the work into a space pass plus a time pass. This is the form that tested best.

Space pass first. Take the 196 patches of one frame. Each patch looks only at the 196 patches of that same frame. It does not look at patches of other frames. Repeat that for all 150 time steps. A food task makes the split concrete. Cooks food task explains parallel frame work. One person makes only one dish. One more person makes only a second dish. A third person makes only rice. Each cook handles a separate task with its own timing. All cooks work side by side. No cook needs to watch the others. In the same way, frame one handles only its 196 patches. It need not watch frame two patches. That split is where the saving comes from.

Time pass next. Fix one patch spot, call it patch XY or patch 0, 0. Watch that same spot across frames. Frame one, frame two, frame three, all the way through. Repeat for all \(H \times W\) spots. An invigilation task makes this concrete. Invigilation time analogy explains temporal patch pass. At 10:40 the watcher asks what each person is doing at that time. Which question is each one answering right now. Time is fixed. People vary. Here the patch spot is fixed. Time varies. That is the time pass.

The same learning happens with far fewer steps. A still corner teaches the point. Say the top left corner always shows sky while jogging. Across all 150 tubelets it never changes. No time data lives there. Now take the patch where the leg sits. As running goes on, the leg enters the patch, leaves it, enters again. That on-off pattern across frames is time data. The fact that the shape is a leg comes from the space pass. The fact that it goes up and down comes from the time pass. The words in the room were that the leg fact is learned by the spatial side and the up-down fact by the temporal side.

Cost check with axes for token count on the horizontal and pair count on the vertical: joint climbs as one steep square, divided climbs as two shallow parts, space \(150 \times 196^{2}\) plus time \(196 \times 150^{2}\). Space pairs are \(150 \times 38,416 = 5,762,400\). Time pairs are \(196 \times 22,500 = 4,410,000\). The sum is about 10 million against 864 million joint. Takeaway: the split keeps the same two facts, shape from space and rhythm from time, at about one percent of the pairs.

Assumption: the split assumes shape and rhythm separate cleanly — space reads the leg, time reads the swing. When shape and motion tangle in one patch, for example fast blur where edges smear along the path, the split loses some joint detail.

9.7.3 Sparse and axial variants

Joint form is heavy. Divided form is lighter. Sparse forms cut further. Start with the 196 tokens of a frame. Instead of facing all 196, each patch faces only nearby patches within distance \(K\). That local window is enough. To know a sky patch is sky, only nearby patches are needed. To know a leg patch is a leg, or whether the leg is up or down, only that local part is needed. This space-only sparse pass is one variant.

Sparse attention (each patch reads a local or strided subset), written with window \(K\) in space or stride \(k\) in time, cuts pairs per token from hundreds to tens. Axial attention (height, width, and time handled on separate axes), written as H-only plus W-only plus T-only passes, cuts them further for very long inputs.

The same cut can hit time. Instead of all 150 time steps, look at every \(k\)-th step. Odd steps only. Even steps only. Every fifth step only. That strided time pass is the time twin of the local window.

Cuts can also split by axis. A leg shape may need only the height axis, not width. A ground track may need only the width axis, since a road stays on the ground and does not go up. Using height alone, width alone, or time alone on separate axes is called axial sparse attention.

One more form mixes local plus known global data. Local neighbors plus some global context that is already known join together. That is the global sparse form. Sparse forms fit very long videos, not very short ones. Short clips with about 8 frames can stay joint. Mid-size inputs fit divided space-time. Very long inputs need sparse forms. Joint fits about 8 frames divided fits mid-size sparse fits very long inputs. The pick rests on setup and clip type.

Pitfalls: do not use sparse windows where fine detail lives far apart — a small window can miss a ball against a far bat. Do not stride time on fast actions — skipping every fifth frame can skip the onset itself.

9.7.4 TimeSformer block and the study finding

The study behind the pick is the paper asking if space-time attention is all you need. The TimeSformer study is the named source for the five-way attention test. It tests space only, joint time and space, divided space-time, sparse, and axial forms side by side. Divided space-time tests best. The finding is that divided space-time works best.

The block order for divided space-time runs like this. Input tokens arrive as tubelets. First a space attention pass over the frame. Then add the residual link and normalize. Then a time attention pass across frames. Then add and normalize again. Then a feed forward net. Repeat the whole block 12 times or 18 times. The last output feeds later steps like a classification head.

In short, each patch looks at all other patches in its own frame \(T\). And each patch looks at its own spot across all other frames. Space means within frame \(T\) only while time means slot \(P\) across frames. One drawing showed the whole frame where it meant one patch slot across frames, and the fix is that space means within-frame links only while time means same-slot links across frames. The attention count drops. The math for the split counts was shown as direct.

With supervised labels in hand, data hunger stays the top worry. Labels for that much video cost a lot. Marked video is scarce. That gap drives masked autoencoders for video, called Video MAE. Video MAE points to self-supervised learning on unlabeled video where marked data is scarce. It learns from unlabeled video with no marks needed. The details were left for the next session.

Exam note: name the five forms — space only, joint, divided, sparse, axial — and pick divided for mid-size, joint for about 8 frames, sparse for very long inputs. Recap: joint sees all at square cost, divided splits space plus time and tests best, sparse and axial trim further, and scarce labels point to Video MAE next.

Key Industry Applications

  • UCF 101 is the standard trimmed-clip baseline with about 101 action classes. Teams test new ideas here first since clips are short and labels are clean.
  • Kinetics is the large pre-training set with 500 plus classes that feeds ready-made video models. Pre-training here then fine-tuning on smaller sets is the common path.
  • HMDB51 is the small baseline set for quick tests, useful when compute is tight or film-style camera motion must be checked.
  • ResNet and EfficientNet backbones are reused across streams and SlowFast pathways, with Google Inception as the worked I3D backbone. Starting from image-trained weights keeps training stable.
  • GPT-2 and GPT-3 show the deep-block pattern that video transformers borrow at 12 to 24 blocks. Video stacks are shallower but follow the same block shape.
  • TimeSformer is the named study that picks divided space-time as the best attention split. Its five-way test guides the joint, divided, sparse, and axial pick by clip length.
  • Video MAE points to self-supervised learning on unlabeled video where marked data is scarce. When labels cost too much, masked pre-training on raw video comes first.
