3.1 Bit Depth, Channels and Color Levels

3.1.1 Channels and What Bit Depth Means

How many shades of red can a computer hold? The answer starts with two plain ideas: how many color planes exist, and how many steps each plane can store.

A single surprising fact opens this topic. Red alone is not three or four reds. Red alone holds 256 reds on standard video, from the lightest tint to the deepest shade. The same holds for green and blue.

A channel (one color plane stored per pixel) sets the plane count. A gray frame has one channel. An RGB frame has three channels, one channel each for red, green, and blue. Each channel stores its own tone map, and the three maps mix at display time.

Bit depth (how many bits store the tone of one channel) sets the step count inside each plane. With 8 bits per channel, each channel holds 8 binary digits. With 10 bits per channel, each channel holds 10 binary digits. The channel count times the bits per channel gives the bits per pixel. So gray at 8 bits uses 8 bits per pixel, while RGB at 8 bits per channel uses 24 bits per pixel.

Think of a channel like a staircase inside one paint tube. Bit depth sets how many stairs the tube holds. More stairs mean smaller steps between near shades. Channel count sets how many tubes sit on the shelf. The staircase picture breaks at one point: real stairs have equal rise, while dark tone steps look larger to the eye than bright steps at the same numeric gap. Still, the core map holds: bits set step count, channels set tube count.

Tone levels double with each added bit depth. We write bits per channel as \(b\) and level count as \(L\), where \(L\) counts how many distinct tones one channel can hold.

\[L = 2^b\]

Here \(b\) is bits per channel and \(L\) is the count of levels. Each added bit doubles the tone steps because one more binary digit doubles the count of code words. For \(b = 8\) we get \(L = 256\) levels.

\[L = 2^8 = 256\]

A gray pixel spans black to white on this scale. Black is stored as 0. White is stored as 255 under 8 bits. A mid gray sits near 125. That 0 to 255 span is the tone scale the eye reads as shade steps.

Worked gray span with black white mid tone swatch. Take a gray ramp at 8 bits per channel. Black maps to \(0\). Mid gray maps to about \(125\). White maps to \(255\). The step from black to white holds 256 stops. A shown swatch with 13 red steps already looks rich to the naked eye. Scale that thought up to 256 steps per channel, and the richness of standard video becomes plain. Final check: 13 steps look rich, so 256 steps give smooth shade.

Step through higher depths with the same rule. For high form video the depth rises to 10 bits per channel. We write that case as \(b = 10\), which gives \(L = 1024\) levels.

\[L = 2^{10} = 1024\]

Cinema grade work uses 12 bits per channel. We write that case as \(b = 12\), which gives \(L = 4096\) levels.

\[L = 2^{12} = 4096\]

Picture the numbers on a line. The horizontal axis is bit depth \(b\) from 1 to 12. The vertical axis is level count \(L\) on a log scale. The curve climbs as a straight ramp on this log view, doubling at each integer step. The landmark points are 256 at 8 bits, 1024 at 10 bits, and 4096 at 12 bits. The takeaway is one line: small moves on the bit axis drive large moves on the level axis.

3.1.2 Total Color Count and Gradation

Three rich tubes mix into a huge paint box. The count follows a plain product.

Mixed color product of levels across red green blue sets the total. Each channel holds \(L\) levels. The mix count \(C\) is the product across red, green, and blue.

\[C = L \times L \times L = L^3\]

Here \(C\) is the count of distinct mixed colors. For \(L = 256\) the product is \(256 \times 256 \times 256\).

\[C = 256^3 = 16777216\]

That is about 16.8 million mixes. In plain talk this is often rounded to about 15 million usable colors. The exact product is 16777216. The rounded talk says 15 million. Both point to the same fact. Standard video holds a huge paint box.

Worked mix count. Fix \(L = 256\). Step 1: square one pair, \(256 \times 256 = 65536\). Step 2: multiply by the third channel, \(65536 \times 256 = 16777216\). So \(C = 16777216\) distinct triples. Pick a triple such as \((200, 50, 30)\) for a brick red and \((201, 50, 30)\) for its neighbor. Only the red word shifts by one step, yet the mix engine treats them as two distinct colors. Final answer: 16777216 mixes, spoken as about 16.8 million or about 15 million usable colors.

Higher bit depth lifts gradation. Gradation (how smoothly one tone melts into the next) improves because more levels mean finer steps between near tones. At 10 bits and 12 bits some neighbor steps pass beyond what the naked eye can split. The data still gains. Fine gradients band less. Edits hold up better. Banding shows as false rings on sky or wall gradients when steps are too coarse. Extra levels push those rings apart until they fade.

Think of white under 8 bits per channel. Behind the scenes white stores as three 8-bit words, one per channel, such as \((255, 255, 255)\). The bit count per word stays small. The level count per channel is what grows when depth rises. Only two bits separate 8 from 10. The tone steps jump from 256 to 1024, a factor of four. That is why a small bit rise still matters a lot.

Scope: the product \(C = L^3\) assumes three free RGB channels with the same depth. Assumption: each code word maps to a distinct display tone. The map fails when a display clips, when a file uses chroma sub-sampling, or when a pipeline drops to gray. Then the true on-screen mixes fall below the file count.

Pitfalls. First, some learners read bits per pixel as bits per channel. For RGB at 8 bits per channel the file holds 24 bits per pixel, not 8. Second, some learners treat the 15 million talk as a distinct standard. It is the same 16777216 product in round talk. Third, some learners expect 10 bits to look four times better in every shot. On flat gradients the gain is large, while on busy texture the eye spots little change.

Exam note: level math \(L = 2^b\) plus the gray span 0 to 255 rewards rote drill. Practice 8, 10, and 12 bit cases by hand. Drill level count powers, gray span, RGB channels, and total mixes until each value comes without pause.

3.1.3 File Size Cost of Depth

Deeper bits cost file size. Each pixel carries more bits. More bits per pixel times millions of pixels times many frames per second add up fast. That is the core trade. Better tone steps against larger files.

Small bit rise still jumps tone steps so watch file size. Moving from 8 to 10 bits adds only 2 bits per channel, yet each channel jumps from 256 to 1024 tones. Across three channels that is 6 added bits per pixel on every pixel of every frame. Storage and wire load feel that jump at once.

This cost sits beside width, height, frame rate, and channel count. Bit depth never acts alone. All four set the raw load together. Later sections turn that joint load into exact bitrate math with \(W\), \(H\), frame rate, and bits per pixel. Channel tone steps scale with bit depth choice, so this section sets the tone and levels base on which all later rate math builds.

Recap: channels set plane count, bit depth sets tone steps per plane through \(L = 2^b\), and the three-channel mix sets total colors through \(C = L^3\). The handoff is direct: the next section asks how to grade those tones once squeeze and rebuild touch them.

3.2 Quality Metrics for Video

3.2.1 Peak Signal to Noise Ratio

When squeeze harms a frame, how do we score the harm with one number? The first answer compares raw signal strength against error strength on a log scale.

A clean frame and its rebuilt twin may look alike, yet differ in every pixel by a small amount. The eye needs a dial that turns those small gaps into one grade. Peak signal to noise ratio is that dial for squeeze and rebuild tests.

PSNR (peak signal to noise ratio) judges raw signal strength against error strength. It builds on MSE (the mean squared error between a clean frame and a changed frame). The verbal rule is peak power over error power, read on a log scale.

Peak signal noise ratio with log base factor ten gives the working form. Write the clean frame as \(I\) and the rebuilt frame as \(K\), each with \(N\) pixels indexed by \(i\). Then mean squared error is the mean of squared gaps.

\[MSE = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(I_i - K_i)^2\]

Here \(I_i\) is the clean tone at pixel \(i\), \(K_i\) is the changed tone at the same spot, and \(N\) is the pixel count. From this base, PSNR follows.

\[PSNR = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{MAX^2}{MSE}\right)\]

Here \(MAX\) is the peak pixel value, such as 255 for 8-bit data. \(MSE\) is the mean squared error across compared pixels. \(\log_{10}\) is log to base 10, not the natural log. The factor 10 out front turns bels into decibels. PSNR is read in dB units.

Why the square and the log? Squaring punishes large gaps far more than small gaps, which matches how one bad blotch hurts more than light grain. The log scale compresses wide ratios into small numbers, so a ratio of 1000 to 1 and a ratio of 100000 to 1 sit only a few dB apart. A higher PSNR means cleaner output. There is no fixed ceiling.

Worked PSNR read. Take 8-bit frames so \(MAX = 255\) and \(MAX^2 = 65025\). Case A: light harm with \(MSE = 6.5\). Then \(65025 / 6.5 \approx 10004\) and \(PSNR = 10 \log_{10}(10004) \approx 40.0\) dB. Case B: heavy harm with \(MSE = 65\). Then \(65025 / 65 \approx 1000\) and \(PSNR = 10 \log_{10}(1000) = 30.0\) dB. A ten-fold rise in error drops the score by exactly 10 dB. Final answers: about 40 dB for light harm, 30 dB at the accept line.

The guide shared in the session sets two marks. Anything above 30 dB is acceptable. Anything near 50 dB is top grade. A score near 50 dB means the mean error is tiny against peak power.

Picture PSNR on a number line in dB. The left end near 20 dB marks heavy harm with clear blotches. The middle near 30 dB marks the accept gate. The right end near 50 dB marks near-clean rebuilds. The takeaway is one line: each 10 dB step means a ten-fold change in error power.

Scope: PSNR assumes pixel-wise alignment and the same peak \(MAX\) on both frames. Assumption: the two frames share width, height, and tone range. The score breaks when frames shift by even one pixel, when tone ranges differ, or when \(MSE = 0\) sends the ratio to infinity. A perfect copy gives unbounded PSNR, not 100 dB.

Pitfalls run in a tight set. First, a common slip is to use the natural log. Keep base 10, since \(\ln\) gives a value about 2.3 times too large before the factor 10. Second, some learners forget the square on \(MAX\) and write \(MAX / MSE\), which mixes tone units with squared-error units. Third, some learners read dB as a bounded grade like a percent. PSNR has no fixed ceiling, so 48 dB can still rise toward 50 dB top grade.

Exam note: PSNR uses log base 10 and the factor 10 out front. Drill the form \(PSNR = 10 \log_{10}(MAX^2 / MSE)\), the peak 255, and the marks 30 dB acceptable and 50 dB top grade.

Real-world use is direct: PSNR is the first pick for squeeze and rebuild tests. When the goal is to pack a file down and unpack it back, PSNR tracks how much harm the round trip caused.

3.2.2 Structural Similarity Index

Pixel gaps miss form. Two frames can share the same MSE yet one keeps edges crisp while the other smears them. The second dial scores build, not just gaps.

Think of two copies of a face photo with the same mean error. One copy adds fine grain everywhere. The other blurs the eyes. The eye rates the blurred eyes as worse, while MSE rates them tied. Structural form scoring breaks that tie.

SSIM (structural similarity index) judges how alike two frames look in build, not just in raw pixel gaps. It compares three facets inside the frames: mean tone, tone spread, and joint pattern. The verbal rule is compare local light and form within the frames themselves, with no outside source.

Similarity score with mean light and spread sets the read. We write the local mean of a patch as \(\mu\), where \(\mu\) is the average tone. We write spread as \(\sigma\), where \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation of tone. For two patches \(x\) and \(y\), the joint spread is the covariance \(\sigma_{xy}\). In plain words, \(\mu_x\) and \(\mu_y\) track local light in the \(x\) and \(y\) runs, which are the row and column runs of the grid. \(\sigma_x\) and \(\sigma_y\) track local contrast along those runs. \(\sigma_{xy}\) tracks how the two patches move together.

\[SSIM(x, y) \in [0, 1]\]

Here \(x\) and \(y\) are the two compared patches, and SSIM is the score. A score near 0 means weak build match. A score near 1 means strong build match. So SSIM reads like a grade without units. Low is poor. High is good.

In full form the score blends three terms: a light term from \(\mu_x\) and \(\mu_y\), a contrast term from \(\sigma_x\) and \(\sigma_y\), and a structure term from \(\sigma_{xy}\). Each term nears 1 when its facet matches. The product then nears 1 only when light, contrast, and joint pattern all match at once.

Worked SSIM read. Take two patches of sky. Patch \(x\) has mean light \(\mu_x = 180\) and spread \(\sigma_x = 4\). Patch \(y\) has \(\mu_y = 182\) and \(\sigma_y = 5\) with strong joint move \(\sigma_{xy} > 0\). Light matches, contrast nearly matches, and joint pattern tracks, so \(SSIM(x, y)\) lands near 0.97. Now blur patch \(y\) hard so edges wash out: \(\mu_y\) stays near 182 but \(\sigma_y\) drops toward 1 and \(\sigma_{xy}\) sinks. The score drops toward 0.80 even when MSE stays fixed. Sense-check: same mean light with lost spread lowers the build grade.

Picture two small windows side by side. The horizontal axis inside each window is pixel position, the vertical axis is tone. In the high-SSIM pair both windows show the same edge step at the same spot. In the low-SSIM pair one window keeps the step while the other slopes gently. The takeaway is one line: matched steps score near 1, smeared steps score lower.

Scope: SSIM is local and needs matched windows. Assumption: the two patches share size, alignment, and tone range. The score breaks on shifted frames, since a one-pixel shift can sink \(\sigma_{xy}\) even when the scene is intact. For such shifts, align first, then score.

Pitfalls center on symbols. Link \(\mu\) to mean light and \(\sigma\) to spread every time the symbols appear. Do not read \(\sigma\) as variance: \(\sigma\) here is the standard deviation, while variance is \(\sigma^2\). Do not read a score of 0.95 as 95 percent pixels correct. It is a build grade, not a hit rate.

Exam note: SSIM spans 0 to 1. Link \(\mu\) to mean light and \(\sigma\) to spread every time the symbols appear. Low is poor and high is good, with near 1 marking a strong build match.

Real-world use is direct: SSIM is the workhorse for made or altered video. When frames are built, cleaned, or filled in by code, SSIM tracks whether forms stayed true.

3.2.3 Machine-Learned Streaming Score

Squeeze grades and form grades still miss watch feel on a live stream. Stalls, detail loss, and motion judder shape the view, so stream teams blend several cues into one dial.

VMAF (video multi-method assessment fusion) stands for the fused stream check. It was built by Netflix for stream checks. It fuses several base cues into one score. Named cues are VIF for visual fidelity facts, DLM for detail loss facts, and motion facts for temporal change. A machine-learned mixer blends them. The verbal rule is blend fidelity, detail loss, and motion into one stream grade.

Stream watch score blends fidelity detail motion into one dial. The mixer is trained on human watch scores, so the blend weights track what viewers rate as good.

\[VMAF \in [0, 100]\]

Here VMAF is the fused score. A score near 0 means poor stream state. A score near 100 means top stream state. Stream teams read it as a single dial for watch feel.

Worked VMAF read. A 1080p drama stream at steady wire holds VIF high, DLM low, and motion smooth, so the fused VMAF sits near 93. The same clip at a starved wire loses fine texture, so DLM rises and VIF sinks, and the fused score drops near 62 even when PSNR stays above 30 dB. A sports clip with fast pans adds motion stress, which pulls the score down further at the same wire. Sense-check: same wire, faster motion, lower watch score.

Place each tool by task. PSNR fits squeeze work. SSIM fits build and form work. VMAF fits stream watch work. The pick follows the use case. One score does not rule all tasks, so follow use case first and metric second. Just as a sorting task wants its own grade and a grouping task wants a distinct grade, video tasks each want their fit metric. Full equations and exact call forms wait until each metric is put to live use. The next module on cleanup returns to PSNR as the dial for whether cleanup is needed and whether it worked.

Pitfalls center on forcing one dial onto all jobs. VMAF needs a reference view and a fixed viewing setup to train well, so do not rate still photos or shifted pairs with it. PSNR stays blind to blur-versus-grain ties, so do not use it to grade cleaned frames alone. SSIM stays local, so do not use it to judge stalls or rebuffering.

Recap: PSNR grades error power in dB, SSIM grades local light and form from 0 to 1, and VMAF grades stream watch feel from 0 to 100. The handoff is plain: holders and squeeze tools decide which of these dials moves next.

3.2.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: What sits behind VMAF, and does it need clean truth frames plus predicted frames like a model test?

A: VMAF is a stream state grade, not a truth frame test. It blends fidelity cues, detail loss cues, and motion cues with a learned mixer. Behind the score sit VIF fidelity facts, DLM detail loss facts, and motion facts. Its range is 0 to 100. Like a model test it compares a changed view against a clean truth view, but the goal is watch feel, not predicted labels. Use it when the task is stream watch state. Later stream work will revisit VMAF line by line and show exact calls.

Q: Is the learned mixer in VMAF an ML ensemble, and is it run with models?

A: Yes, the fusion step learns from data and acts as an ensemble mixer. The learned mixer is trained as an ML ensemble over VIF, DLM, and motion cues, and it is run with models at score time. Still, the pick rule stays task first. Stream watch state calls for VMAF. Squeeze checks call for PSNR. Form checks call for SSIM. Fusion work tunes the blend, but task fit picks the dial. Do not force one metric onto all tasks.

3.3 Containers and Codecs

3.3.1 Raw Capture and Why Files Must Change Form

Why can a camera roll for minutes yet fill a whole disk? The answer is that a camera take lands as raw video. Raw (untouched takes stored straight to disk) keeps every tone word with no squeeze. A common raw shape on Windows is AVI. Studio kits and event shooters keep such takes on local disks or swap packs. Crews swap power packs and disk packs across long shoots since raw takes eat space fast.

A fresh take feels light because it just plays. Behind that ease sits a heavy file: millions of pixels times three channels times many frames per second, all stored with no savings. That weight is why raw is a capture form, not a share form.

Raw takes need more work before they can move well. They need a shared pack form plus squeeze rules. That is where holders and squeeze tools enter. The holder packs streams together. The squeeze tool shrinks each stream for travel and regrows it at play time. Without this pair, shares stall, phones choke, and web play fails.

Picture the shoot day as a water source. Raw capture is the full pipe with the valve wide open. Holders are the bottles that carry mixed drinks. Codecs are the press that shrinks each drink for the trip and restores it at the table. The source stays the same, but the carry form must change.

Recap: raw capture keeps all facts at high cost, so each take must gain a shared pack form plus squeeze rules before travel. The handoff names that pair: holders pack, squeeze tools shrink.

3.3.2 Holders That Pack and Tools That Squeeze

Two jobs sound alike but split cleanly: one packs, one shrinks.

A container (a wrapper that holds video, audio, captions, and tags in one box) packs without shrinking. It does not shrink data. It packs data. Each stream inside can use its own shrink rule. Video can use one rule. Audio can use a distinct rule. Text facts can use a third rule. The box holds all of them as one file. Timestamps inside keep the streams in sync at play time.

A codec (a coder plus decoder pair) shrinks at write time and regrows at read time. Codec names point to exact shrink plans. H.264 and H.265 are two such plans for video. VP9 and AV1 are more such plans. Audio has its own codec names outside this scope. The coder trims repeat and fine facts to save space. The decoder rebuilds a watchable view from the trimmed record.

A side-by-side table makes the split firm.

Fact Container Codec
Job Packs streams into one file Shrinks each stream for travel
Acts on Mix of video, audio, captions, tags One stream at a time
Names MP4, MKV, AVI, WebM, MOV H.264, H.265, VP9, AV1
Pick by OS and play tools Size, grade, and rights cost

Pick the holder when sharing across tools. Pick the codec when trading size against grade.

A fridge box gives a firm mental image that the session kept. Toss carrots, peppers, and tomatoes loose into one chiller tray and they mix with no order. That loose tray is a raw holder. Now sort each item into its own bag or box, seal some bags tight, tag each bag, and stack them back in the tray. The tray still holds all food. The bags plus seals are the added squeeze and order. A hot item like chilies earns its own sealed box so it cannot taint the rest. In the same way, a fridge tray with sealed bags maps holder and codec: a holder file keeps its tray role while each stream gains its own codec bag.

The fridge map breaks at one point: food bags seal to block smell, while codec bags trim facts to save bits. Still, the core map holds: tray packs, bags shrink and seal, tags guide the reopen.

Pitfalls center on swapping the terms. A container change alone never shrinks a file: re-wrapping AVI into MP4 with the same codec keeps the byte count nearly fixed. A codec change does shrink it. Name each layer on sight: holder first, squeeze plan second.

3.3.3 Common Holders and Codec Trade Choices

With the split firm, the choice narrows to names and trades.

Common holders are MP4, MKV, AVI, WebM, and MOV. MP4 runs across systems and earns the tag universal. MKV is open source and runs across systems too, with strong tag and multi-track support. AVI leans Windows and often holds raw takes. MOV leans Apple play tools. WebM leans web play. The choice tracks the OS and play tools in use. One MP4 box can hold one video plan plus a distinct audio plan, so holder and codec pair freely.

Codec picks trade size, grade, and rights cost. H.264 is the widely heard plan and cuts about half the raw load, which makes it the safe default. H.265 squeezes a bit more but may carry patent cost. VP9, as used by YouTube, matches H.265 grade while staying open. AV1 leans on neural nets for still better squeeze and fits stream use. Each holder can pair with varied codecs.

Worked pairing read. Take one short shoot. Wrap it as MP4 with H.264 for a phone share: broad play, mid size. Wrap the same shoot as MKV with VP9 for an open archive: broad play, smaller size, open rights. Wrap it as MOV with H.265 for an Apple edit bay: top grade, higher rights care. Same pixels, three distinct holder-plus-codec bags. Sense-check: holder sets where it plays, codec sets how large it lands.

So code must accept all holders and all codecs. A plan that only reads MP4 or only reads AVI will fail in the wild. Mixed plans force a trade. No single size fits all codecs. The trade has no dial or unit. It is a judgment call made while picking the method chain: shrink more for travel, keep more for edit, stay open for share.

Real-world picks stay stable: YouTube leans on VP9 for open high-grade squeeze. Studio kits dump raw AVI before the squeeze pass. Phone shares lean on MP4 for broad play.

Scope: holder choice never fixes grade on its own. Two MP4 files can differ by a factor of two in size when one uses H.264 and the other uses AV1. Rate grade by the codec bag, not the tray name.

Recap: holders pack streams while codecs squeeze them, and the pair is picked by play tools plus size, grade, and rights cost. The handoff is direct: squeeze lives off repeat data, which the next section names and counts.

3.4 Spatial and Temporal Redundancy with Frame Types

3.4.1 Two Kinds of Repeat Data

Why do long videos squeeze so well? Because most facts repeat. Squeeze lives off repeat data. Repeat data (facts stored twice with no new sight facts) fills video in two axes. Video holds two kinds. Spatial repeats sit inside one frame. Temporal repeats sit across frames in time.

A chase scene with two characters darting past a fixed hole in a hall wall makes the split vivid. Most of the wall, floor, and hole stay put from frame to frame. Only the two runners shift spots. The still wall is repeat. The moving runners are news.

Or take a calm road shot with trees, a building, and a small puddle. Step from frame 3 forward one frame at a time toward frame 23. The building seems frozen. The puddle seems frozen. Only tiny specks move. The frozen building and puddle mark temporal repeat across frames. Flat sky inside any one frame marks spatial repeat within that frame.

At 60 frames per second the per-frame shift is small. One pixel or two pixels of shift is the norm. Even five changed pixels is small against a 1920 by 1080 grid. A plain frame-to-frame gap check catches this well. Compare frame 22 with frame 23. A tiny bright disc of sky in the top left corner present in one frame is gone in the next. That one-speck gap is the scale of change at play. Such micro gaps can be dropped with no visual harm.

Worked road shot gap check with tiny sky disc. Fix width \(W = 1920\). A five-pixel change set \(G = 5\) spans \(5 / 1920 \approx 0.0026\) of one row, about one quarter of one percent. Frame 22 holds a tiny bright sky disc in the top left corner. Frame 23 drops it. The mean gap map lights only that disc zone. All else stays dark for still. Final read: micro change near 0.3 percent of a row, safe to store as delta.

Picture the gap map as a dark frame with one white speck. The horizontal axis is column, the vertical axis is row. Dark marks still ground, trees, and walls. One bright speck marks the lost sky disc. The takeaway is one line: at high frame rates, news is specks on a still field.

Scope: the repeat claim assumes a mostly still camera and still set. Assumption: light holds steady and motion stays small between frames. The claim breaks on hard cuts, whip pans, and strobe light, where most pixels change at once and deltas swell.

3.4.2 Spatial Squeeze and the Full Fact Frame

Within one frame, neighbors tend to match. Spatial squeeze exploits neighbor likeness inside one frame. The working guess is plain. Pick any pixel. Its neighbors most likely share its tone. So store one tone for the patch plus notes on where tone breaks. That is the whole trick.

Spatial squeeze keeps one tone per flat patch and logs the breaks. In practice this leans on entropy coding and block cosine plans of the JPEG family. The verbal rule is keep one tone per flat patch and log the breaks. Smooth sky, wall, and road zones shrink hard under this rule, while busy texture shrinks less.

The frame that keeps full spatial facts after this pass is the I-frame (the information frame). It is squeezed yet whole. It needs no other frame to paint fully. It serves as the anchor on which later delta frames lean.

We write the grid size facts as width \(W\) and height \(H\), where \(W = 1920\) and \(H = 1080\) in the road shot case. A five-pixel change set \(G\) against \(W\) alone reads as \(5\) over \(1920\), a tiny share. That share is why spatial plans win big on flat zones like sky, wall, and road.

Pitfalls center on reading I-frames as raw frames. An I-frame is squeezed yet whole, not untouched raw. It trims spatial repeat inside itself, but it keeps enough facts to paint alone with no outside help.

3.4.3 Temporal Squeeze with Forward and Two-Way Frames

Across frames, the past predicts the now. Temporal squeeze exploits likeness across frames. Keep one full frame. For later frames keep only the delta, the changed specks. The verbal rule is full facts once, then deltas after. From a full frame plus a delta, a later frame can be rebuilt by plain add steps or by a learned predictor. No neural net is needed for the base form.

Compression ratio is raw size over packed size, which is the same ratio lens used for frame chains. We write raw size as \(S_{raw}\) and packed size as \(S_{pack}\).

\[C = \frac{S_{raw}}{S_{pack}}\]

Here \(C\) is the ratio of raw facts to stored facts. Delta frames lift \(C\) because each delta stores specks, not full grids.

The three frame kinds form a chain. The I-frame holds full facts. The P-frame (the predicted frame) builds by forward look from an I-frame plus deltas. The B-frame (the two-way frame) builds by looking both ways at near full or predicted frames. So a B-frame with index 2 can blend facts from the I-frame and the next P-frame. A later B-frame with index 3 can blend the same pair with distinct weights. B-frames keep the least data of the three.

A rough cadence makes the chain firm. With 30 frames in one second, mark every 10th frame as an I-frame. Frames 1, 10, 20, and 30 anchor the second. Mark every 5th frame between anchors as a P-frame. Frames 5, 15, and 25 predict forward from anchors. All frames between these marks, such as 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, and 9, are B-frames fused from both sides.

Worked chain trace. Anchor frame 1 as I with full facts. Frame 5 as P stores forward deltas from frame 1: shifted runner spots plus the lost sky disc. Frame 2 as B blends frame 1 and frame 5 with weights that favor the nearer anchor. At rebuild, frame 2 = blend(I1, P5) + small fix-up. Frame 3 blends the same pair with shifted weights. Sense-check: I holds the most data, P holds less, B holds the least.

This chain bites at sample time. A B-frame sample holds far less true detail than an I-frame sample. So when one frame per second is drawn from a 30-frame run, the draw must land on an I-frame. Blind draw may land on weak frame so land on full frame. A blind draw that lands on a B-frame or a P-frame hands weak facts to all later steps, since every later grade then scores thinned facts.

3.5 Bitrate, Compression Ratio and 4K Transfer Math

3.5.1 Ratio and Rate Facts

Two numbers steer every move-or-store choice: how much smaller the file got, and how many bits must move each second.

Compression ratio (raw size over packed size) compares raw size with packed size. We write raw size as \(S_{raw}\) and packed size as \(S_{pack}\), both in the same units. The verbal rule is raw over packed.

\[C = \frac{S_{raw}}{S_{pack}}\]

Here \(C\) is the ratio, often spoken as \(C\) to 1. Values like 20 to 1 or 30 to 1 are common. Deep squeeze can reach 200 to 1. But deep squeeze harms grade since mid frames thin out. Grade loss then leaks into later analysis steps, so a detector trained on clean frames may miss on thinned frames.

Bitrate (bits moved per second) sets the wire load. We write it in bps units. High bitrate means strong grade plus large files. Low bitrate means small files plus weak grade. Squeeze, travel, and store move as one linked triple. A codec pick moves all three at once: a stronger squeeze lowers travel and store together, but also lowers grade.

Picture the triple as a triangle with squeeze, travel, and store at the corners. Pulling one corner drags the other two. The takeaway is one line: no codec moves one corner alone.

Scope: the ratio \(C\) compares like with like: same clip, same length, raw versus packed. Assumption: both sizes use the same units. The ratio breaks when raw counts headers or audio that packed drops, so fix the count scope before dividing.

3.5.2 Mathematical Formulation of Raw Rate

Raw rate math is a straight product with no hidden steps.

Raw rate from width height frames bits pixel sets the base. We write width as \(W\), height as \(H\), frames per second as \(f\), and bits per channel-pixel as \(B\), where \(B\) counts all channels. For RGB at 8 bits per channel, \(B = 8 \times 3 = 24\). The verbal rule is frame pixels times frames each second times bits per pixel.

\[R_{raw} = W \times H \times f \times B\]

Here \(R_{raw}\) is raw bits per second. \(W \times H\) is pixels per frame. \(f\) is frames per second. \(B\) is bits per pixel across channels. Nothing is split into bytes here. Travel math stays in bits.

Packed rate follows by splitting raw rate by the ratio. We write the codec ratio as \(C\) and the travel rate as \(R_{net}\). Packed wire rate is raw over codec ratio.

\[R_{net} = \frac{R_{raw}}{C}\]

Here \(R_{net}\) is bits per second on the wire for that codec. A larger \(C\) means a smaller wire load. Wire load near half across codecs at stated ratios is the pattern to watch: double the ratio, halve the wire.

Worked unit check. Take \(W = 1920\), \(H = 1080\), \(f = 30\), \(B = 24\). Pixels per frame: \(1920 \times 1080 = 2073600\) pixels. Pixels per second: \(2073600 \times 30 = 62208000\). Bits per second: \(62208000 \times 24 = 1492992000\) bps. Units track as pixels times frames per second times bits per pixel, leaving bits per second. Final answer: about \(1.5 \times 10^9\) bps raw.

Pitfalls center on units. Never split travel math by 8 into bytes. Never use 8 alone for \(B\) on RGB: three channels each need 8 bits, so \(B = 24\). Never mix Mbps with MBps: Mbps is megabits per second on the wire, while MBps would be megabytes per second on disk.

3.5.3 Worked 4K Transfer Case

A full 4K pass pins the chain down with real numbers.

Worked 4K transfer math with AV1 and H264 wire rates. Setup: 4K frame at 3840 by 2160, rate 60 frames per second, RGB at 8 bits per channel. So channel bits give \(B = 24\). AV1 ratio is given as 800. H.264 ratio is taken as 400. Goal: least wire rate that still moves the run well.

Step 1: pixels per frame. \(3840 \times 2160 = 8294400\). Step 2: pixels per second. \(8294400 \times 60 = 497664000\). Step 3: raw bits per second. \(497664000 \times 24 = 11943936000\). So \(R_{raw} = 11943936000\) bps, about \(11.94 \times 10^9\) bps. Keep it in bits per second with no byte split.

Step 4: AV1 wire rate. \(R_{net} = 11943936000 / 800 = 14929920\) bps, about 14.93 Mbps, spoken as about 15 Mbps. Step 5: H.264 wire rate. \(R_{net} = 11943936000 / 400 = 29859840\) bps, about 29.86 Mbps. So the AV1 wire load is about half the H.264 wire load at these stated ratios. The squeeze pick sets the wire cost head-on. Final answers: raw 11943936000 bps, AV1 near 15 Mbps, H.264 near 30 Mbps.

Sense-check the scale: 4K at 60 frames per second moves about eight times the pixels per second of 1080p at 30 frames per second, so a raw rate near 12 billion bits per second fits. Packing by 800 brings it into a home-stream wire near 15 Mbps, which matches real stream tiers.

Exam note: raw rate is \(W \times H \times f \times B\) with \(B = 24\) for RGB at 8 bits per channel. Packed rate is raw over ratio. Drill raw rate product, packed rate split, and bits per second answers. Keep answers in bits per second.

A: No, keep bits per second as is. Travel and stream math count bits moved per second, not bytes held on disk. 3.8 Frame Sampling and Frame Quality Checks

3.8.1 Four Ways to Draw Frames

Full takes are too dense to move frame by frame each time. Draws cut the load. Four draw plans ship in the helper set. Uniform draw keeps every \(s\)-th frame. Rate draw keeps frames to hit a target rate. Key draw keeps only scene-change frames. Dense draw keeps all frames with no skip.

A ten-minute phone take at 30 fps holds 18000 frames. Scoring all of them on a laptop stalls the loop. Keeping 30 sharp frames that span the story gives nearly the same signal at a tiny fraction of the cost. Sampling is that trade made exact.

Uniform math is plain. We write step as \(s\), kept slot as \(k\), and source spot as \(i_k\), where kept frame \(k\) maps to source spot \(k\) times step. Python spots start at 0, so spots run 0, 10, 20, 30, and on for \(s = 10\).

\[i_k = k \times s\]

Here \(s = 10\) keeps every 10th frame. With \(N = 298\) frames the keep count is about 30 frames banked as JPEG files in a frames folder. Rate math is also plain. We write source rate as \(f\) and target rate as \(g\). The gap \(v\) is source over target.

\[v = \frac{f}{g}\]

Here \(g = 1\) keeps about one frame per second. For \(f = 30\) and \(g = 1\), the gap is \(v = 30\), so every 30th frame is kept. Key math rests on gaps. We write past frame as \(F_{t-1}\) and now frame as \(F_t\). A frame turns key when the mean gap tops 30, which marks a scene break.

\[key \iff mean(|F_t - F_{t-1}|) > 30\]

Dense draw skips all math and banks each frame. Dense runs take time and disk since each banked JPEG writes to the local drive. Patience pays here.

Picture the four plans on one timeline. Uniform ticks at fixed steps. Rate ticks at one-per-second marks. Key ticks only at jumps where the gap spikes past 30. Dense ticks everywhere. The takeaway is one line: fixed steps span time, key steps catch story joints.

3.9 Preprocessing Steps and Motion Masks

3.9.1 Five-Step Chain and Its Order

Models do not eat raw takes. Cleanup before model work hinges on the end task. Act capture, fall watch, lane trace, and foot patrol each want a distinct chain. Some chains need all five steps. Some chains skip one or two. The five base steps are size set, range set, color set, grain cut, and time steady.

A detector trained on 224 by 224 art photos will starve on 1080p road frames unless each frame is first remapped to its grid. A shape tracker will drown in color noise unless paint is first trimmed to gray. The chain is the adapter between wild takes and fixed nets.

Each step has one job. Size set remaps each frame to a model-sized grid. A backbone such as ResNet wants one grid size. A detector such as YOLO wants a distinct grid size. Range set maps 0 to 255 tones into 0 to 1. That min-max map suits deep nets, which train best on small centered inputs. Color set swaps color form. Grain cut trims sensor grain with a soft blur. Time steady tracks gaps across frames to calm flicker.

Order matters. First size set with linear blend, since later steps then touch fewer pixels. Next grain cut on the small frame when asked. Next color set on the cleaned small frame. Last range set by a split of 255. Never swap color set ahead of size set and grain cut in this chain. The chain acts frame by frame, not on the whole file at once. All size, flag, and form values stay tunable. Order matters so size first and range last: shrink early to save work, scale late to feed the net clean numbers.

The range map is one line. We write raw tone as \(p\) and mapped tone as \(q\). Range map splits raw tone by 255 into the unit span.

\[q = \frac{p}{255}\]

Here \(p\) spans 0 to 255 and \(q\) spans 0 to 1. The map hits RGB, HSV, and YUV forms alike since all channel words split by 255. A mid tone \(p = 125\) lands at \(q \approx 0.49\), just below half.

Color pick tracks the task. When tone shapes carry the task, such as act or shape trace, gray form saves compute with no harm. When paint carries the task, such as garb or art sort, RGB form must stay. HSV splits paint angle from depth and light: hue holds the paint angle, saturation holds depth, value holds light. YUV splits light from paint gaps: Y holds light, U and V hold paint gaps. Each form serves a distinct task family.

Scope: the five-step chain assumes frame-wise still models. Assumption: each frame is scored on its own. The chain breaks for clip models that need time order kept intact, where time steady and gap logic must widen into true temporal windows rather than single-frame passes.

Pitfalls center on order swaps. Sizing after color swap wastes work on pixels that shrink will drop. Ranging before grain cut lets grain set the min-max span. Denoising on full HD before sizing burns time for no gain. Hold the order: size set, then grain cut, then color set, then range set.
