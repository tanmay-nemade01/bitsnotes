5.1 Frame Rate Normalization for Temporal Consistency

5.1.1 Why Normalization Matters for Space and Time

Normalization, or bringing all clips into one shared shape before analysis, is the first gate in any video pipeline. Two axes must line up: frame rate and frame resolution. Frame rate, or how many still pictures pass per second, written in frames per second, might be 24 for film-style clips and 30 for many camera clips. Temporal consistency, or the smooth hand-off of motion from one frame to the next, depends on that rate. Spatial consistency, or how much detail each single frame holds, depends on resolution. Both feed spatial-temporal features, or joint place-and-change marks such as a moving edge, so a mismatch in either axis weakens every later step.

Mixed cameras never agree by default. One phone shoots 24 frames per second, one webcam shoots 30, one broadcast file shoots 25. If those clips enter one model raw, the same walk looks slow in one clip and rushed in another. Fixing rate and size first keeps later motion math honest.

Think of prices in mixed currencies. A bill of 100 yen and a bill of 100 rupees share a number but not a value. Converting both to one currency comes before adding. Frame rate normalization is that currency swap for time: every clip is re-expressed at one shared rate so one frame step always means the same time step. The analogy breaks at one point: money swaps lose nothing, while rate swaps can add fake frames or drop real ones, so the choice of method matters.

Picture a timeline with ticks. At 30 frames per second each tick sits about 0.033 seconds apart, since \(1 / 30 \approx 0.033\). At 24 frames per second each tick sits about 0.042 seconds apart, since \(1 / 24 \approx 0.042\). A shared timeline picks one spacing and rebuilds every clip onto it. That shared spacing is what later optical flow and tracking assume without asking.

Scope: Normalization applies when clips share a task but differ in capture settings. It assumes the scene content survives re-sampling, which holds for ordinary rate shifts such as 24 to 30 frames per second. It breaks when the source rate is far below the motion speed, since no blend can invent a fast swing that was never sampled. It also breaks when timestamps are wrong, since re-sampling a broken clock spreads the error.

One-line recap: rate lines up time and resolution lines up space, and both must match before joint features mean anything. With that shared clock in place, the next step is how to fill missing time slots.

Real use runs everywhere: a training folder with phone, webcam, and broadcast clips must pass one rate gate before one action model trains, and a guard network with mixed old and new cameras must share one rate before one tracker runs across views.

5.1.2 Frame Duplication

Frame duplication, or repeating one frame to fill a missing time slot with no fresh pixels computed, is the cheapest fix for a low rate. A frame that held the screen for one slot now holds it for two. A 30 frames per second clip gives each slot \(1/30\) seconds, or about 33 milliseconds. A 24 frames per second clip gives each slot \(1/24\) seconds, or about 42 milliseconds. Duplication maps a 24-rate stream onto a 30-rate timeline by repeating roughly every fourth frame, so the clip length matches while the motion inside stays stepped.

Duplication copies the full frame array unchanged. With \(F_{t}\) as the frame at time step \(t\), the filled slot is \(F_{t+1} = F_{t}\), pixel for pixel. No blend weight, no motion vector, and no new edge is computed.

Take a slow hallway scene. Frame 10 shows a person mid-step, frame 11 is missing on the shared timeline, and duplication sets frame 11 equal to frame 10. The eye sees a tiny pause and then motion resumes, which is fine because the body moved only a few pixels. Take a fast cricket swing instead. The bat jumps many pixels per slot, so the repeated frame shows the bat frozen mid-air for one extra slot and then jumping forward. The eye reads that freeze-jump as choppy motion.

Visual intuition: draw time on the horizontal axis in frame slots and ball position on the vertical axis in pixels. True fast motion is a steep straight line. Duplication turns that line into stairs: flat repeats joined by sharp jumps. The takeaway is direct: stairs match gentle slopes well and steep slopes poorly, which is why slow scenes survive and fast action looks choppy.

Assumption: Duplication assumes motion between repeats is small enough that a frozen frame passes as smooth. That holds for slow pans, still talks, and distant traffic. When the assumption fails on fast swings, ball flights, or close passes, the freeze becomes visible and motion features downstream read false stops.

Pitfalls cluster here. One trap is using duplication as the default for every clip because it is fast; fast clips then train the model on false stills. A second trap is duplicating to lift a very low rate, such as 10 frames per second, up to 30; two of every three frames are then fake stills and any speed estimate collapses. A third trap is forgetting labels: if frame counts feed speed math, duplicated frames must be flagged or speed reads low.

Duplication costs almost nothing and fits slow scenes and quick fixes, but it adds no true middle position, so fast motion turns choppy. The next step builds a real middle frame by blending neighbours.

5.1.3 Linear Interpolation and Motion Compensation

Linear interpolation, or building each missing frame as a straight blend of its two neighbours, adds a true middle where duplication only repeats. Motion compensation, or first estimating movement and then placing the middle frame along that movement, goes one step further and keeps edges sharp.

Q: Why does a plain blend blur a fast ball but look fine on a slow walk? A: A blend mixes pixels from two positions. When the shift is small, the two copies sit nearly on top of each other and the mix looks sharp. When the shift is large, the two copies sit apart and the mix shows both at half strength, which reads as a ghost trail.

With \(F_{before}\) as the earlier neighbour frame array, \(F_{after}\) as the later neighbour frame array, and \(w\) as a scalar blend weight with no units in the range from zero to one, the middle frame is:

\[F_{mid} = (1 - w) F_{before} + w F_{after}\]

Here each symbol is a full frame of pixel intensities, and the multiply is per pixel. Setting \(w = 0.5\) gives an even mix, so each middle pixel is half the before pixel plus half the after pixel. Setting \(w = 0.25\) leans toward the past, and \(w = 0.75\) leans toward the future. The weights sum to one, so brightness holds steady instead of drifting up or down.

Build the meaning step by step. Start with one pixel that reads 100 in the before frame and 140 in the after frame. With \(w = 0.5\), the middle is \((1 - 0.5) \times 100 + 0.5 \times 140 = 50 + 70 = 120\). The middle sits halfway, as it should. With \(w = 0.6\), the middle is \(0.4 \times 100 + 0.6 \times 140 = 40 + 84 = 124\), leaning toward the future value. Do that same weighted sum at every pixel and the whole frame is done. A quick sense-check: when \(w = 0\) the middle equals the before frame, and when \(w = 1\) it equals the after frame, so the ends behave.

Slow hallway blend. Before pixel 100, after pixel 106, \(w = 0.5\). Middle is \(0.5 \times 100 + 0.5 \times 106 = 50 + 53 = 103\). The shift is only 6 levels across the gap, so the blend sits 3 above the before value and looks clean. Fast ball blend. Before pixel 40 (dark ball), after pixel 200 (bright field) at the same spot because the ball moved on, \(w = 0.5\). Middle is \(0.5 \times 40 + 0.5 \times 200 = 20 + 100 = 120\), a mid grey that matches neither ball nor field. The sense-check is visual: slow gaps blend into sharp middles, fast gaps blend into ghosts, which is exactly when motion compensation earns its cost.

Motion compensation answers the ghost. First estimate a motion field between the neighbours, often as per-pixel shifts \((u, v)\) in pixels, then warp one or both neighbours along that field before blending. Because real movement shapes the blend, edges stay sharper than with plain blending. The price is compute and failure risk: dense motion estimation costs far more than a weighted sum, and when motion is hard to estimate near occlusions, textureless walls, or abrupt cuts, the sharp guess can land in the wrong place and look worse than a soft ghost.

Visual intuition: draw two ball positions on paper, one left and one right, with time on the horizontal axis. Plain blending draws both balls faintly at once in the middle slot. Motion compensation draws one ball halfway along the arrow from left to right. Name both axes: horizontal is time in frame slots, vertical is image position in pixels. The landmark is the arrow midpoint. The takeaway: arrows give one sharp middle, blends give two faint ends.

Slow motion suits fast linear blending because the ghost is too small to see and the compute stays tiny. Fast motion repays motion compensation because the sharp middle saves edge and tracking math downstream. The trap is paying for motion work on slow clips where no eye and no model can tell the difference, or trusting motion vectors across a scene cut where no true arrow exists.

Exam note: State the blend with symbols named, set \(w = 0.5\) for the even case, and give the working rule: linear blend for slow motion, motion compensation for fast motion where sharper middles repay added compute and added risk. The bridge from here is space: once time slots line up, frame sizes must line up too.

Mixed capture pipelines, sports archives mixing 24 and 30 frame-per-second sources, and guard networks with old and new cameras all lean on this exact ladder: duplicate for a quick slow fix, blend for a cheap smooth fix, compensate when fast edges must stay sharp.

5.1.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: When should we pick linear interpolation over motion compensation? A: Pick linear interpolation for slow motion because it is fast and good enough: the neighbour shift is small, the ghost is hidden, and one weighted sum per pixel does the job. Move to motion compensation for fast motion, where its sharper middle frames repay the added compute and added risk, since plain blends smear fast edges into double images.

5.2 Spatial Resolution Normalization by Upscaling

5.2.1 The Mixed Resolution Problem

Resolution, or the pixel width by height of each frame such as 640 by 480, sets how much detail one frame can hold. A training folder rarely arrives uniform: one clip may arrive in standard definition at 640 by 480 pixels, another at 1280 by 960 as stated in the lecture, and a third at yet another setting. Pixel counts then refuse to line up, and a shared model input becomes awkward because one batch holds small grids and the next holds large ones.

Why does size matter before any detector runs? A convolution trunk, a pooling step, and a dense tail all assume fixed grid shapes or fixed down-sample ratios. Mixed grids break batching, shift receptive fields, and bend speed math. Fixing size first lets every later layer mean the same thing on every clip.

Two broad paths were laid out. One path leans on deep models that accept mixed sizes through adaptive pooling or fully convolutional heads, but those models grow complex and harder to ship. The other path fixes frames first so every frame shares one size. That second path is the focus here: duplicate and blend pixels with care so the eye senses no break. The bias is to grow small frames up, not to shrink big frames down, because shrinking throws away detail that can never come back, while growing keeps what exists and fills the rest by rule.

On sizes: the lecture states the larger clip as 1280 by 960. Standard high-definition practice differs: 1280 by 720 is the common 16 is to 9 high-definition mark (720p), and 1920 by 1080 is full high definition, while 1280 by 960 is a 4 is to 3 frame at twice 640 by 480 in each axis. Keep the lecture's 1280 by 960 as the clip size used in the discussion; read it as a doubled 4 is to 3 frame rather than the broadcast 16 is to 9 high-definition shape. The working point stands either way: mixed grids must be rebuilt onto one shared grid.

Picture mixed phone, webcam, and broadcast clips landing in one folder. One grid is narrow, one is wide, one is tall. Resizing redraws each onto one canvas, such as 640 by 480, before training starts. The analogy to currency holds again: one shared canvas is one shared currency, and the blend rule is the exchange rate.

Scope: Frame-first sizing fits when one model must eat every clip and when detail loss from shrinking is the main fear. It assumes the upscale rule does not invent false texture that later detectors trust as real. When clips differ wildly in quality, blind upscaling can turn a tiny noisy thumbnail into a large noisy frame that wastes compute while adding no signal.

One-line recap: mixed grids block shared models, so fix size first and grow small frames up to guard detail. The next step shows the blend that does the growing.

Broadcast ingest folders, multi-phone datasets, and mixed webcam archives all face this exact fork between a size-free model and fixed-size frames.

5.2.2 How Bilinear Upscaling Works

Bilinear upscaling, or setting each new pixel from a weighted blend of its four input neighbours in two passes, is the workhorse for growing frames. Nearest-neighbour copy, or stamping the closest old value across new slots, is the cheap rival that turns smooth ramps into visible blocks.

Take one new pixel \(P\), a scalar output intensity with no fixed units (0 to 255 on an 8-bit frame). Map it back onto the small grid. Around its footprint sit four input neighbours: top-left, top-right, bottom-left, and bottom-right. In the taught grid the four neighbours named for the worked pixel were pixels 1, 2, 4, and 5 from the input grid, with pixels 1 and 2 on the top row and pixels 4 and 5 on the row below. First blend each row into one intermediate, then blend the two intermediates down the column.

First the two horizontal pairs are blended. The lecture phrase for this step is that the average of the pair gives the intermediate \(P_0\). With pixel 1 at intensity 100 and pixel 2 at intensity 150, the plain arithmetic mean is:

\[(100 + 150) / 2 = 125\]

The lecture states \(P_0\) as 130 for this pair. Keep 130 as the lecture's stated intermediate for the worked pixel; the standard mean of 100 and 150 is 125, so the 5-level gap is best read as a rounded or weighted teaching value rather than a fresh rule. Here \(P_0\) is the top-row intermediate intensity, a scalar in intensity units. In the same way a second intermediate \(P_1\) comes from pixels 4 and 5 in the row below, where \(P_1\) is the bottom-row intermediate intensity. As a concrete anchor, if pixels 4 and 5 read 110 and 170, their plain mean is \((110 + 170) / 2 = 140\), so \(P_1 = 140\) in standard arithmetic.

Then the two intermediates are blended down the column with position weights. The lecture states the blend in this form, with \(\Delta x\) as the horizontal weight and \(\Delta y\) as the vertical weight, both scalars with no units:

\[P = P_0 \Delta x + P_1 \Delta y\]

followed by averaging as described in the lecture. Keep this stated form as the lecture's working rule. The standard textbook bilinear form differs: with \(dx\) as the fractional distance across the row and \(dy\) as the fractional distance down the column, both in the range from zero to one, the standard build is:

\[\begin{aligned} P_0 &= (1 - dx) I_{11} + dx I_{21} &\text{(top row blend)} \\ P_1 &= (1 - dx) I_{12} + dx I_{22} &\text{(bottom row blend)} \\ P &= (1 - dy) P_0 + dy P_1 &\text{(column blend)} \end{aligned}\]

where \(I_{11}, I_{21}, I_{12}, I_{22}\) are the four corner intensities. The two forms agree in spirit: row blends first, then one column blend, with weights that sum to one so brightness holds. They differ in weight naming: the standard form splits each blend into \((1 - d)\) and \(d\), while the lecture form writes the pair as \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta y\) with an averaging step. When both directions matter the same, \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta y\) are each set to 0.5, and those weights act as settings chosen per job. When growth runs stronger along one axis, the weights tilt to match. Each output pixel gets its own blend, and the whole frame grows step by step.

Worked centre pixel. Inputs: pixel 1 is 100, pixel 2 is 150, pixel 4 is 110, pixel 5 is 170. Top intermediate in standard arithmetic is \((100 + 150)/2 = 125\); the lecture states 130, so carry 130 as the lecture value and 125 as the plain-mean check. Bottom intermediate is \((110 + 170)/2 = 140\). With even weights \(\Delta x = 0.5\) and \(\Delta y = 0.5\), the lecture blend gives \(P = 130 \times 0.5 + 140 \times 0.5 = 65 + 70 = 135\), then the described averaging holds the result near the middle of the four inputs. The standard textbook column blend with \(dy = 0.5\) gives \(P = 0.5 \times 125 + 0.5 \times 140 = 62.5 + 70 = 132.5\), or about 133 after rounding. Sense-check: 133 to 135 sits inside the input range from 100 to 170 and near the four-way mean of 132.5, so both paths land where a smooth blend should land, far from the blocky copy value of 100.

Simple copy would stamp the same value across new slots, the way strided steps in convolution can repeat values. That repeat looks blocky because one flat tile replaces a ramp. The weighted blend replaces the tile with a ramp, so edges step in small stairs instead of large jumps. To grow a 3 by 3 grid into a 6 by 6 grid, each small cell parents about four output cells, and each output cell blends its own four parents by its own position weights.

Visual intuition: draw the small grid with intensities as heights, like a bumpy sheet. Copying builds flat mesas with cliffs between them. Bilinear blending stretches a smooth skin over the bumps. Name the axes: horizontal is pixel column, depth is pixel row, height is intensity from 0 to 255. The landmark is the centre pixel with neighbours on all sides, which is why it teaches the general case while edge pixels are simpler cases of the same idea. Takeaway: smooth skin hides the grid, flat mesas show it.

Upscaling assumes the missing between-pixel values vary smoothly, which holds for soft shading and gentle gradients. The assumption breaks at hard edges and thin lines, where a smooth blend invents grey pixels that belong to neither side. That is why text and lane marks can look soft after a large upscale.

Name neighbours 1, 2, 4, and 5 in the worked grid, form top mean \(P_0\) and bottom mean \(P_1\), then blend with the delta weights. Standard arithmetic gives 125 for the 100 and 150 pair against the lecture's stated 130, and the standard bilinear column uses \((1 - dy)\) and \(dy\); both land near 132 to 135 and both beat plain copying. The next choice is how many neighbours to trust.

5.2.3 Neighbour Counts and Method Choice

Neighbour count, or how many input pixels shape one output pixel, sets the price of smoothness. Interpolation family, or the named rule behind the blend, sets the shape of that smoothness.

Four-neighbour mode uses the four corner pixels around the target, which is the bilinear footprint. Eight-neighbour mode adds the side pixels as well, reaching the 3 by 3 ring around the target, and many teams reach for eight neighbours when they want a closer match on curves and texture. Which count to use rests on the build at hand: four is fast and fits most jobs, eight costs more and helps where fine marks matter.

Named options beyond plain copying are nearest neighbour, bilinear, bicubic, and Lanczos. Nearest neighbour copies one closest value and runs fastest with blocky output. Bilinear blends four neighbours with linear weights and is the usual first pick because it is fast and fits most jobs. Bicubic fits a cubic surface through sixteen neighbours, so curves stay smoother at higher cost. Lanczos windows a sinc kernel over a wider ring, often 36 neighbours in a 6 by 6 footprint, and guards ringing best at the highest cost among this set. The lecture name heard in audio resolves to Lanczos, named for Cornelius Lanczos, which matches the standard resampling ladder of nearest, bilinear, bicubic, and Lanczos.

Picture a slanted roof edge. Nearest neighbour renders it as stairs. Bilinear renders it as small ramps. Bicubic renders it as a smooth slope. Lanczos renders it as a crisp slope with the least halo. The takeaway: each rung up the ladder buys smoothness with compute.

Assumption: Stronger kernels assume the extra neighbours carry true signal rather than noise. On clean photos that holds and bicubic or Lanczos win. On heavy grain that fails, and wide kernels smear noise into false texture that later detectors may trust.

Pitfalls to hold: reaching for Lanczos on every frame burns time for gains no small model can use; staying on nearest neighbour for model input leaves block edges that a detector may read as true contours; and mixing methods across a dataset without logging bends comparisons because one split looks sharper for rule reasons rather than scene reasons.

Bilinear first for speed, bicubic when bilinear falls short on curves, Lanczos when ringing control repays the cost. The bridge from here is the reverse trip: growing guards detail, while shrinking spends it.

5.2.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Where does the new pixel value come from instead of plain copying? A: It comes from the four input neighbours around it: pixels 1, 2, 4, and 5 in the worked grid. Average the top pair into \(P_0\) (lecture states 130 for the 100 and 150 pair; plain mean is 125), average the bottom pair into \(P_1\), then blend \(P_0\) and \(P_1\) with the delta-x and delta-y weights to set the new pixel.

5.3 Downscaling and Aspect Ratio Handling

5.3.1 Downscaling by Averaging

Downscaling, or rebuilding a large grid onto a smaller one, reverses the upscale trip. A 5 by 5 patch may need to become 3 by 3. The fix is to take each small block, such as each 2 by 2 area, average its values, and write that mean into the matching output cell. With four inputs \(a, b, c, d\) in one 2 by 2 block, the output is:

\[g = \frac{a + b + c + d}{4}\]

As a number: inputs 100, 140, 120, and 160 give \(g = (100 + 140 + 120 + 160)/4 = 520/4 = 130\). Do that per block across the frame and the grid shrinks while each output still speaks for its patch.

Downscaling by averaging is the spatial twin of average pooling. Max pooling keeps the brightest cell in each block and guards peaks. Average pooling keeps the block mean and guards overall tone. Pooling choice shapes how well peaks and fine marks survive: max keeps a thin bright line alive, average may wash it toward its neighbours.

Think of a mosaic made of tiles. Upscaling cuts each tile into smaller tiles by rule. Downscaling melts each group of tiles into one tile of their average colour. The analogy breaks where detail lives: melting never recovers the chip pattern once tiles merge.

Shrinking has a known cost: aliasing, or added wobble and shape distortion when fine stripes fold into false coarse patterns. A thin fence sampled too coarsely returns a wavy band that was never there. The standard guard is to soften before shrinking, so the false pattern never forms. Each job must weigh keeping detail against saving size. Upscaling guards detail; downscaling spends it to buy speed and memory.

Scope: Averaging downscale fits when the small grid only needs overall tone and coarse shape, such as a thumbnail check or a fast first pass. It breaks when thin lines, small text, or distant faces carry the task, since one mean per block erases them. In those jobs, keep the large grid or crop to the region of interest instead of shrinking the whole frame.

Visual intuition: draw input width on the horizontal axis in pixels and output width below it, with arrows from each 2 by 2 block to one cell. The shape is a funnel. The landmark is the block mean sitting between its four parents. Takeaway: funnels save space by merging, and merging spends fine marks first.

Downscale by block means, pick max pooling to guard peaks and average pooling to guard tone, and budget for aliasing whenever fine stripes shrink. The next wrinkle is shape: size can shrink evenly, but ratio changes pull on one axis only.

5.3.2 Aspect Ratio Changes

Aspect ratio, or width divided by height such as 4 is to 3 or 16 is to 9, sets the frame shape. Scaling grows or shrinks both axes together and keeps shape. Aspect work moves in one direction only, stretching or squeezing one axis while the other holds.

A common swap is 4 is to 3 into 16 is to 9. Screens and tablets lean toward 16 is to 9, while print sizes such as A4 lean toward 4 is to 3. In the worked case the width of 640 pixels stayed fixed while the height moved from 480 to 360. Check the ratios: \(640/480 = 1.333\), or 4 is to 3, and \(640/360 \approx 1.778\), or 16 is to 9. The height ratio is \(360/480 = 0.75\), so content along that axis had to give up one quarter of its extent by some rule.

Three fixes were compared. Padding, also called letterboxing, holds every pixel and fills the spare band with black, which is padding with zeros. Nothing is lost, but dark bars stay on screen. Centre cropping cuts away the rim and keeps the middle, so the frame size fits with no bars while rim content is lost. Stretching keeps all pixels and all content yet warps shapes through non-uniform scaling, so a circle slumps into an ellipse. Because no team wants to lose content or warp shapes, letterboxing is the common default, while cropping stays in mind for special tasks.

Fix Content kept? Shape kept? Cost seen on screen
Padding with black All pixels kept Shapes true Dark bars remain
Centre crop Rim pixels lost Shapes true Middle only, edges gone
Stretch All pixels kept Shapes warp, circle to ellipse Full frame, bent geometry

Pick by task. When the rim may hold clues, such as a bag at the frame edge, padding wins because nothing is cut. When the prize sits central, such as a face check-in box, cropping wins because bars waste model capacity. When geometry feeds measurement, such as size or speed from pixels, stretching loses because warped shapes bend every later measure.

Worked 640 by 480 to 640 by 360 swap. Padding: keep all 640 by 480 pixels, place them on a 640 by 360 canvas by trimming nothing and instead pad the mismatch axis with zeros; in practice the 480-tall picture on a 360-tall canvas needs 120 rows handled by bars or placement, so all scene pixels survive and black bands carry the slack. Cropping: keep the middle 360 rows out of 480 and drop 60 rows top and bottom; the frame fits with no bars but any rim clue in those 120 dropped rows is gone. Stretching: map 480 rows onto 360 rows with a vertical scale of 0.75 while width stays 1.0; a circle 100 pixels wide and 100 tall becomes 100 wide and 75 tall, an ellipse. Sense-check: only padding keeps both the 100-pixel circle and all rim pixels; the price is the bars.

Face-centred tasks assume the key object sits near the middle, so centre or smart crops that cut to the region of interest fit well there. Object detection followed by tracking uses the same idea: find the box, cut to it, drop the rest, and work at the wanted ratio. That region cut is a smart crop: size and ratio are fixed together around the prize rather than around the frame centre.

The vivid warning from the lecture sticks: stretching warps a circle into an ellipse, and that shape loss sticks in memory because every later size call inherits the bend. Never stretch when shape feeds the answer, such as face geometry, plate reading, or ball size. Never centre-crop when the prize may sit on the rim, such as a gate edge or a platform border.

Visual intuition: draw the 4 is to 3 frame as a tall box and the 16 is to 9 frame as a wide bar. Padding draws the tall box centred inside the wide bar with black wings. Cropping draws scissors across the top and bottom of the tall box. Stretching draws arrows squeezing only the vertical axis. Takeaway: wings cost screen, scissors cost content, squeeze costs truth.

Exam note: Padding keeps content with bars, cropping keeps size while rim content drops, stretching warps shapes. Tie each pick to a task such as face-centred crops, and quote the circle-to-ellipse warp as the shape warning.

5.3.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Which aspect fix keeps content, and which keeps shape? A: Padding with black keeps all content and all shapes but leaves bars. Centre cropping keeps the frame full but drops rim content. Stretching keeps content and frame size but warps shapes, so a circle reads as an ellipse.

5.4 Corrupted Frame Detection and Temporal Recovery

5.4.1 Where Corruption Comes From

Corruption, or single-frame damage with no scene cause, often traces to the link, the hardware, or the coding used in transit. A packet drops mid-frame, a sensor glitches for one readout, or a decoder mis-reads one block. That damage breaks temporal consistency, or the smooth hand-off from frame to frame: one frame jumps while its neighbours hold steady. Detection must run before analysis, or broken frames poison motion features downstream, since one wild frame can bend flow, tracking, and averaging for many steps after it.

Why check before anything else? Later stages trust neighbours. A temporal mean trusts five frames, a tracker trusts the last box, and flow trusts last-pixel matches. One corrupt frame breaks all three trusts at once. A cheap gate up front saves every expensive stage behind it.

Picture airport security trays. Every bag passes the scanner before the gate, not after boarding. Frame checks are that scanner: null tests, size tests, range tests, and motion tests run before any detector boards the frame.

Clean-first is the rule: no gaps and no wild frames enter feature math. The next step builds the gate itself.

Guard feeds with wireless hops, plant floors with vibration-prone cameras, and long street archives with mixed codecs all meet this damage, which is why the gate below is worth memorizing.

5.4.2 The Detection Algorithm

The gate is a short procedure with named inputs, named outputs, and ordered checks. Threshold, written \(\tau\) (tau, the Greek t for a limit), is the scalar cap on mean frame change tuned per task; the audio hearing of this name resolves to tau.

Inputs are the video, the expected width \(W\) and height \(H\) in pixels, and a threshold \(\tau\). Here \(\tau\) is a scalar limit on mean frame change, tuned per task. The output is a list \(C\) of damaged frame indices. The scan walks frames \(F_0\) through \(F_{N-1}\), where \(F_t\) is the frame at time step \(t\) and \(F_{t-1}\) is the prior frame, both arrays of pixel intensities.

For each frame the checks run in this order. First, read \(F_t\) and test for null or empty, meaning no values arrived; a null frame joins list \(C\). Next, test width and height against the expected \(W\) and \(H\); a size mismatch joins list \(C\). Next, test pixel values for sense. With 8-bit coding, valid intensities run 0 to 255, so a value such as 258 is out of range as stated. With normalized coding, valid values run 0 to 1, so 1.3 is out of range as stated. A frame of all one value, or any out-of-range value found by the pixel-by-pixel check, joins list \(C\).

A flat all-one-value frame deserves a note. A truly still lens cap can read near-flat, but a live scene almost never holds every pixel exactly equal, so an all-equal frame is treated as a stuck sensor or a failed decode rather than a quiet scene. That is a judgment call tuned per camera, not a law of physics.

Last comes the motion check against the prior frame. With \(N\) as the count of pixels in one frame, a scalar:

\[D = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \lvert F_{t,i} - F_{t-1,i} \rvert\]

Here \(D\) is the mean absolute gap between frames in intensity units, \(F_{t,i}\) is intensity of pixel \(i\) in the current frame, and \(F_{t-1,i}\) is intensity of the same pixel in the prior frame. When \(D\) tops \(\tau\), the frame joins list \(C\). Then \(F_t\) becomes the prior frame for the next loop round. The same family of checks sits inside common frame readers such as the OpenCV reader, which bails out when a frame fails to load.

Build the motion check in plain steps. Subtract each pixel from its twin in the prior frame. Take the absolute value so rises and falls both count. Sum across all \(N\) pixels. Divide by \(N\) so the result reads as mean change per pixel rather than a total that grows with frame size. As numbers: a 640 by 480 frame holds \(N = 307{,}200\) pixels; a summed gap of 3,072,000 gives \(D = 10\) intensity levels per pixel, a strong jump worth flagging, while a summed gap of 307,200 gives \(D = 1\), a mild shimmer.

The threshold \(\tau\) is set per job. A still hallway tolerates a low \(\tau\) such as 3 to 5 levels, since any larger jump means damage or entry. A busy street needs a higher \(\tau\) such as 10 to 20, since normal traffic already moves many pixels. Setting \(\tau\) too low flags every walker; setting it too high misses real glitches.

Visual intuition: plot frame index on the horizontal axis and \(D\) on the vertical axis in intensity levels. Ordinary motion draws low rolling hills. A corrupt frame draws a sharp spike. The horizontal line at \(\tau\) is the trip wire. Takeaway: spikes above the wire join list \(C\), hills below it pass.

Scope: The mean-gap check assumes neighbours change little, which holds for steady cameras and scattered hits. It breaks on scene cuts, flashing lights, and fast pans, where large true \(D\) values look like damage. In those jobs, gate cuts separately or pair the gap test with a cut detector so true cuts are not jailed as glitches.

Null, size, range, flat, then mean-gap against \(\tau\), in that order, with \(F_t\) becoming the prior for the next round. The worked cases below lock the pattern.

5.4.3 Worked Examples

Example 1 — null frame. Expected shape is 640 by 480. Frame 42 arrives null with no values, so the null test fires at once. It is marked damaged and its index 42 joins list \(C\). No pixel math runs because there are no pixels to test. Sense-check: a missing frame can never be a quiet scene, so early exit is safe.

Example 2 — range break. A 640 by 480 frame arrives full but one cell reads 258 in an 8-bit stream whose top is 255. Since \(258 > 255\), the range test fires. It is marked damaged and joins list \(C\). The twin case is 1.3 in a normalized stream whose top is 1; since \(1.3 > 1.0\), it is marked damaged too. Sense-check: out-of-range values cannot come from a true sensor reading, so they prove a coding or decode fault. A further spot-check: a frame reading all 128s passes range yet fails the flat test, since a live 307,200-pixel scene never holds one value everywhere.

A mean-gap number rounds out the set. Take a prior hallway frame and a current frame where one decoded block of 10,000 pixels jumps by 100 levels while the rest hold. The sum is \(10{,}000 \times 100 = 1{,}000{,}000\); with \(N = 307{,}200\), \(D \approx 3.3\). Against a hallway \(\tau = 3\), that block trips the wire. Against a street \(\tau = 12\), it passes as ordinary motion. The same \(D\) means different things per scene, which is why \(\tau\) is tuned per task.

5.4.4 Temporal Interpolation for Recovery

Temporal interpolation, or rebuilding a damaged frame from clean neighbours, works when list \(C\) holds only a few scattered hits. Call the gap \(F_{gap}\), its closest good prior frame \(F_{before}\), and its closest good later frame \(F_{after}\). All three should show small change between them, which is the temporal consistency the fix leans on. When damage runs through long runs, no clean neighbours exist and this fix cannot work.

The blend uses one weight that splits trust between past and future. With scalar weights that sum to one:

\[F_{recovered} = 0.4 F_{before} + 0.6 F_{after}\]

Here \(F_{recovered}\) is the rebuilt frame array, \(F_{before}\) is the prior good frame, and \(F_{after}\) is the later good frame. Each pixel blends as 0.4 times the before pixel plus 0.6 times the after pixel. A 0.6 weight leans toward the future frame, which matches the lecture line that the before content counts 0.4 and the after content counts 0.6.

As numbers: a before pixel of 100 and an after pixel of 150 give \(0.4 \times 100 + 0.6 \times 150 = 40 + 90 = 130\). A before pixel of 80 and an after pixel of 90 give \(0.4 \times 80 + 0.6 \times 90 = 32 + 54 = 86\). The general form with weight \(w\) on the future is \(F_{recovered} = (1 - w) F_{before} + w F_{after}\); the worked case sets \(w = 0.6\). Sense-check: \(w = 0\) returns the past, \(w = 1\) returns the future, and \(0.4 + 0.6 = 1\) so brightness holds.

Gaps with no clean future frame need other plans. Repeating the last good frame is simple duplication, but ten repeats in a row freeze the picture and jar the viewer, which is the lecture's freeze warning. Skipping damaged frames outright is another option, at the cost of a time jump that bends speed math. Learned inpainting that fills gaps from training data is the strongest option when data and compute allow. The pick rests on the task, the content, and live versus offline limits: live feeds favour duplication or skipping because the future has not arrived yet, while offline archives can wait for both neighbours and blend.

Visual intuition: draw good, gap, good on a timeline. The blend draws the middle as a point 60 percent of the way from past to future along each pixel's ramp. When gaps run long, the timeline shows a desert with no wells on one side, and no blend can cross it.

Recovery assumes small change across the gap and clean frames on both sides. The assumption breaks for long runs, fast motion across the gap, and live edges with no future frame yet. In those cases duplication freezes, skipping jumps, and only inpainting or re-capture truly helps.

Exam note: Scattered gaps rebuild as 0.4 times the before frame plus 0.6 times the after frame, leaning future. Long runs with no clean neighbours need duplication, skipping, or learned inpainting instead. The bridge from here is noise: once gaps are filled, grain still remains.

5.4.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Can damaged frames be won back when only a few are hit? A: Yes when each gap has a clean frame before and after it. Blend those two neighbours with weights such as 0.4 and 0.6 to rebuild the gap, leaning toward the future frame. Long damaged runs with no clean neighbours cannot be rebuilt this way and need duplication, skipping, or learned inpainting.

5.5 Image Denoising with Spatial and Temporal Filters

5.5.1 What Noise Means Here

Noise, or stray pixel intensity with no scene cause, covers machine faults, link faults, and random shifts. Spatial noise dots single frames, such as lone bright cells on a dark wall. Temporal noise shows as flicker, or frame-to-frame shimmer the eye catches fast, such as a street lamp band that breathes across frames. Both hurt later analysis, so removal comes before feature work: edges, corners, and flow all read stray dots as true structure when cleaning is skipped.

How does noise differ from texture? Texture repeats with a pattern, such as grass blades or brick lines, while noise breaks the pattern at random. A detector tuned for grass learns the repeat; a detector fed noise learns the breaks. Cleaning keeps the repeat and drops the breaks.

Picture an old radio. The station is the scene, the hiss is the noise, and the tuning dial is the filter. Too little filtering leaves hiss over speech. Too much filtering dulls speech along with hiss. Every filter below buys quiet with some loss of sharpness.

Noise first, features after: dots poison space math and flicker poisons time math. The next steps pick the right dial for each noise kind.

5.5.2 Linear Averaging Filters

Linear averaging, or replacing a suspect pixel with the mean of its patch, is the simplest spatial cleaner. Smoothing is the same step under its effect name, since means soften sharp jumps.

A linear spatial filter replaces a suspect pixel with the mean of its patch. The usual patch is 3 by 3, or nine pixels counting the centre. With \(g\) as the output intensity at position \((x, y)\) and \(f\) as input intensity over the nine patch cells:

\[g(x, y) = \frac{1}{9} \sum_{j=1}^{9} f_j\]

Here \(g(x, y)\) is the cleaned scalar intensity, \(f_j\) is each of the nine input intensities in the patch, and 9 is the pixel count. The \(1/9\) keeps brightness steady: nine cells in, one mean out.

Work it with numbers. Take a 3 by 3 patch reading 120, 122, 118 across the top, 121, 200, 119 across the middle, and 120, 121, 122 across the bottom, where 200 is a noise spike at the centre. The sum is \(120 + 122 + 118 + 121 + 200 + 119 + 120 + 121 + 122 = 1163\). Divide by 9: \(1163 / 9 \approx 129.2\), or about 129. The spike of 200 drops to 129, close to its honest street near 120. Sense-check: the output sits inside the input range and near the eight clean neighbours, so the spike is tamed without shifting the whole patch.

This step is also called smoothing. It trims noise but softens edges, so each use trades noise loss against detail loss. Run the same mean across a true edge, such as 50s on the left and 200s on the right, and the middle returns a mid grey near 125 that belongs to neither side. Edge-heavy jobs should think twice before leaning on plain means.

Visual intuition: plot patch position on the horizontal axis and intensity on the vertical axis. The spike is a lone tower. Averaging saws the tower down but also rounds the nearby cliff of a true edge. Takeaway: means calm towers and cliffs alike, which is both the cure and the cost.

Scope: Plain means fit mild random grain on smooth areas where edges are rare. They break on thin lines, text, and sharp rims, where the mean invents middle tones that soften every boundary. Repeated passes compound the blur, so one pass is often the limit before edges wash out.

Nine-cell means kill spikes fast and cheap, but budget the edge softening they always bring. The next filters keep edges while still cutting grain.

5.5.3 Median and Bilateral Filters

Median filtering and bilateral filtering are the edge-aware answers to plain means. Salt and pepper noise, or scattered pure black and pure white dots, is the median's home ground. Speckle and Gaussian noise with edge detail intact call for the bilateral step.

A median filter swaps the mean for the median of the same nine cells: the centre plus its eight neighbours, sorted, middle value wins. It fits salt and pepper noise well, keeps edges better than plain means, and runs fairly fast. Its weak spot is scope: other noise kinds do not bend to it.

Run the same spike patch through a median. Sorted, the nine values 118, 119, 120, 120, 121, 121, 122, 122, 200 place 121 in the middle. The output is 121, nearly the true street, with no trace of the 200. Now run a true edge patch, five 50s and four 200s, through both rules. The mean returns about 117, a false middle. The median returns 50, a true side. That is why medians guard edges: middles of sorted lists snap to one side, while means slide between sides.

Speckle and Gaussian noise with edge detail intact call for stronger tools such as the bilateral filter. It guards edges while cutting grain by weighting each neighbour twice: once by closeness in space and once by closeness in tone. A near neighbour with a far tone, such as across an edge, earns little weight, so edges survive while grain inside each side still averages away. The price is speed: two weights per neighbour plus wider footprints run slow. The pick rests on content: match the filter to the noise kind and the detail that must survive.

Filter Wins on Edge care Speed
Mean 3 by 3 Mild random grain Softens edges Fastest
Median 3 by 3 Salt and pepper dots Good, snaps to one side Fairly fast
Bilateral Speckle and Gaussian grain with detail intact Best of the three Slow

A common trap is running a median on Gaussian grain and wondering why haze remains: medians delete spikes, not haze. The mirror trap is running a bilateral on heavy salt and pepper: the white dot's far tone earns it low weight, so the dot survives. Name the noise first, then pick the tool.

Picture a white wall with black pepper dots versus a foggy window. The median flicks pepper off the wall in one pass. The bilateral wipes the fog while keeping the window frame crisp, slowly. Each tool owns one weather.

Means for speed with soft edges, median for salt and pepper dots with edge care, bilateral for grain with detail intact at low speed. Time flicker needs a different axis, which comes next.

5.5.4 Temporal Averaging with Alignment

Temporal averaging, or mean-pooling matched pixels through time, kills flicker that no single-frame filter can see. Alignment, or warping frames so matching content shares coordinates before the mean, is what makes the average honest.

Flicker dies when frames are averaged through time, but only after alignment. Take five frames \(F_{t-2}\), \(F_{t-1}\), \(F_t\), \(F_{t+1}\), \(F_{t+2}\), where the subscript marks time offset from the centre frame. First lock a still anchor, such as one fixed leaf on a tree, across all five frames. Then average each anchored pixel stack:

\[G_t = \frac{1}{5} \sum_{k=-2}^{2} F_{t+k}\]

Here \(G_t\) is the cleaned centre frame array and \(F_{t+k}\) are the five aligned frames. The \(1/5\) keeps brightness steady across the five-frame stack.

Work it with numbers. One anchored leaf pixel reads 120, 124, 118, 122, 121 across the five aligned frames. The sum is \(120 + 124 + 118 + 122 + 121 = 605\). Divide by 5: \(605/5 = 121\). Random flicker of plus or minus 3 collapses to a steady 121. Sense-check: the mean sits inside the input span and matches the centre frame's street, so tone holds while shimmer dies.

The catch is motion: moving things smear under a plain mean. A ball at column 100 in frame one and column 110 in frame five averages into a streak from 100 to 110. Motion compensation answers that by first estimating movement, often from two to five past frames, then warping frames into line before the mean. The working order is: grab frames, estimate movement, align on a still anchor, then run the temporal filter. Think of a ball tracked at \((x, y)\): at step two it sits 5 right and 5 up from step one, and the trend predicts a like shift next. That predicted shift shapes the warp so motion stays smooth with no smear. In numbers: positions \((100, 200)\), then \((105, 195)\), predict \((110, 190)\) next, and warp each frame by the reverse shift before averaging so the ball stacks onto itself.

Visual intuition: draw five transparent sheets with a tree and a ball. Pin the tree leaf through all sheets first so the trees stack exactly. Then slide each sheet so the balls stack too. Only then press them into one picture. Unpinned averaging presses first and slides never, so trees double and balls streak.

Assumption: Aligned averaging assumes the warp is right. Small camera shake and steady drift warp well. Occlusions, fast spins, and deforming shapes warp poorly, and a wrong warp smears worse than no average. When motion will not lock, shorten the stack or skip the moving zone rather than forcing five frames.

Plant-floor and street clips with shimmer gain much from aligned temporal means before any detector runs, since flicker otherwise trips every edge test once per cycle.

Exam note: Grab frames, estimate movement, align on a still anchor, then mean across five. Unaligned means smear moving parts while still parts hold, which is exactly the failure the anchor prevents.

5.5.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why must frames be aligned before temporal averaging? A: Averaging raw stacks mixes pixels that show different things, so still parts hold while moving parts smear. Locking a still anchor across frames lines up matching content first, and motion compensation then guards moving parts against blur by warping each frame into line before the mean.

5.6 Denoising with CNNs and Domain Limits

5.6.1 Noise Maps from a CNN

Residual learning, or training a net to predict the unwanted part rather than the wanted scene, drives learned denoising. A noise map, or a full-size array holding the guessed grain per pixel, is the net's output. Subtraction does the rest.

Why predict noise instead of the clean scene? Scenes vary wildly: faces, streets, sonar beds. Noise varies narrowly: dots, streaks, haze. Learning the narrow unwanted part is easier than painting every possible clean world from scratch, so the net learns the small residue and leaves the large scene to subtraction.

A learned path sends the noisy frame through a convolutional net that predicts a noise map, not a clean frame. With \(I_{noisy}\) as the input frame array, \(M_{noise}\) as the predicted noise map array of the same size, and \(I_{clean}\) as the rebuilt frame:

\[I_{clean} = I_{noisy} - M_{noise}\]

Learning the noise is easier than painting the scene from scratch. Once the map is known, one plain subtraction gives the cleaned frame. Net shape, depth, kernel sizes, kernel counts, batch steps, and ReLU choices form the design task around this idea, and the same map idea sits near the heart of diffusion-style thinking, where nets learn noise steps and then run them backward to recover shape.

Work it per pixel. A noisy pixel reads 140 while the true scene is 121, so the net should predict \(M_{noise} = 19\) there and return \(140 - 19 = 121\). A clean pixel reading 121 with no grain should draw \(M_{noise} \approx 0\) and return \(121 - 0 = 121\). Run that subtraction at every cell and the grain lifts off while true edges stay, since the map holds spikes and the scene holds structure.

Visual intuition: picture the noisy frame as a photo under tracing paper speckled with dots. The net inks only the dots onto the tracing sheet. Peeling the sheet off leaves the photo. The landmark is the one-to-one size match: map and frame share every row and column, so subtraction lines up exactly.

Scope: Map subtraction assumes grain adds onto the scene, which fits sensor and speckle-style dots well. It breaks when damage multiplies, blanks out, or moves content, such as missing blocks or motion blur; those need inpainting or deblurring heads rather than one subtraction.

One-line recap: the net learns the small noise sheet, subtraction reveals the large clean picture beneath. The same net body can serve wider ends, which comes next.

State the subtraction with all three symbols named, and stress the size match: map and frame share height and width cell for cell. That size match is the most tested fact in this section.

5.6.2 CNN Use Modes

One net body can serve three ends. The trunk is shared; the head decides the job.

For sorting whole frames, such as cat against dog, the full stack with convolution, pooling, and dense layers returns one tag per frame. Input is a full grid, output is one label. For noise or saliency maps, the net returns a full-size array: a 640 by 480 input yields a 640 by 480 map, with each cell flagged as noise or not, so input and output shapes match exactly. For feature pulls, the dense tail is cut away and only the convolution plus pooling trunk remains, so the last feature map flows straight into later stages with no sorting step, feeding a tracker or a detector instead of a label.

Mode Head after trunk Output shape for 640 by 480 input Answers
Sorting Dense plus label One tag, such as cat What is in the frame
Map Full-size map head 640 by 480 flags Where is the noise
Feature pull No head, trunk only Down-sampled feature grid What marks feed the next stage

The confusion to avoid is answering a map question with a sorting story. A tag never says where the dot sits. A map never says cat. Exam answers that swap the two lose the point even when the net body is described rightly.

Picture one kitchen mixer with three attachments: a grinder for tags, a slicer for maps, a dough hook for features. The motor is shared, the attachment sets the dish.

Cutting the dense tail for features assumes the trunk marks already separate the scene well. When the trunk is shallow or trained on the wrong field, its maps carry the wrong pact and every later stage inherits the mismatch. That field mismatch is the whole sonar story below.

One trunk, three heads: one tag for sorting, one full-size map for noise, bare trunk for features. Hold the 640 by 480 in-to-out match as the map-mode signature.

5.6.3 Worked Examples

Example 1 — pepper dots on white. A 5 by 5 frame holds black dots, or zeros, on a bright field reading near 255. The net returns a 5 by 5 map marking dot cells with high noise values and clean cells near zero, so map and frame match cell for cell. Subtraction then lifts the dots out: a dot cell at 0 with map 255 would be wrong, so the trained map instead holds about 255 minus field tone at dot cells and near 0 elsewhere, and each cleaned cell returns near field tone. Sense-check: dot cells move, clean cells hold, and output size stays 5 by 5.

Example 2 — green triangle test. A green triangle sits on white, and one stray black cell lands inside the green. Its neighbours are green, so it breaks local agreement and counts as noise. A broad black shape spanning many cells with black neighbours keeps local agreement and does not count as noise. The net must learn that split, called the neighbourhood pact: cells in one thing should agree, and a true edge is where that agreement ends. Sense-check: the lone cell goes to the map, the broad mass stays in the scene.

Example 3 — grey line test. Cells read 125, 128, 127, 126, then one cell jumps to 250, then 130, 131, 129, 127. The 250 cell breaks from its street near 128 by over 120 levels and is marked as noise. A black line running through several touching cells keeps company with like cells, so it is kept as shape, not noise. Sense-check with a quick mean: the street without the spike averages near 129, so 250 stands about 121 above its pact while true line cells sit within a few levels of their neighbours.

Together the three cases teach one rule the net must learn from field frames: lone breaks go, grouped agreement stays, whether the field is white, green, or grey.

5.6.4 Black and White Sonar Limits

Sonar, radar, and medical frames taught a hard lesson about training data. Colour ImageNet weights fail on grey sonar because the world they learned never matches this world, so each field needs its own trained net. Public sonar sets are thin, and benchmark sets may not exist at all. With only a handful of noisy frames, say five or ten, full taught training is out of reach. Semi-taught or untaught paths fit better, such as diffusion-style flows that add noise on purpose and then learn the return trip to the wanted shapes. Ways to fake sonar frames were judged not workable for this field, since fake grain misses the true sonar pact.

The neighbourhood pact guides the net: cells in one thing should agree, and a true edge is where that agreement ends. The net learns the pact from field data, not from colour photo weights. Colour nets learned daylight pact, fur grain, and sharp colour edges; sonar needs grey speckle pact, black-on-black masses, and shadow-shifted rims.

Sonar brings its own traps. Grain from speckle and salt and pepper dots litters frames. Wanted things often show black on black, so a lone dark cell may be grain while a broad dark mass is the prize. Shadows can copy an object shape and shift its rim, so size and edge calls grow thorny: the shadow looks like a second object glued to the first. The net must learn all three splits from the few field frames it holds, which is why partly taught and untaught flows beat a colour transplant.

Picture a coal pile at night. One loose coal chip on snow is noise. The whole dark pile is the prize. Its long shadow is neither. Only a watcher trained on night coal, not on daylight cats, can call all three rightly.

Assumption: Field-only training assumes the handful of frames covers the pact, which is fragile with five or ten samples. When coverage fails, the net memorizes those frames and breaks on the next deployment. Wide augmentation within the field, careful cross-checks, and untaught priors that need less data cushion the thin set.

Colour weights fail on grey sonar grain and black-on-black prizes; thin open sets push teams to field-only nets with semi-taught or untaught diffusion-style paths. The pact, not the photo prior, decides what is noise.

Q: Is the noise CNN sorting frames into tags like cat and dog? A: No. It predicts per-cell noise flags and returns a full-size map. A 640 by 480 input yields a 640 by 480 noise map, and subtraction gives the cleaned frame. Sorting tags is a separate use mode of the same net body, with a dense head returning one tag per frame.

Q: Why does a colour photo net fail on black and white sonar frames? A: It learned pact, grain, and shapes from colour photos, which do not match sonar grain and black-on-black prizes. Sonar needs its own trained net, yet open sets are thin, so teams lean on untaught or partly taught paths such as diffusion-style flows from the few frames they hold.

Body text separates the violet boxes: the first answer fixes the head confusion, the second fixes the field confusion, and the third below fixes the decision rule itself.

Q: How do we tell pepper noise from a true black shape? A: By neighbourhood pact. A lone dark cell among bright or green neighbours breaks pact and counts as noise. A dark cell ringed by dark cells keeps pact and counts as shape. The net must learn that split from field frames.

5.7 Video Editing Pipeline in Practice

5.7.1 File Properties and Containers

Container, or the wrapper around coded picture and sound such as MP4, sets how a clip ships without changing the scene inside. Bitrate, or bits per second stored, ties size to quality: more bits per second mean a bigger file and a finer picture.

A shared screen demo walked through one editing tool, Filmora, to ground the theory. First stop was file traits: the MP4 wrapper, clip length, frame width, frame height, and bitrate. The demo clip ran at 30 frames per second, and audio rate was set aside for this pass. Those six traits fix size, quality, and timing before any edit starts.

Export showed wrapper swaps in action: MP4, AVI, MKV, MOV, and the newer AV1 among them. MP4 ships near everywhere, while a job may still call for MOV or others. One demo moved a clip from MP4 to MOV with no change in scene, only in wrapper. Think of water poured from a round bottle into a square jug: the water is the coded frames, the bottle is the wrapper. Swapping bottles never changes the water.

Picture a shipping label with six lines: wrapper, length, width, height, bitrate, rate. Every editor reads that label before cutting. Missing lines cause surprises halfway through export.

Scope: Wrapper swaps are safe when the target chain accepts the new wrapper's rules. They break when a player or pipeline step assumes MP4-only features such as fast-start layout; the frames are fine but the handoff stalls. Always match the wrapper to the delivery chain, not to habit.

Hand a team MP4 masters for wide play, and mint MOV or other wraps only when the target chain calls for them.

Wrapper holds the clip, bitrate prices its quality, and the six-line label comes before any cut. Project dials come next.

5.7.2 Project Settings

Project settings, or the ratio, size, rate, and colour room chosen at edit start, lock what the timeline assumes about every clip.

Project setup asked for ratio, size, rate, and colour room. Ratios shown were 4 is to 3 and 16 is to 9 widescreen, with 16 is to 9 the pick for TV play. Sizes shown ran from standard marks through high definition and full HD at 1920 by 1080 up to 4K marks at 3840 by 2160 and 4096 by 2160. Rates shown spanned 25 to 50 frames per second, with the 30 frames per second source kept at 30 for the edit, so no rate rebuild was forced. Colour room offered standard and high definition picks, setting how colour numbers map to screen light.

Size math is worth holding: 1920 by 1080 holds \(1920 \times 1080 = 2{,}073{,}600\) pixels per frame, while 3840 by 2160 holds \(3840 \times 2160 = 8{,}294{,}400\), or exactly four times as many. Doubling each side quadruples the grid, which is why 4K timelines demand far more disk and graphics power than their sharper look suggests.

Keeping source rate at source rate avoids the whole 5.1 rebuild: 30 stays 30, so no duplication, blend, or compensation is spent. Changing 30 to 50 would force fresh middles for 20 of every 50 slots. The same logic holds for size: holding full HD avoids a 4K upscale bill.

A common trap is opening a 4K timeline for a full HD source out of habit. The tool then upscales every frame on import, pays 4K compute throughout, and exports a large file with no true 4K detail. Match the timeline to the delivery, not to the largest dial.

Ratio for shape, size for detail, rate for motion, colour room for light: lock all four to the delivery before importing. Colour and export dials then price the result.

5.7.3 Colour and Export Dials

HSL, or hue, saturation, and luminance, is the editor's colour handle. Hue names the colour family such as blue. Saturation sets colour richness from grey to vivid. Luminance reads as brightness. Encoder and bitrate then price the finished look into bytes.

Colour work centred on HSL, where luminance reads as brightness. Sliders lifted and cut brightness on a side view, pushed colour richness up and down, and tuned single hues such as blue alone without touching other families. Curves with RGB and luma gave finer hands on tone, lifting shadows or taming highlights along a drawn line rather than one global slider.

Export upscale and price. One full HD clip at 1920 by 1080 and 30 frames per second was grown to 4K at 3840 by 2160 and 50 frames per second inside the tool. Encoder picks were MPEG-4 and H.264. Bitrate told the price story: lifting the rate from 15000 to 30000 kilobits per second pushed the file from 20 megabytes toward 159 megabytes, with 80000 kilobits per second shown at the high end and a flag that thin rates harm quality. Sense-check: pixels quadruple from full HD to 4K and frames rise by \(50/30 \approx 1.67\), so raw load grows about \(4 \times 1.67 \approx 6.7\) times, near the described near-eightfold size jump once wrapper overhead joins; doubling bitrate roughly doubles file size, so the 20 toward 159 megabyte climb tracks the combined grid, rate, and bitrate lift.

Visual intuition: draw bitrate on the horizontal axis in kilobits per second and visible quality on the vertical axis. The curve climbs fast at first, then flattens: thin rates look blocky, mid rates look clean, and very high rates spend megabytes where no eye follows. The landmark is the knee where extra bits stop buying visible sharpness. Takeaway: sit at the knee, not at the ceiling.

Ship 4K at 50 frames per second only when screens and links merit the near eightfold size jump; otherwise hold full HD masters. That is both a money and a speed call: large files stall uploads, choke players, and slow every later model that must read them.

Thin bitrates harm quality first at edges and motion: text halos, grass turns to mush, and fast balls trail blocks. When a delivery must stay small, shrink the grid or the rate before starving the bitrate, since a clean small file beats a starved large one.

Exam note: Grade in HSL, shape tone with curves, encode in MPEG-4 or H.264, and price with bitrate: 15000 to 30000 kilobits per second across the full HD to 4K demo marks the size-for-quality trade every export question revisits.

5.8 From Clean Video to Task-Driven Features

5.8.1 The Bridge from Pixels to Tasks

Features, or compact marks that keep task signal and drop pixel bulk, are the bridge from clean video to answers. Once frames test clean, with no gaps and no harsh noise, the next span runs from raw pixels to task-ready features. Sample tasks: spot intrusion in a guard feed, spot a walker and guess their path for a self-driving car, catch paint flaws as cars ride a line, or flag a lone bag in an airport hall. Each task names its prize: a walker with shape and skin tones, a car body with motion quirks, or a still bag with no owner near it.

Why let the task pick the feature? A guard feed cares about crossing instants, so frame gaps win. A walker forecast cares about shape plus drift, so edges plus flow win. A paint check cares about surface tone plus stalls, so grain plus motion quirks win. One feature set never serves all four prizes well.

Indian street scenes sharpened the walker case: a walker on the kerb may stay on the kerb, cut ahead of the car, or pass behind it. The call needs both shape and drift, since shape alone cannot tell stay from cut. 5.8.2 Five Feature Families

Features split five ways. Low-level handbuilt marks, such as edges and corners from plain code with no learning, serve single frames. Spatial CNN marks come from learned backbones, owned or ready-made, and also serve single frames. Motion marks track change across frames through handbuilt paths such as path tracks, optical flow, and frame gaps. Temporal learned marks track change with nets such as RNN, LSTM, and 3D CNN forms. Joint spatial-temporal marks bind place and change when the task needs both at once, such as a walker cutting across lanes.

5.9 Low-Level Spatial Features

5.9.1 Edges in Space and Time

Edge, or the rim where one zone meets another, is the oldest shape mark. Picture a blue disc on white: the rim line is the edge, and its trace gives the shape or outline. Edge code often boils to a first-order slope test: where the slope runs high, an edge is called. Ready tools such as the Canny and Sobel detectors ship this test.

A first-order slope test scans brightness row by row and column by column, takes the difference across a few pixels, and calls an edge where that slope tops a limit. With \(I(x)\) as intensity along a row, the discrete slope \(I(x+1) - I(x)\) near 0 means flat wall, while a jump such as \(180 - 60 = 120\) means a rim. Sobel fills that slope with a small weighted kernel, and Canny chains thin peaks, drops weak strays with two limits, and links rims into outlines.

On the lecture's math naming: the lecture names the discrete Fourier transform for spatial-edge slopes. Keep that naming as the lecture's stated link, since frequency views do expose sharp rims as high-frequency energy. The standard edge-code form differs: Canny, Sobel, and Harris-style steps lean on spatial derivatives (first- and second-order slope tests shaped into gradient magnitude and tensor forms), not on a Fourier transform per pixel. Read the two as partners: derivatives find where the rim sits, frequency views explain why sharp rims carry high-frequency content. For an applied answer, show the slope test and name Canny or Sobel as the tool.

Time holds edges too. A guard camera views a gate. A shape steps in: that start rim is a temporal edge. It paces across, then steps out: that end rim is the second edge. Start plus end mark the span where the thing was present. Space edges trace outlines in pixels; time edges stamp entry and exit in frames.

Visual intuition: draw a brightness step as a cliff: flat low, sharp rise, flat high. The slope underfoot spikes at the cliff face. Canny marks only the spike crest and links crests into a line. Time edges are gate lines on a timeline: first crossing stamps entry, second stamps exit. Takeaway: cliffs in space, stamps in time.

Scope: Slope tests assume the jump comes from a true rim rather than grain. On heavy dots every spike trips the limit and rims drown in false marks. That is why smoothing or median cleaning comes before edge calls whenever grain runs high.

Rims in space from slope peaks via Canny and Sobel, spans in time from entry and exit stamps. Gap sums turn those stamps into code, next.

5.9.2 Frame Gaps Without Learning

Rim-crossing by gaps, or flagging entry from summed absolute frame differences, needs no trained net. Keep the prior frame, take the current frame, and sum the absolute gaps.

With \(F_{current}\) as now and \(F_{previous}\) as before:

\[D = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \lvert F_{current,i} - F_{previous,i} \rvert\]

Here \(D\) is the summed gap in intensity units and \(N\) is the pixel count. Averaging by \(N\) is allowed but not needed for a flag. In code the loop is plain: for each frame, subtract, take absolute values, sum into \(D\), and raise the flag when \(D\) tops the limit.

Work it with numbers. Three steady hallway frames give \(D\) near 50,000 summed levels on a 307,200-pixel grid, or under 0.2 per pixel. A walker enters and 20,000 pixels jump by 80 levels: the added sum is \(20{,}000 \times 80 = 1{,}600{,}000\), so \(D\) jumps to about 1,650,000. Against a limit of 500,000 the flag fires. Mean form would read \(1{,}650{,}000 / 307{,}200 \approx 5.4\) levels per pixel, same decision per pixel. Sense-check: still frames sit low, entry spikes high, and the limit between them separates the two regimes.

Gate-crossing trace. Frames 1 to 3: \(D\) reads 40,000, 55,000, 48,000, all below the 500,000 limit, so no flag. Frame 4 with a new shape: \(D\) reads 1,650,000, above the limit, so entry stamps at frame 4. Frame 8 when the shape leaves: \(D\) spikes again and exit stamps at frame 8. Start plus end mark the presence span from 4 to 8. Sense-check: two spikes bracket one visit, which matches the temporal-edge pair of start rim and end rim.

Set two gate lines as the trip pair, and let the gap sum mark entry and exit stamps for each crossing. In code the call is plain loops, sums, and a limit test, which is why the deeper why matters more than the call shape.

Gap sums assume a still camera and steady light. A pan, a cloud shadow, or auto-exposure swing lifts every pixel at once and trips the flag with no walker in view. Lock the camera, fix exposure, or mask to gate lines before trusting the sum.

Exam note: Show the subtraction, the absolute step, the sum into \(D\), and the flag test against the limit. That four-step chain answers every gate-crossing and span-marking question.

5.9.3 Corners for Motion

Corner, or the point where two edges meet, pins motion better than a straight edge. Right angles count, sharp triangle tips count, and fans of lines count too. Ready tools such as Harris corners find them with first and second slope tests shaped into a tensor form: flat zones show small slopes both ways, edges show one large slope, corners show two large slopes, so the tensor's two energies separate the three cases.

Why do trackers love corners? A straight edge slides along itself with no fixed grip: a horizontal rim shifted sideways looks the same. A corner locks both axes at once, so its match across frames sits at one spot. Tracking a ball over grass thus starts at corners and strong dots, not at long smooth rims.

Corners earn their keep in motion math. A ball rolling over grass reads as a zone of interest sliding over a still field. Edges plus corners pin that zone frame to frame, and with grain marks added they tell ball drift apart from camera shake: shared drift across all marks means the camera moved, lone drift of the ball marks means the ball moved.

Picture a framed photo hung by one nail versus two nails. One nail lets the frame swing along the edge. Two nails at a corner lock it. Edges are one nail, corners are two.

Corner tests assume texture enough to form two slopes. On blank walls and clear skies no corner fires and tracking starves. In those zones, widen the search, trust motion priors, or admit the gap rather than chasing false corners in grain.

Two slopes make a grip: corners lock frames where edges slide. Grain and colour add the surface words next.

5.9.4 Grain and Colour

Grain, or texture, marks surfaces by repeat pattern. Colour, or hue plus richness, marks zones by tone. Together with edges and corners they form the four space marks, all running frame by frame, with temporal edges standing apart as the time-only kin.

Grass shows one tight mix of greens; clay shows another brown mix. A football and a basketball both throw circles on screen, yet their skins read apart by grain. Two tool families lead: Local Binary Patterns, which rank each cell against its ring, and Gabor banks, which sweep Gaussian-tuned filters across angles and scales to lift surface flow. Grain marks suit face acts and surface checks, where wrinkles, weaves, and pores carry the signal.

Local Binary Patterns threshold each ring cell against the centre, read the wins as bits around the circle, and pool those codes into a histogram: uniform rings mean smooth skin, mixed rings mean rough weave. Gabor banks tile orientation and scale with Gaussian-windowed waves, so one filter fires on vertical stripes at one width while its sibling fires on diagonal stripes at another. Green grass against brown clay first hits the eye by hue, and a colour histogram, or a move into HSB-style rooms, names that hue and lets later steps key on the zone of interest by colour.

Work a tiny Local Binary Pattern. Centre 100, ring 90, 110, 95, 105, 102, 98, 108, 92. Wins against 100 read 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0 around the ring, or bits 01011010, which pool into one histogram bin for this patch type. Grass patches pile into mixed-code bins; smooth clay piles into uniform bins. Colour closes the set: hue near green flags grass, hue near brown flags clay, and saturation drops grey road pixels that no hue call should trust.

In code the call is often one line, such as the OpenCV Canny call with default dials, which is why the deeper why matters more than the call shape. These handbuilt marks also set the baseline: once their limits bite on tangled grain and acts, the payoff of learned nets turns plain.

Grain assumes close-up repeat visible at the working grid. Far fields shrink weaves below one pixel and grain calls turn to mush; colour assumes steady light, while dusk and floodlights bend every hue. Re-tune per light or normalize colour rooms before trusting hue keys.
