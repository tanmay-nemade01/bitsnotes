Video Analysis

Tracking Motion in Video

Published: 2026-09-16
Level: postgraduate
Audience: Postgraduate students in Video Analysis

6.1 Why Position and Movement Split the Driving Problem

A car drifts a few inches toward your lane on a highway. You notice it before it crosses the line. How did your brain catch that early sign from just two glances?

Your own car is marked M and the car ahead is marked O. You want to know where both cars are and where both cars will go next. That splits into two linked needs. One need is position. The other need is movement. Position tells you which lane each car sits in right now. Movement tells you whether the car ahead will stay in its lane, drift left, drift right, speed up, or slow down. You check this all the time while driving. You watch your own lane and you watch the other car at the same time. Both cars move at once, at same or mixed speeds, so the task is harder than watching one still scene.

An absolute position (a spot measured against a fixed frame corner) answers "where is it on the picture". A relative position (a spot measured against another thing, such as a car against a lane line) answers "where is it compared to me". Driving runs on relative position, because every car moves at once.

Think of three lanes on a highway. You sit in one lane and the other car sits in a nearby lane. You keep asking if that car will hold its path or cross into your lane. If it edges toward your side, you get careful. If it edges away, you relax. If it holds its path, you hold yours. Your brain predicts its next spot from its present spot and its present action. That is the exact job motion tools do with frames. One frame gives a still shot of spots. Two or more frames spaced in time give change. Change over time is movement.

A plain everyday picture helps. Think of walking beside a friend down a wide corridor with painted floor strips. One glance tells you which strip each of you stands on. That is position. Two glances a second apart tell you whether your friend is drifting toward your strip. That is movement. A single photo can never show the drift. The pair of photos can. The picture breaks down at one point: corridor strips are flat and fixed under your feet, while highway lanes tilt with perspective and both cars roll forward, so the video version must also handle depth and joint motion. The core idea still holds: still view for place, paired views over time for drift.

So the split is space against time. Space features give position. Time features give motion. Lane finding is a space job. Path prediction is a time job. You need both to drive well. You need both to track any object in video. The same split returns in every later method. Frame differencing, background models, optical flow, and path building all start from this split. Frame differencing asks "which spots changed". Background models ask "which spots break from the known back". Optical flow asks "which way did each spot shift". Path building asks "how do those shifts link into a route". Each is a time-side tool built on top of the space-side place fix.

Picture the highway view as a chart to fix this split in mind. The horizontal axis is across the road in pixels, left to right. The vertical axis is down the frame in pixels, top to bottom. In frame one, car M sits as a box centered in the middle lane and car O sits as a box inside the right lane. In frame two, the M box sits at nearly the same across-spot while the O box sits a little closer to the shared lane line. The takeaway in one line: the across-gap between the O box and the lane line shrinks from frame one to frame two, and that shrink is the movement signal sitting on top of the position read.

Assumption: this split assumes the frame rate is fast enough that a small shift means a slow drift and a large shift means a fast move. Scope: it applies to any tracked thing, from cars to balls to people. When the camera itself shakes hard or frames drop, the time read mixes camera shake with thing drift, and later steps must pay back that camera shift before the split can be trusted.

A common trap is reading movement from a single frame. A car sitting near a lane line in one still shot may be holding steady at the edge or actively crossing it. Only the frame-to-frame shift tells the two apart. A second trap is tracking only the other car and forgetting your own motion. Since both M and O move, your own lane holding is part of the same joint task.

6.1.1 The Two Questions in Every Frame

The first question is where things are now. The answer uses edges, lines, contours, and texture in one frame. Edges are sharp jumps in brightness where one area meets another. Contours are linked edge chains that bound a shape. Texture is the small repeat pattern on a face such as road grain. The second question is how things shift from frame to frame. The answer compares matched spots across time. A still object keeps the same spot in frame one and frame two. A moving object sits at a new spot in frame two. The size of the shift hints at speed. The side of the shift hints at path.

A helpful way to say it is that position is a still photo and movement is the flip-book. One page shows layout. Fast flipping shows action. Video study needs both views at once. A tracker that only finds boxes but never links them across time can count cars but cannot warn about a lane change. A differencer that only flags changed spots but never places them against lanes can sense motion but cannot say whose lane is at risk. The lecture keeps both questions live in every later section for this reason.

6.1.2 Worked Mini-Scene

Take frame one at time \(t\). Your car M sits in the middle lane. The other car O sits ahead in the right lane. Take frame two at time \(t+1\). Both cars have rolled forward. M still sits centered in its lane. O has edged a little toward the lane line. The space read at frame two says O is still in the right lane but near the edge. The time read says O shifted sideways by a small amount between frames. That small sideways shift is the early sign of a lane change. A driver who sees only one frame misses the sign. A driver who compares both frames catches it and slows or holds back from passing.

Walk the mini-scene with concrete reads. In frame one, the space fix places M at its lane middle and O well inside the right lane, so the lane-keeping verdict is "all clear". In frame two, the space fix places O close to the shared lane line but still inside, so the verdict is still "inside" yet tighter. The time fix compares the O across-spot in frame one with frame two and finds a small step toward your lane. Step one (place each car against its lane) uses space cues. Step two (subtract across-spots over time) uses time cues. Step three (act: ease off, hold speed, or delay the pass) follows from the pair. Sense-check: a single-frame view would give step one only and miss the drift, which is why the two-frame compare is the whole point.

6.1.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Are we predicting both our own path and the other car path at once?

A: Yes. You hold your lane and keep forward motion while you also watch the other car for drift, speed change, or lane crossing. Both cars move, so you track both paths and pick a safe act such as slow down, hold speed, or pass. Several students asked this in different words, and the professor's answer stayed the same: ego motion and other-car motion are one joint tracking task, not two separate ones.

Highway driving needs a space read (which lane each car sits in now) plus a time read (how each car shifts frame to frame). That space-against-time split drives every tool ahead, from differencing to flow to full paths.

Dash-cam driver aids use this split directly. They track lane lines for own position and track nearby cars for path prediction, then warn or brake. The lane tracker is the space half. The car-path predictor is the time half. The brake or warn act needs both halves to agree that risk is rising.

6.2 Spatial Position and Lane Finding

A lane line looks tilted in a dash-cam view even though it is straight on the road. How does a program still pin the car between two tilted lines?

Position work starts from picture spots. Each spot has an \(x\) across, a \(y\) down, and a frame index \(t\). The top-left corner of the frame acts as \(x = 0, y = 0\). An absolute position is the spot of a thing against that fixed corner, written as a pair \((x_{t}, y_{t})\), for example \((500, 400)\) meaning 500 steps right and 400 steps down from the corner. A relative position is the spot of a thing against another thing in the same frame, such as a car against a lane line. Driving uses relative position most. You care where the other car sits with respect to your car or with respect to lane lines, not its raw corner count.

An edge (a sharp jump in brightness where one area meets another, such as white paint against dark road) is the raw cue. A contour (a linked chain of edges that bounds a shape, such as the shut outline of a car) groups those jumps. Texture (the small repeat pattern on a face such as road grain or cloth weave) fills in the face between the bounds. Lane paint gives a strong edge. Car bodies give shut contours. Road face gives texture. Together they fix where things sit.

Lanes look odd from the driver seat. Painted lines are side by side on the road, yet in the front view they seem to meet far ahead. That is perspective. Lines tilt inward and join near the far point. A lane finder must still pull those tilted lines out of each frame and then place the car between them. Simple line or contour finding can do this base job. It is not easy in rain, glare, or worn paint, but the core idea stays plain. Find the two border lines. Check the car sits between them. Repeat each frame.

A bottle on a belt shows when absolute position fits better. The belt and camera stay fixed. The bottle spot against the frame corner is the full story. No lane or second car matters there. So absolute suits fixed rigs. Relative suits driving, where all things shift at once.

Write the brightness at a spot as \(I(x, y, t)\). Here \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) is the across spot, \(y \in \mathbb{R}\) is the down spot, \(t \in \{1, 2, \dots\}\) is the frame index, and \(I\) is the color or gray strength at that spot. The plain words behind this sign are "the color at across \(x\), down \(y\), in frame \(t\)". For color frames the value is a triplet \((R, G, B)\), with each part in \([0, 255]\). A pure green patch reads \((0, 255, 0)\). A black patch reads \((0, 0, 0)\).

Absolute position of a point \(P\) in frame \(t\) is the pair:

\[ P_{t} = (x_{t}, y_{t}) \]

The plain words are "across and down counts from the top-left corner". Relative position of O against a lane center \(C\) in the same frame is the gap:

\[ d_{t} = x^{O}_{t} - x^{C}_{t} \]

The plain words are "how far the other car sits to the side of lane middle". Here \(x^{O}_{t} \in \mathbb{R}\) is the across-spot of the other car middle at row \(y\), \(x^{C}_{t} \in \mathbb{R}\) is the across-spot of the lane middle at the same row, and \(d_{t} \in \mathbb{R}\) is the signed side gap in pixels. When \(d_{t}\) stays near zero, the car holds center. When \(d_{t}\) grows toward the lane half-width, the car nears the edge. Lane width in pixels shifts with depth because of perspective, so this test must scale with the row \(y\).

The converging perspective lanes from the lecture make this math stick. Far ahead, the two border lines sit only a few pixels apart. Near the car hood, the same two lines sit hundreds of pixels apart. So a 25-pixel side step means little far ahead and a lot nearby. That is why the gap test scales with \(y\): each row gets its own lane middle \(x^{C}_{t}\) and its own half-width, and \(d_{t}\) is read against that local width.

Picture the lane chart to lock this in. The horizontal axis is across-spot \(x\) in pixels. The vertical axis is down-spot \(y\) in pixels. Two tilted lines run from wide apart at the bottom to nearly joined at the far point near the top. A car box sits between them. A center dashed line marks \(x^{C}_{t}\) at each row, and a short double arrow marks \(d_{t}\) from that dashed line to the car middle. The takeaway in one line: position is the box against the tilted lines, and the double arrow is the relative read the driver acts on.

Assumption: the frame corner is a stable origin and \((x, y)\) addressing is shared across frames. Scope: the gap test \(d_{t}\) applies per row \(y\) and needs a fresh lane-middle estimate each frame. It breaks when lane paint is gone, hidden by snow, or washed by glare, and when heavy perspective or lens bend warps straight lines. Fixed-rig jobs can trust absolute \((x_{t}, y_{t})\); driving jobs must use relative \(d_{t}\).

A common trap is comparing raw \(x\) counts across depths. A car at \(x = 500\) near the top of the frame and a car at \(x = 500\) near the bottom sit in different world lanes because the pixel width of a lane changes with \(y\). Always pair \(x\) with its row \(y\) and its local lane middle. A second trap is treating one pixel as proof of place. Real things span many pixels, so the fix averages over the car body or lane mark, not one lucky spot.

6.2.1 Mathematical Formulation

The brightness function \(I(x, y, t)\) is the shared ground for the whole lecture. For gray frames, \(I(x, y, t) \in [0, 255]\) is one strength number. For color frames, \(I(x, y, t) = (R, G, B)\) is three numbers, each in \([0, 255]\). The indices run over the frame grid: \(x\) steps right, \(y\) steps down, \(t\) steps forward in time. All later tools (differencing, back locks, flow) read from this same stack, so learning to address it now pays off everywhere.

6.2.2 Worked Examples

Example 1: green patch addressing. Say a green triangle sits with its top-left block at \(x = 0, y = 1\) in frame one. Its color reads \(I(0, 1, 1) = (0, 255, 0)\). The same block in frame two reads \(I(0, 1, 2) = (0, 0, 0)\). The shift from green triplet to black triplet at fixed \((x, y)\) proves that the old cover has left that block. A nearby block at \(x = 2, y = 3\) flips the other way, from \((0, 0, 0)\) in frame one to \((0, 255, 0)\) in frame two. That flip proves cover has moved into the new block. This is the pixel truth behind all later differencing. Match same \((x, y)\) across \(t\). Note the color change. Sense-check: a leave-blob paired with a come-blob is the footprint of one moving cover, not two separate events.

Example 2: lane-relative read. Say the right-lane middle at row \(y = 400\) sits at \(x^{C} = 500\). The other car middle at the same row sits at \(x^{O} = 505\) in frame one and \(x^{O} = 530\) in frame two. Then \(d_{1} = 505 - 500 = 5\) pixels and \(d_{2} = 530 - 500 = 30\) pixels. The car has edged \(30 - 5 = 25\) pixels toward the line. The space read says it still lies inside a 60-pixel half-width, since \(30 < 60\), but the trend points at crossing: the gap grew six-fold in one frame step. That trend is what a driver acts on. Sense-check: \(d = 0\) would mean dead center, \(d\) near 60 would mean touching paint, so 30 means "halfway to trouble and moving outward".

6.2.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: At the start the method sees only numbers, not things or angles. When does it start calling a group of pixels a thing?

A: At level zero the method sees only spots \((x, y, t)\) plus color strength. Edges, borders, and corners get built from those numbers first. A shut chain of edges then counts as a thing. Angle and pose come much later, once the shape is firm. So the path is pixels, then edges and corners, then shut shape, then name and angle.

Place comes from \((x, y, t)\) addressing plus the relative gap \(d_{t}\) against a per-row lane middle. That gap, scaled with perspective row \(y\), is the space read every time-side tool builds on.

Relative lane reads feed dash-cam aids, while absolute corner reads feed fixed rigs such as bottle-on-belt checkers. The math is the same addressing; the choice of origin is what changes with the job.

6.3 Three Kinds of Motion in Video

A tree filmed from a walking camera slides across the frame. The tree never moved. So what actually moved?

Motion can come from the thing, the camera, or both. Case one is a still camera with moving things. A fixed shop camera watching walking people fits here. Only thing spots change. Case two is a moving camera with still things. Walking around a still tree while filming fits here. The tree does not move, yet its view shifts each frame because the view spot moves. The right word is viewpoint change. Case three is both moving at once. A side camera that pans to follow runners fits here. The runners move and the camera moves. Film sets with crane shots fit here too. Actors walk while the camera glides.

Think of filming a friend from a roundabout. Your friend stands still at the middle while you walk the rim. In your film your friend sweeps across the frame, yet your friend never took a step. The sweep is pure viewpoint change. Now have your friend walk while you keep walking: the sweep mixes their steps with yours. That mix is the both-moving case, and sorting it out is the core hard task of this section.

All three cases show up as frame-to-frame shifts. The cause differs, so the fix differs. Still-camera shifts map straight to thing motion. Moving-camera shifts mix thing motion with camera motion. Untangling that mix needs extra steps such as camera-motion pay-back, covered later. Action naming, thing finding, and thing tracking all sit on top of this base split. Tracking in a self-driving car is one use. Tracking players on a field is another. The label changes but the core step stays the same: compare frames over time and sort out what moved and why.

Think of eyes as the main sensor while driving. You look ahead, take in your lane and the cars ahead in one view, and judge in a split second if the car ahead will speed up, slow down, or turn. A brake light, a slight rightward creep, or a turn blink all feed that split-second call. Frame tools try to copy that fast judge with numbers.

Picture the three cases as small top-down maps. In case one, the camera dot stays put while people dots walk past it. In case two, the tree dot stays put while the camera dot walks an arc around it, so the bearing line swings even though the tree never moves. In case three, both dots move and the bearing line swings from both causes at once. The takeaway in one line: the same pixel shift can mean thing motion, camera motion, or a mix, so the cause must be named before the fix is picked.

Assumption: the shared test (do matched spots hold still across \(t\)?) assumes frames are matched by \((x, y)\) addressing and lighting is steady enough to match. Scope: still-camera logic applies to fixed shop, gate, and desk cameras. Moving-camera logic applies to car-mounted, hand-held, and crane shots. Both-moving logic applies to follow-cams and sports pans. Picking the wrong case sends every later tool off track.

A common trap is calling every pixel shift "thing motion" on a moving camera. A parked car rushing past a dash-cam is viewpoint change, not a speeding parked car. Always ask "did the camera move?" before blaming the thing. A second trap is the reverse: on a still camera, slow lighting drift can look like motion, so the shift still needs a limit test before it counts.

6.3.1 Formal Split

Let \(I(x, y, t)\) be the frame stack as in 6.2, with \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) across, \(y \in \mathbb{R}\) down, and \(t \in \{1, 2, \dots\}\) the frame index. A still-camera moving thing keeps camera pose fixed, so any shift in \(I\) maps to thing shift. A moving-camera still thing keeps world spots fixed, so shifts in \(I\) map to camera pose shift. Both-moving mixes the two. The shared test for all three is whether matched spots hold still across \(t\). Same spot means still. Changed spot means motion of some kind. Cause comes next.

In signs, the test is simple: compare \(I(x, y, t)\) with \(I(x, y, t+1)\) at matched \((x, y)\). A match means still along that ray. A break means something shifted, and the case label (thing, camera, or both) decides how to read the break. This is why later sections pair each time tool with its camera case: differencing and fixed backs assume case one, while flow plus camera pay-back handles cases two and three.

6.3.2 Student Questions and Answers

Q: If the camera moves, does a still thing look like it moves?

A: Yes. The view spot changes, so the thing lands on new \((x, y)\) each frame. The pixels shift even though the thing never moves in the world. You must allow for camera shift before you call it thing motion. That pay-back step returns in the pyramid and learned-flow sections.

Name the motion cause first: moving things, moving camera (viewpoint change), or both at once. The pixel shifts look alike, but the fix differs, so this label picks the right tool downstream.

Self-driving tracking and player tracking both sit on this split. The car case is mostly both-moving and needs camera pay-back. The fixed shop camera is case one and can use plain differencing. The label picks the pipeline.

6.4 Frame Differencing

A yard camera watches an empty gate for hours on battery. How does it sleep through the quiet yet wake the moment a person steps in?

Frame differencing is the plainest time test. Take two back-to-back frames. Subtract matched spots. Keep the size of the gap. Large gaps mark change zones. Small gaps mark still zones.

The plain words are "same \((x, y)\), frame one against frame two, how much did the color shift". In signs, with \(D\) for gap strength, \(I(x, y, t)\) the brightness at across \(x\), down \(y\), frame \(t\), and \(|\cdot|\) the size (absolute value):

\[ D(x, y, t) = |I(x, y, t+1) - I(x, y, t)| \]

For color, take the gap per path or the sum of path gaps, then test against a limit \(\tau\). The rule is:

\[ M(x, y, t) = 1 \text{ if } D(x, y, t) > \tau, \text{ else } 0 \]

Here \(M \in \{0, 1\}\) is a change flag, \(1\) means changed zone and \(0\) means still zone, and \(\tau \in \mathbb{R}\) is the shift limit you set. The plain words are "flag a spot as changed only when its color shift beats the limit". This limit keeps tiny light wobbles from counting as motion. A studio hand-wave test might use a small \(\tau\). A bright yard test needs a larger \(\tau\).

Think of it as a trip wire across every pixel. Each spot gets its own tiny alarm. A small rustle below the limit stays silent. A real step above the limit rings. One ringing pixel proves little, but a linked blob of ringing pixels is a visitor. That blob is what wakes the heavier finder.

What you get is a zone map, not a path arrow. It says where change sits. It does not say which way things moved, how fast, or what the thing is. It never draws the motion arrow. That is its bound. But it is fast and cheap. Each spot needs one minus and one size step. No learning, no training, no deep net. That is why it fits real-time front ends and grainy closed-circuit feeds. It acts as a trip wire. When the changed zone is large, wake the heavier finder. When the zone is small, stay in low-power watch.

Picture the output as a black-and-white flag frame. The horizontal axis is \(x\) and the vertical axis is \(y\), same as the input. White blobs mark \(M = 1\) zones where cover left or arrived. Black marks \(M = 0\) still zones. A moved triangle shows as two white blobs: a leave-blob at the old cover and a come-blob at the new cover. The takeaway in one line: two blobs mark the span of motion with no arrow between them.

Assumption: the camera stays still and light holds roughly steady between the two frames, so a large gap means cover change. Scope: best for still cameras watching thin moves against steady backs. It breaks under fast camera shake, sweeping light change, or heavy sensor grain, where gaps come from the rig or sky rather than the thing.

A common trap is setting \(\tau = 0\) to "catch everything". Light strength always wobbles and sensors add grain, so a zero limit flags the whole frame all the time. A second trap is reading the leave-blob plus come-blob as two things. The pair is one move seen twice: uncovered back at the old spot plus covered back at the new spot.

6.4.1 Mathematical Formulation

For gray frames, \(I(x, y, t) \in [0, 255]\) is the gray strength. The gap \(D(x, y, t)\) is in \([0, 255]\). Pick \(\tau\) by hand or by a self-tuning rule. Spots with \(D > \tau\) join the zone of interest. The zone of interest is the set of spots worth a closer look. One pixel proves nothing alone. A real thing covers many linked pixels, so the zone is often a blob of flagged spots.

Shot-cut finding is a kin use. When nearly the whole frame flags at once, the view itself has cut to a new shot. That global flag uses the same minus step, just summed over all \((x, y)\). So a local blob means "something moved here" while a global flash means "the shot itself changed".

6.4.2 Worked Examples

Example 1: single green block. Frame one block at \((1, 0)\) reads \((0, 255, 0)\). Frame two same block reads \((0, 0, 0)\). Per-path gaps are \(|0-0| = 0\), \(|0-255| = 255\), \(|0-0| = 0\). Sum gap is \(0 + 255 + 0 = 255\). With \(\tau = 30\), \(255 > 30\) so \(M = 1\). The block flags as changed. A block that stays \((0, 255, 0)\) in both frames gives gap \(0\) and \(M = 0\). Walk this test over each \((x, y)\) and the moved triangle lights up as two blobs: one where cover left and one where cover came. Sense-check: the gap 255 dwarfs the limit 30, so this is a firm flag, not a borderline wobble.

Example 2: block pair \((3, 2)\). Frame one reads \((0, 0, 0)\). Frame two reads \((0, 255, 0)\). Per-path gaps are \(0\), \(255\), \(0\), so the sum gap is \(255\), again above \(\tau = 30\), so it flags with \(M = 1\). Pair the leave-blob (green turned black) with the come-blob (black turned green) and you get the rough span of motion, still with no arrow. Sense-check: leave plus come is one triangle move; counting blobs as things would double-count it.

Example 3: closed-circuit night gate. The yard stays empty for hours. Set \(\tau\) to skip sensor grain, say \(\tau = 30\) on gray strengths. Frame after frame, \(D\) sits near 2 to 8 from grain, so \(M\) stays 0, and the rig stays in passive watch and saves power. A person steps in. A linked blob of spots jumps to gaps of 60 to 150, so \(M = 1\) pops up across that blob. The rig wakes the thing finder only then. Light flicker from a cloud can false-trip this wire if \(\tau\) sits too low. A small slow hand shift can be missed if \(\tau\) sits too high. That trade-off is the whole art of setting \(\tau\).

6.4.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Does the gap map give the path arrow?

A: No. It marks where color strength changed between matched spots. It never says left, right, up, or down. To get the arrow you need flow tools that solve for shift sides, covered next.

Q: Why set a limit at all? Why not flag any gap above zero?

A: Light strength always wobbles a little, and sensors add grain. A zero limit would flag the whole frame all the time. The limit \(\tau\) keeps only moves that matter. Self-tuning rules can shift \(\tau\) with scene noise, at the cost of more code. Several students pressed this point, and the answer stayed the same: the limit is what turns raw gaps into trusted motion.

6.4.4 Strengths and Limits

Good points: tiny math, fast pace, no training, works on grainy feeds, good trip wire for still cameras. Weak points: trips on light change such as sun behind clouds, misses small moves when \(\tau\) is high, gives no path arrow, fails when the camera itself shakes a lot. Real-world: low-traffic yard cameras use it to flip between passive watch and active finding.

Differencing turns two frames into a change-flag map with one minus, one size, and one limit test per spot. It is the cheapest trip wire, but it never draws the motion arrow.

A passive watch that flips to active finding only when a change blob appears is the canonical use. That flip saves power for hours and spends compute only when something new enters.

6.5 Background Subtraction

A video-chat app must cut you out from your room in every frame. It cannot ask you to hold still. How does it know which pixels are you and which are the wall?

Background subtraction suits scenes where the back stays put while the front moves. A news desk, a chat window, or a fixed gate camera all fit. First lock the back. Then each new frame is tested against that lock. Spots that match the back count as back. Spots that break from it count as front.

The back lock is called a background model, written \(B(x, y)\). The plain words are "the expected color at each spot when no front thing is there". The easy form fixes one color, such as green screen \((0, 255, 0)\) at all spots. The richer form fits a bell curve per spot, so small wobbles still count as back. The test per spot in frame \(t\) is:

\[ F(x, y, t) = 1 \text{ if } |I(x, y, t) - B(x, y)| > \tau, \text{ else } 0 \]

Here \(F \in \{0, 1\}\) is the front flag, \(1\) means front and \(0\) means back, and \(\tau\) is again the shift limit. The plain words are "call a spot front only when it stands far from its back lock". A fixed-color back may use \(\tau\) near 5 intensity steps. A bell-curve back may use \(\tau\) near 50. Self-tuning limits exist but add code weight.

Think of the back lock as a wanted poster for each pixel. Every frame, each spot shows its current color to its poster. Close match means "you belong here". Far break means "you are a visitor". The flag map of zeros and ones is called a mask. A mask is a same-size frame where 0 means back and 1 means front, for example a white hand shape on a black room. Later steps keep only mask-1 zones for finding and tracking.

6.5.1 Mathematical Formulation

Static-color back: \(B(x, y) = (0, 255, 0)\) for green screen. Bell-curve back per spot: brightness values bunch around a mean \(\mu(x, y)\) with spread \(\sigma(x, y)\). In signs, with \(\mathcal{N}\) for the bell curve:

\[ p(I(x, y)) = \mathcal{N}(I; \mu(x, y), \sigma^{2}(x, y)) \]

Here \(\mu \in \mathbb{R}\) is the mean strength, \(\sigma^{2} \in \mathbb{R}\) is the spread, and \(I\) is the seen strength. The plain words are "back colors sit near the mean, with spread set by \(\sigma\)". A spot far out on the tails counts as front.

Time index \(t\) drops out of \(B(x, y)\) for a truly fixed back. Sixty frames at 30 frames per second still show the same wall color when no front thing covers it. The \((x, y)\) stays because the wall left side is brown wood while the right side is bright white wall. Each \((x, y)\) needs its own lock value. If a teacher sits like a statue, only eye and mouth blobs flag as front. That is both a plus and a minus. It proves the lock is steady, but it also shows still front things melt into the back. Anything that holds still long enough gets absorbed into the expected back.

Picture the bell curve to fix the richer form. The horizontal axis is brightness strength. The vertical axis is how often that strength was seen during training. A hump centers at \(\mu\) with width set by \(\sigma\). Spots landing under the hump count as back. Spots landing far on the tails count as front. The takeaway in one line: the lock is a per-spot hump, not one global color.

Assumption: the back is steady over time (fixed \(B(x, y)\)) and each spot's wobble is small enough for one hump to cover it. Scope: fits news desks, chat windows, and fixed gates with controlled light. It breaks when the back moves fast, as with a car-mounted camera where curbs rush past, and when travelling shadows or mirror and glass faces throw light about. Studio lights tame shadows, but street sun does not.

A common trap is fitting the whole room with one global color. Still over time is not the same as same color over space: left wood, mid face zone, and right white wall each need their own mean. A second trap is forgetting the statue effect. A front thing that stops moving stops flagging, so background tools alone can never hold a stopped visitor.

6.5.2 Adaptive Backs for Small Shifts

Curtains in fan wind or leaves in a light breeze break a fixed lock. The fix lets the lock track slow shifts. This is called an adaptive back, written \(B(x, y, t)\). The plain words are "today back lock for this spot in this frame". A running-mean update blends old lock with new frame:

\[ B(x, y, t) = (1 - \alpha) \, B(x, y, t-1) + \alpha \, I(x, y, t) \]

Here \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) is the learn rate, a small number such as 0.01 to 0.1. The plain words are "keep most of yesterday lock, mix in a little of today frame". Small \(\alpha\) forgets slowly and stays steady. Large \(\alpha\) tracks fast but may eat slow front things into the back. This handles a breeze. It will not handle a storm. Fast or large back shifts need other tools.

Worked blend: old lock 100, new frame 120, \(\alpha = 0.1\). Then \(B = 0.9 \times 100 + 0.1 \times 120 = 90 + 12 = 102\). The lock creeps from 100 toward 120 by 2 steps in one frame. Repeat this for 30 frames and the lock glides most of the way to the new light without ever jumping. Sense-check: with \(\alpha = 0.1\), about 90 percent of the past survives each step, so a one-frame visitor barely moves the lock while a lasting light shift steadily pulls it.

6.5.3 Many-Color Backs and Mixture Models

One lock value fails when the back itself holds many faces. A room corner with dark wood, bright wall, and mid-tone door needs three locks, not one. The fix is a mixture of bell curves, short-named GMM. A GMM (a weighted sum of several bell curves that together describe a multi-faced back, such as wall plus wood plus door) gives each face its own hump. Each face gets its own bell curve with its own weight:

\[ p(I(x, y)) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_{k} \, \mathcal{N}(I; \mu_{k}(x, y), \sigma^{2}_{k}(x, y)) \]

Here \(K \in \{2, 3, \dots\}\) is the count of faces, \(w_{k} \in [0, 1]\) are mix weights with \(\sum_{k} w_{k} = 1\), \(\mu_{k}\) is the mean of face \(k\), and \(\sigma^{2}_{k}\) is its spread. The plain words are "back color is one of \(K\) usual colors, each with its own mean and spread". A three-face corner uses \(K = 3\): one curve for wall, one for wood, one for door. Texture, which is fine-grain surface pattern, pushes toward bell curves rather than fixed triplets, because texture spreads values instead of pinning one triplet.

The mixture can also track over time. Each frame nudges weights, means, and spreads. That adaptive mixture fits gate and road watch where light and small back shifts creep frame to frame. It is used for traffic watch and left-bag watch. A left bag is a new hump that was never in the lock: it flags as front and stays front, which is exactly the alarm the watch wants.

6.5.4 Worked Examples

Example 1: green-screen hand. Back lock \((0, 255, 0)\), \(\tau = 5\). A wall spot reading \((0, 253, 0)\) gives a green-path gap of \(|253 - 255| = 2\), which is below 5, so it stays back. A hand spot reading \((120, 100, 80)\) breaks far from \((0, 255, 0)\) on all three paths, so its gap dwarfs \(\tau\) and it flags front with \(F = 1\). When the hand skin tone nears the wall tone, gaps shrink and edge spots may flip wrong. That near-tie is a known hard case. Sense-check: a gap of 2 against a limit of 5 is firm back, while a gap in the hundreds is firm front; only the near-tie band around \(\tau\) is fragile.

Example 2: two-face wall plus wood. One lock of \((0, 255, 0)\) cannot fit both brown wood near \((120, 80, 40)\) and white wall near \((240, 240, 240)\). A single rule with OR logic could list both triplets for fixed colors. With breeze texture, use two bell curves instead, one per face, so \(K = 2\). Each spot tests against its local curve: wood spots against the wood hump, wall spots against the wall hump. Sense-check: one mean between brown and white would match neither face, while two humps match both.

6.5.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why does the back lock \(B(x, y)\) skip \(t\) while the frame keeps \(t\)?

A: A fixed back does not change with time. Sixty frames in two minutes show the same wall, so one lock per \((x, y)\) is enough. Time returns only when the back itself drifts, giving \(B(x, y, t)\) with a learn rate.

Q: Why not fit the whole back with one model when it stays still over time?

A: Still over time is not the same as same color over space. Left wood, mid face zone, and right white wall each need their own mean strength. One mean cannot span brown and white at once. Space needs three locks even when time needs one. That is why the mixture uses \(K = 3\) curves here.

Q: Where does this tool break?

A: It breaks when the back moves fast, as with a car-mounted camera where curbs rush past. It breaks with moving shadows that travel with the front thing. It breaks with mirror or glass faces that throw light about. Studio lights tame shadows, but street sun does not.

6.5.6 Limits Shared With Frame Differencing

Both tools flag change zones yet give no path arrow. Both need a well-set limit. Both like still cameras. Fast camera shake, harsh shadow travel, and glass or mirror faces hurt both. When the back is fully on the move, drop these tools and move to flow.

Lock the back per spot, test each frame against that lock, and upgrade the lock from one color to one hump to a mixture as the room gains faces and breeze. The mask that results feeds every finder downstream.

News desks and chat apps use fixed or mixture backs to cut the speaker from the room for blur or swap, while traffic watch and left-bag watch use adaptive mixtures that track slow back drift while flagging new front things.

6.6 Optical Flow From Brightness Hold

Differencing says "something changed here" but never says "it went left". What extra idea turns a change blob into a path arrow?

Optical flow gives what the last two tools miss: the path arrow per spot. Its result is a small arrow \((u, v)\) at each spot. Here \(u \in \mathbb{R}\) is the side shift in pixels along \(+x\) and \(v \in \mathbb{R}\) is the up-down shift in pixels along \(+y\). So \((u, v) = (3, 6)\) means 3 pixels to the good \(x\) side and 6 pixels to the good \(y\) side. A value of \((3, -2)\) means 3 pixels to the good \(x\) side and 2 pixels to the bad \(y\) side. The pair is at times written as a vector \(\mathbf{v} = (u, v)\). The size \(\sqrt{u^{2}+v^{2}}\) is speed in pixels per frame gap. The angle \(\tan^{-1}(v/u)\) is path side.

Think of a blue sticker sliding across a wall. One photo tells you the sticker is on the wall. Two photos tell you it moved. But only by matching the exact blue shade from the old spot to the new spot can you draw the arrow between them. That shade match is brightness hold, and asking the sticker's neighbors to agree on the same arrow is neighbor likeness. The analogy breaks at one point: real light shifts and shiny faces change the shade mid-move, so the hold is only safe over short steps with steady light.

Two ideas make the solve work. Idea one is brightness hold. Along a short move, a spot keeps its color strength. A blue tag that reads deep blue in frame \(t\) still reads deep blue after it shifts in frame \(t+1\), as long as light on it holds steady. In signs, with \(I\) as before:

\[ I(x, y, t) = I(x+u, y+v, t+1) \]

The plain words are "color at old spot in old frame matches color at shifted spot in new frame". All knowers here are the two \(I\) reads. The unknowns are \(u\) and \(v\). One equation with two unknowns will not fix both. That gap is called the aperture snag: peeking through a tiny hole, you can see something cross but cannot tell its full side.

Idea two is neighbor likeness. Spots near each other on the same face tend to shift together. So instead of solving one spot alone, solve a small patch at once, often \(5 \times 5\) with the spot of interest at the middle. That gives 25 equations for the same \((u, v)\). The patch is small enough that one shift fits all, yet large enough to pin both sides. A \(3 \times 3\) patch is at times too small and jumps with noise, so \(5 \times 5\) is the usual pick.

6.6.1 Mathematical Formulation

Start from brightness hold and assume the shift is small. A first-order Taylor spread gives the flow equation. With \(I_{x}\), \(I_{y}\), \(I_{t}\) for rates along \(x\), \(y\), \(t\):

\[ I_{x} \, u + I_{y} \, v + I_{t} = 0 \]

The plain words are "side rate times side shift plus down rate times down shift plus time rate sums to zero". Here \(I_{x} \in \mathbb{R}\) is how fast color shifts across \(x\), \(I_{y}\) is how fast it shifts down \(y\), and \(I_{t}\) is how fast it shifts over time at fixed \((x, y)\). All three rates are known from frame gaps. Only \(u\) and \(v\) are unknown. One row still leaves two unknowns.

Build the spread in full so no algebra is skipped. Start from the hold \(I(x+u, y+v, t+1) = I(x, y, t)\). For small \((u, v)\) and a unit time step, the first-order spread of the left side about \((x, y, t)\) is \(I(x, y, t) + I_{x} u + I_{y} v + I_{t}\). Setting that equal to the right side \(I(x, y, t)\) and cancelling \(I(x, y, t)\) on both sides leaves \(I_{x} u + I_{y} v + I_{t} = 0\). That is the flow equation: one line per spot, two unknowns per line.

Stack the rows over the \(5 \times 5\) patch. With \(N = 25\) rows, form map \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 2}\) and column \(b \in \mathbb{R}^{N}\) with \(b_{i} = -I_{t,i}\):

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} I_{x,1} & I_{y,1} \\ I_{x,2} & I_{y,2} \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ I_{x,N} & I_{y,N} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix}, \quad A \mathbf{v} + b = 0 \]

The plain words are "each patch spot gives one line that the same \((u, v)\) must meet". The least-squares fix that best meets all lines in one go is:

\[ \mathbf{v} = (A^{\top} A)^{-1} A^{\top} b \]

The plain words are "flip and solve the patch system to get the shared shift". Here \(A^{\top}A \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}\) must have an inverse. Flat patches with no texture give a weak map with no clean inverse, so the solve wobbles there. Edge lines that cut the patch also break the one-shift idea, because two faces want two shifts. That edge case needs corner-aware or edge-aware kin tools.

Why least squares? The \(N\) rows will not agree exactly because of noise, so no single \((u, v)\) meets all of them. The fix picks the \((u, v)\) that keeps the total squared break smallest, which is exactly the flip form above. In code this is a tiny \(2 \times 2\) inverse per spot, so full-frame use vectorizes well and runs fast.

Picture the patch vote as a chart. Each of the 25 rows is a line in \((u, v)\) space. With rich texture, the lines cross near one point and the vote is sharp. With a flat wall, the lines run nearly parallel and no crossing pins the point, so the solve drifts. With a straight edge cutting the patch, the lines split into two camps, one per face. The takeaway in one line: texture sharpens the vote, flatness dulls it, edges split it.

Assumption: shifts are small (a few pixels), light holds steady along the path, and all 25 patch mates share one \((u, v)\). Scope: fits slow thin moves such as hand waves, head nods, and dance steps with wide still gaps between. It breaks on fast jumps, thick all-frame motion, shifting light, and patches straddling two faces.

A common trap is trusting flow on a flat wall. With \(I_{x}\) and \(I_{y}\) near zero, \(A^{\top}A\) has no clean inverse and the arrow is noise. A second trap is reading the minus sign in \((3, -2)\) as an error. The sign is path news: it tells which way along the axis the patch went.

6.6.2 Worked Examples

Example 1: patch count. Middle spot \((x, y, t)\) gives row one: \(I(x, y, t) = I(x+u, y+v, t+1)\). Its upper mate \((x, y-1, t)\) gives row two: \(I(x, y-1, t) = I(x+u, y+v-1, t+1)\). Its left mate \((x-1, y, t)\) gives row three: \(I(x-1, y, t) = I(x+u-1, y+v, t+1)\). Keep going out to the full \(5 \times 5\) ring. Count is \(25\) rows, \(2\) unknowns. Solve with the flip form above. In code this is a tiny \(2 \times 2\) inverse per spot, so full-frame use vectorizes well and runs fast. Sense-check: 25 rows for 2 unknowns is over-pinned by design, which is what tames noise.

Example 2: reading \((u, v)\). Say the solve returns \((u, v) = (3, 6)\). That reads as 3 pixels along \(+x\) and 6 pixels along \(+y\). Speed is \(\sqrt{3^{2}+6^{2}} = \sqrt{9+36} = \sqrt{45} \approx 6.7\) pixels per frame gap. Path side is \(\tan^{-1}(6/3) = \tan^{-1}(2) \approx 63\) degrees above the \(+x\) axis. A return of \((3, -2)\) reads as 3 pixels along \(+x\) and 2 pixels back along \(y\), with speed \(\sqrt{9+4} = \sqrt{13} \approx 3.6\) pixels per gap. The minus sign is path news, not noise. It tells which way along the axis the patch went. Sense-check: a zero vector would mean still, so any firm non-zero pair is motion with a side and a speed.

6.6.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Two far spots can share the same color strength. Does that clash with brightness hold?

A: No, because the hold acts inside a small patch along the true path, not as a global color key. Neighbor likeness adds 24 more rows that must all agree on one \((u, v)\). A chance twin color far away will not meet the whole patch system, so it loses the vote.

Q: What if light on the tag shifts while it moves?

A: Then brightness hold bends. The base form assumes light holds steady along the path. When light shifts a lot, the match gaps grow and the \((u, v)\) fix drifts. Stronger kin tools that allow for light change are needed then.

6.6.4 When the Base Form Shines and When It Fails

It shines on slow, thin motion. Hand waves, head nods, and dance steps span few pixels per frame gap and solve neatly. It likes thin fields where moves sit at a few blobs with wide still gaps between. It runs fast once vectorized. It needs no training.

It fails on fast moves like race cars, where a thing jumps many pixels per gap and leaves the small-shift range. It fails on thick motion where all spots move at once. It wobbles on straight edges inside the patch, where only the cross-edge side is pinned. Real-world: studio clips and dance clips suit it. Race feeds do not.

One hold equation plus 25 patch rows turns change zones into arrows: solve the tiny least-squares system per spot, read \((u, v)\) as side plus speed, and trust it only where texture pins both axes.

The moving blue tag demo from the lecture grounds this whole section: steady light plus a textured \(5 \times 5\) patch gives a firm arrow, while flat walls and edge cuts warn that the one-shift rule has limits.

6.7 Fast Paths Pyramids and Learned Flow

A race car jumps 32 pixels between frames, far past the small-shift range. How can the same plain flow core still catch it without rewriting the math?

Three add-ons stretch base flow further. First, corner-first flow. Find good corners with a space corner finder, then solve flow only at those corners. Corners pin both \(x\) and \(y\) because they bend in two sides, unlike straight edges. This space-then-time link cuts work and lifts trust. Flat walls are skipped. Strong corners are kept.

Second, pyramid flow for fast moves. Shrink the frame to half, quarter, eighth, and at times down to one-sixteenth size. A 32-pixel jump at full size is only a 2-pixel jump at one-sixteenth size, back inside the small-shift range. Solve \((u, v)\) at the tiny size. Blow that guess up to the next size as a start guess. Warp the frame by that guess and solve the leftover small fix. Walk up step by step to full size. This coarse-to-fine warp lets a plain flow core catch moves it would miss at full size alone. Depth, which is how many shrink steps to use, depends on top speed and frame size.

Third, learned flow. FlowNet is a light learned net trained at first on fake render sets, then tuned on real frames. It is light enough for edge use but gives up some exactness. RAFT is a heavier learned net that holds up on large jumps, blocks where one thing hides behind another, shifting backs, and light shifts. It costs more compute, so it fits offline study more than edge live runs. Both are pre-trained nets that still gain from task tuning on real data, not full fresh training.

Think of reading a street sign from a moving car. From far away you catch the big letters first (coarse), then as you near you read the small print (fine). Pyramids work the same way: the tiny level catches the big jump, each larger level only fixes the small leftover. Learned nets add a second skill on top: years of past road reading (pre-training) plus a short local tune to this town's signs.

A prime learned-flow job is camera-motion pay-back, also called ego-motion pay-back. A car-mounted camera moves, so frame gaps mix camera shift with thing shift. A person on the curb may seem to bob up and down only because the car body shakes. The fix gauges camera shift and takes it out, leaving true thing motion. RAFT can serve as the offline base gauge for that camera shift. Live runs may then use a lighter tuned net once the study phase locks the design.

Exam note: study trade-offs by use case: edge live run against offline study, light tuned FlowNet against heavy RAFT, exactness against compute load. The picker is the scene facts plus the power cap, not brand loyalty.

6.7.1 Mathematical Formulation

Pyramid levels index \(l = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) with \(l = 0\) full size. Level \(l\) scale is \(1/2^{l}\). So \(l = 4\) is \(1/16\) size. Let \(\mathbf{v}^{l} = (u^{l}, v^{l})\) be the flow at level \(l\). The update up the stack is:

\[ \mathbf{v}^{l} = 2 \, \mathrm{up}(\mathbf{v}^{l+1}) + \Delta \mathbf{v}^{l} \]

The plain words are "double the coarse guess, then add the small leftover fix at this size". Here \(\mathrm{up}(\cdot)\) blows the coarse field up to this size and \(\Delta \mathbf{v}^{l}\) is the small fix solved by warped matching at level \(l\). Corner-first flow uses the same base solve but only at corner spots \((x_{c}, y_{c})\) where both \(I_{x}\) and \(I_{y}\) vary strongly.

Walk one climb to see why the factor 2 appears. A jump of 2 pixels at level \(l+1\) spans twice as many pixels at level \(l\), because level \(l\) is twice as wide. So the coarse arrow must double on the way up before the small leftover is added. Each level then only solves a few pixels of leftover, staying inside the small-shift range the base core trusts.

Picture the stack as a set of nested frames. The bottom (coarsest) frame is tiny and blurry but the car jump is short. Arrows there are short and firm. Each step up doubles those arrows and sharpens them with a small fix. The top (full-size) frame holds the full 32-pixel arrow built from safe small steps. The takeaway in one line: big jumps are climbed as doubled guesses plus small fixes.

Assumption: the coarse guess points near the true match, warping removes most of the jump, and corners or texture exist to pin the fix. Scope: pyramids rescue fast but rigid shifts; learned nets rescue blocks, shifting backs, and light shifts too. Both still assume the scene keeps enough matchable structure level to level.

A common trap is treating pre-trained weights as finished. Pre-trained weights come from mixed or fake sets, so a short tune on real task frames closes the gap, and skipping it leaves bias on real roads and real rooms. A second trap is shipping RAFT to a tight edge box. RAFT fits offline study and base-truth runs; edge live runs favor lighter tuned nets.

6.7.2 Worked Examples

Example 1: race-car jump. Full-size jump is 32 pixels right, so \((u, v) = (32, 0)\). Base one-level solve fails. At \(1/16\) size the jump is \(32/16 = 2\) pixels, which solves well. Blow up step by step: \(2 \times 2 = 4\) at \(1/8\) plus a small fix, \(4 \times 2 = 8\) at \(1/4\) plus a fix, \(8 \times 2 = 16\) at \(1/2\) plus a fix, \(16 \times 2 = 32\) at full size plus a final fix. Each level only solves a few pixels of leftover, staying in range. Sense-check: four doublings turn 2 into 32, so the climb exactly rebuilds the jump the base core could never take in one bite.

Example 2: model pick. Offline traffic study with time to spare picks RAFT for best arrows through blocks and glare. Edge live box with tight power picks FlowNet plus task tuning, trading a little exactness for speed. Real-world: offline path mining uses the heavy net as base truth while the live car uses the light tuned net. Sense-check: the offline run can afford seconds per frame for exactness, while the live box must keep up with the road at frame rate on a power cap.

6.7.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Does learned flow still need tuning if it ships pre-trained?

A: Yes. Pre-trained weights come from mixed or fake sets. A short tune on real task frames closes the gap. The tune is small next to full training, but skipping it leaves bias on real roads and real rooms.

Q: High-pitched breaks in sound blocked parts of the talk. Was RAFT picked for live edge runs?

A: No. RAFT fits offline study and base-truth runs because it costs more compute. Edge live runs favor lighter tuned nets. The pick hinges on use case, power cap, speed need, and exactness need.

Catch fast jumps with pyramids (shrink, solve small, double up with fixes), spend compute wisely with FlowNet on edge and RAFT offline, and always close with a short task tune plus camera pay-back where the rig moves.

Offline path mining uses the heavy net as base truth while the live car uses the light tuned net, and dash-cam study pipelines use heavy offline flow as the base gauge for camera-motion pay-back before shipping lighter tuned nets for live runs.

6.8 Paths Smoothing and Spatio-Temporal Join

Two arrows tell you two ticks of motion. When does a pile of ticks become the story of a ball flight or a car slowdown?

Two-frame arrows do not yet make a path. A path links arrows over many frames into one track. Say thing middle at frame \(t\) is \(P_{t} = (x_{t}, y_{t})\). Add each frame arrow in turn:

\[ P_{t+1} = P_{t} + (u_{t}, v_{t}), \quad P_{t+2} = P_{t+1} + (u_{t+1}, v_{t+1}), \dots \]

The plain words are "walk the dot forward by each frame arrow to draw the full route". Link ten or thirty frames and the route of a ball, player, or car appears. Speed along the route is gap length over frame time. A ball-track use, a player-run use, and a car-speed use all need this multi-frame link. Two frames alone give a tick. Many frames give the story.

Think of footprints on a beach. One print tells you someone stood there. A line of prints tells you where they walked, how fast, and where they turned. Smoothing is the tide brushing light wind marks away while keeping the true turn at the rocks. The analogy breaks at one point: footprints sit still once made, while video dots jitter from sensor noise and missed finds, so the smoothing pass must trim jitter without erasing true kinks such as a ball bounce.

Flow is one route source. Other route sources skip flow and read rendering of face tilts instead. HOG builds counts of tilt sides over cells. A HOG code (a stack of tilt-side counts per cell that describes face shape, such as a player outline) fires on shape. MBH builds counts of tilt sides of the flow field bounds, which stresses moving edges while muting flat camera drift. An MBH code (tilt-side counts of flow borders that stress moving edges and wash out steady camera drift) fires on motion borders. Both run over the full frame span, not just one pair, then link peaks into routes. Raw routes then get a smoothing pass that trims jitter while keeping true turns. Only after smoothing do higher tasks such as act spotting, odd-event flagging, or thing tracking run.

Space and time cannot be split at the end. A ball spot at a time needs both: where on the frame plus when in the run. That joint read is called spatio-temporal. Motion cues weigh more than still cues in video, because still layout alone cannot tell a kick from a hold. But still cues still matter for shape and place. The strong systems marry both.

Real-world: ball-track tools link per-frame ball dots into flight arcs. Third-official review tools zoom from full view down to pixel crops, a pyramid-like drill-down, plus learned checks, to rule on close calls.

6.8.1 Mathematical Formulation

Dense-route build in signs: given starts \(P_{0} = (x_{0}, y_{0})\) and arrows \((u_{t}, v_{t})\) for \(t = 0 \dots T-1\), the route is the sum walk:

\[ P_{T} = P_{0} + \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} (u_{t}, v_{t}) \]

The plain words are "start dot plus all frame ticks gives end dot". HOG-style counts bin tilt side \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(I_{y}/I_{x})\) into side bins per cell, then stack cells into a face code. Here \(\theta\) is the edge tilt side at a spot, \(I_{x}\) and \(I_{y}\) are the across and down brightness rates, and each cell votes its pixels into a few side bins. MBH takes side counts of flow-bound strength instead, so steady camera drift washes out while true moving edges pop.

Picture the route chart to fix this. The horizontal axis is frame time \(t\). The vertical axis is along-track spot. Raw dots jitter up and down around the true arc. The smoothed line runs through the middle, keeping the bounce kink but trimming the buzz. The takeaway in one line: link first, smooth second, decide third.

Assumption: arrows chain head-to-tail with matched identity (the same ball, same car) and frame gaps are even in time. Scope: fits ball arcs, player runs, and car-speed reads over tens of frames. It breaks when blocks hide the thing (one thing hides behind another), glass or mirror faces fake the arrows, or identity swaps between nearby things.

A common trap is running act spotting on raw unsmoothed dots. Jitter then reads as fake turns and fake speed bursts. Smooth first while keeping true kinks, then measure. A second trap is trusting still layout alone for actions. A kick and a hold can share one pose; only the time link tells them apart.

6.8.2 Worked Examples

Example 1: cricket-ball arc. Frames at 30 per second give 10 frames over one-third second, since \(10/30 = 1/3\). Dots \((x_{t}, y_{t})\) linked in order trace the arc. Gaps between dots divided by \(1/30\) second give speed per tick: a 6-pixel gap means \(6/(1/30) = 180\) pixels per second. Smoothing trims sensor jitter but keeps the bounce kink. Sense-check: the arc should bend smoothly between bounces and kink only at the bounce, so any kink elsewhere is leftover noise.

Example 2: car-speed read. Link car-middle dots over 30 frames (one second at 30 frames per second). End minus start in pixels, scaled by road calibration to meters, gives meters per second. Say the pixel run is 150 pixels and calibration is 0.2 meters per pixel at that depth: distance is \(150 \times 0.2 = 30\) meters in one second, so speed is 30 meters per second. A shrinking gap series means the car ahead slows, so the ego car should brake. Sense-check: steady gaps mean steady speed, growing gaps mean speeding up, shrinking gaps mean slowing down.

6.8.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Can ball tracks seen in cricket coverage be tied to this topic?

A: Yes. Ball dots joined over frames are motion routes. That is the same link-many-arrows idea, just with a ball finder feeding dots instead of raw flow feeding arrows. IPL ball tracking is the stadium-scale version of the same sum walk.

Q: Third-official reviews zoom from full view to pixel crops. Is that like pyramids?

A: In spirit yes. Both move from coarse wide views to fine pixel views to lock a close call. Reviews add human rule checks and learned checks on top, plus at times decision-policy tuning beyond the taught classical and deep route tools. The official starts wide for context, then drills to pixels for the edge call.

6.8.4 Pick Rules and Open Hard Cases

Pick by scene facts. Light shift breaks fixed locks, so add adaptive or light-steady flow. Blocks where one thing hides behind another break single-layer links, so allow multi-hypothesis or heavier nets. Large jumps break one-level flow, so use pyramids or learned nets. Mirror or glass faces break brightness hold, so mask those faces or use shape cues. Thin moves need only thin arrows, written sparse. Full-frame swirls need thick arrows, written dense. Power cap picks light nets for live runs and heavy nets for offline study. Sun flicker, glass faces, and large jumps are the open hard cases that guide these picks.

Link ticks into routes with the sum walk, smooth the jitter while keeping true turns, and marry space shape (HOG) with time borders (MBH). That spatio-temporal join is what higher tasks actually decide on.

Ball-track arcs, player runs, and car-speed braking calls all run on this join, and review-room zooms from full view to pixel crops close the loop from coarse context to fine ruling.

Key Industry Applications

Self-driving stacks use lane-line finding for ego place plus flow and routes for nearby-car paths, then brake, hold, or pass. The space half holds the lane. The time half predicts the neighbor. The act follows the pair.

Closed-circuit yard cameras use frame differencing as a low-power trip wire that wakes heavier finders only on change blobs. Hours of quiet cost almost nothing. One blob spends the compute.

News desks and chat apps use fixed or mixture backs to cut the speaker from the room for blur or swap. The mask is the product: sharp on the speaker edge, steady through small light drift.

Traffic watch and left-bag watch use adaptive mixtures that track slow back drift while flagging new front things. The drift is learned. The new bag never was in the lock, so it stays flagged.

Sports coverage uses dot linking for ball arcs and player runs, plus coarse-to-fine review zooms for close calls. The arc is the sum walk. The zoom is the pyramid spirit with human rules on top.

Dash-cam study pipelines use heavy offline flow as base truth for camera-motion pay-back, then ship lighter tuned nets for live runs. Study pays for exactness once. The car pays for speed every frame.

Dance-clip and studio-clip tools use base flow for slow thin moves with no training cost. Small shifts, steady light, and textured patches are all the core needs.
