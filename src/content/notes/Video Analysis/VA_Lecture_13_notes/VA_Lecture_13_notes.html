13.1 What Counts as an Anomaly in Video

13.1.1 The working definition

An anomaly (something that breaks the expected pattern of a place) is the starting question of this whole lecture: what should the camera treat as strange enough to flag?

Everyone knows the gut feeling. People file through the gate for entry into a secured building, one after another, and then one person climbs over the fence instead; that entry feels odd at once. Nobody needs a rulebook to feel that the fence-climb is odd. The lecture starts exactly there and then turns the gut feeling into an engineering definition.

Video anomaly detection (automatic spotting of unusual patterns inside a video sequence) means teaching a machine to feel that same oddness, frame by frame, without a human watching every screen. The input is a video clip, the output is a yes-or-no call: normal or worth flagging.

Two words carry the full weight of the definition: deviate and significantly. A clip counts as anomalous when it deviates significantly from normal behavior. Normal behavior (the expected movement pattern in a given setting) is things like how pedestrians walk on a footpath, how shoppers drift across a shop floor, or how vehicles flow along marked lanes. The model learns that normal pattern first. Then any behavior that moves far enough away from it gets flagged.

A plain-language restatement used in the session runs like this: learn how traffic normally flows, and treat a vehicle driving against that flow as a deviation worth flagging. That sentence matters because every scoring rule later in the lecture is just a formal version of it. The professor keeps returning to it whenever a new formula threatens to hide the idea.

A deviation score (a number that says how far a clip sits from the learned normal pattern), written \(s(x)\), is computed for each clip \(x\). Let \(\mathcal{N}\) stand for the learned model of normal behavior, and let \(\tau\) stand for the tolerance threshold, the cutoff the designer fixes. Then the decision rule is to flag the clip when the score sits above the threshold:

\[ \text{anomaly}(x) = 1 \quad \text{if} \quad s(x) > \tau, \quad \text{else} \quad 0 \]

Here \(1\) means anomalous and \(0\) means normal. Learning \(\mathcal{N}\), designing \(s(x)\), and choosing \(\tau\) is the entire subject in one line.

Think of it like a school corridor monitor. The monitor first learns the normal hum: students walking on the left, at walking pace, between classes. Then each new passer-by earns a mental strangeness score, and only a score past some tolerance, say sprinting down the middle screaming, triggers a shout. The analogy breaks at one point: a human monitor adjusts tolerance without thinking, while our system needs \(\tau\) written down as a number, which is why the rest of the lecture obsesses over thresholds.

Picture the score axis as a number line from zero upward. Most everyday clips pile up near zero, forming a tall hump. A vertical cutoff line at \(\tau\) splits the axis: everything left of it is routine, everything right of it is flagged. Moving the line left catches more events but cries wolf more often; moving it right stays quiet but risks missing the fence-climber. That single sliding line is the business end of the whole definition.

13.1.2 Normal behavior and the tolerance question

Defining normal behavior is only half the job. The other half is saying how much straying the system will tolerate, and this is where real deployments live or die.

Take the missed-turn case from the session. A vehicle rolls a short distance in reverse because the driver missed a left turn, then turns left and drives on. Suppose the reverse stretch is about 3 meters over 2 seconds at roughly 5 km/h, while true wrong-way driving in the same lane runs 50 meters or more at full speed. Both have a nonzero deviation score, say \(s(x) = 0.25\) for the brief reversal against \(s(x) = 0.9\) for the sustained wrong-way run. With \(\tau = 0.5\), the first passes as normal and the second fires. The brief reversal stays normal and the sustained run is flagged. Sense-check: a threshold that flagged every 2-second correction would drown operators in false alarms.

The same question appears on a shop floor. Suppose shoppers are expected to walk clockwise around the displays. A shopper who walks anticlockwise has broken the expected pattern. Is that a violation worth flagging, or just someone who forgot something at the entrance? Another case: the expected pattern is that every item goes into the shopping cart. An item that goes into a personal bag instead has left the expected path. But then the designer must define what counts as a cart and what counts as a bag before any flag can fire, including the tricky middle cases like a store-branded shopping bag sitting inside the cart.

So the working recipe is simple to state and hard to tune. Learn the normal behavior for the setting. Fix a tolerance level for small departures. Treat any departure past that tolerance as an anomaly. The tolerance \(\tau\) absorbs all the designer judgment: how strict is this place, how costly is a false alarm, how costly is a miss.

Scope: the decision rule only means something once \(\mathcal{N}\) and \(\tau\) are tied to one setting. Assumption: clips used to learn \(\mathcal{N}\) genuinely represent the routine of that place. When the training footage mixes two places, say a footpath and a service road, one tolerance cannot serve both, and the rule misfires in both directions.

A common beginner trap is reading \(s(x) > \tau\) as a law of nature. It is a design choice. Two deployments watching the same video with different \(\tau\) values will disagree, and both can be right for their own cost model. A second trap is forgetting that \(s(x)\) inherits every blind spot of \(\mathcal{N}\): if the normal model never saw night footage, every night clip scores high for the wrong reason.

Picture the reverse-driving example as a speed-versus-time sketch: the horizontal axis is seconds, the vertical axis is signed speed along the lane. Normal flow is a steady band near plus 30 km/h. The missed-turn event dips briefly to minus 5 km/h for two seconds and returns. A sustained wrong-way event holds at minus 30 km/h for a full minute. The takeaway in one line: duration and size together decide strangeness, not direction alone.

Everyday life already runs this rule. Airport security lanes tolerate a slow shuffle but flag a sprint back through the exit. Libraries tolerate whispering but flag loud calls. Highway cameras tolerate a brief wobble inside a lane but flag a U-turn. Video anomaly detection just writes the same human judgment as \(s(x) > \tau\).

Recap: an anomaly is a significant deviation from learned normal behavior, decided by the rule \(s(x) > \tau\). The handoff: the next section shows where that one-line rule earns its keep, from surveillance rooms to factory floors.

13.1.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: How do we formally define abnormal instead of just calling it odd?

A: Define normal behavior for the setting first, such as the expected walk of a pedestrian or the expected flow of traffic, then learn it as \(\mathcal{N}\), score each new clip by its departure \(s(x)\), and call it anomalous when \(s(x) > \tau\). The model trains to learn exactly that normal pattern, so abnormal is whatever strays significantly past the tolerated amount. The question seemed worth asking because odd is a feeling while the score-plus-threshold turns it into something a machine can compute.

13.2 Where Anomaly Detection Gets Used

13.2.1 Safety, quality, and medical monitoring

Why build any of this machinery at all? Because in four kinds of places, a camera that cries wolf only when it matters saves labor, product, health, or lives.

Anomaly detection earns its place anywhere a deviation can be caught on camera and acted upon. The session groups the uses into four broad areas, and each one maps back to the \(s(x) > \tau\) rule with a different cost for getting it wrong.

Safety and security comes first. Surveillance cameras watch public and private spaces around the clock. A system that flags suspicious behavior on its own lets a small team cover far more ground. Anything that strays from the expected pattern, say loitering beside a gate at 3 a.m. or a bag left behind on a platform, draws attention at once. The score here is strangeness of movement or presence, and the threshold is set so officers review dozens of clips per shift, not thousands.

Quality control comes second. On an assembly line, cameras record products, machines, and the process itself. A product with a visible fault, a machine arm moving in the wrong arc, or a step done out of order all count as deviations from the normal run. Flagging them early keeps bad units from moving ahead. Here the normal model is unusually crisp because the line repeats the same motion thousands of times a day, so even a small deviation score means something.

Medical diagnosis comes third. Cameras can track the expected activity of patients, and anything that breaks the pattern points at trouble. Monitoring of senior citizens is the running example through the session. The camera learns the expected slow movement of the person being watched, say shuffling between bedroom, kitchen, and armchair at roughly 0.5 m/s, and a sudden change such as a rapid drop to the floor calls for help. The threshold choice is deliberately sensitive because a missed fall costs far more than a false visit.

Risk prediction is the fourth use. Traffic flow is the example here. The system does not stop the accident by itself. It flags the risky movement, a halted car in a live lane, a vehicle crawling at 10 km/h in 80 km/h flow, so people can step in. Traffic control rooms already review flagged clips from highway cameras before sending response teams, which is exactly the human-in-the-loop pattern the lecture keeps recommending.

The four areas differ in what \(\mathcal{N}\) looks like and who pays for errors: security pays in guard hours, factories pay in scrapped units, medical monitoring pays in missed emergencies, and traffic pays in response delay. The same scoring rule serves all four, but each setting retunes \(\tau\) to its own costs.

Think of it like smoke detectors across buildings. A kitchen detector tolerates cooking haze, a server-room detector tolerates nothing, and a warehouse detector sits somewhere between. Same sensor idea, different tolerance per room. The analogy breaks in one way: smoke is one signal, while video normal behavior is thousands of motion patterns that must each be learned.

13.2.2 The printed circuit board story

A student dissertation project shows what deployment looks like in practice. The boards under test are the familiar green printed circuit boards with thin golden conducting lines drawn across them by machine. A board counts as normal when every golden line has the right width and shape, say a trace designed at 0.2 mm coming out at 0.2 mm plus or minus a hair. A line drawn too thin, say 0.1 mm, or broken clean through means the board is faulty and must be rejected.

Earlier, human quality checkers inspected each board by eye and managed about 30 to 50 boards a day, roughly 40 on average. The automated image-based system tested about three times that number, roughly 120 to 150 boards a day, about 135 on average. Its raw accuracy sat near 60 to 70 percent, call it 65 percent, with a fair number of false positives and false negatives mixed in, and no F1 score was quoted.

Work the numbers: at 135 boards a day and 65 percent correct, about 88 boards are decided correctly by the machine and need no second look, while about 47 doubtful or flagged boards go back to humans. Before automation, humans stared at all 135. After it, they stare at 47, roughly a third of the load, while throughput triples from 40 to 135. Throughput roughly tripled while human effort fell to about a third. Sense-check: a 65 percent screener is useless as a final judge but excellent as a first filter, which is exactly how it was used.

The discussion drew out why that lower accuracy still paid off. The true positives and true negatives, about 60 percent of the load, needed no second look. Human checkers only had to re-examine the flagged and doubtful cases. So human effort dropped sharply while throughput tripled. The checkers kept their jobs and spent their time on the hard calls instead of routine passes. Electronics factories pair fast automated screening with human review of rejects for exactly this reason. The same session noted the latency of that student system was quite high, so speed still needed work before it could sit on a live line.

Picture a two-bin sorting sketch: the horizontal axis is the board batch, the vertical split is the machine verdict. The big pass bin flows straight to shipping. The small review bin loops back to a human bench with a magnifier. The takeaway: automation clears the easy majority and humans spend their hours only on the small hard pile.

13.2.3 Business view of accuracy

The session makes a deliberate point about not chasing perfect scores. Getting a very high F1 score on anomaly detection is risky and costly. It can demand a very deep model plus heavy customization, weeks of tuning for the last few percentage points. Most businesses prefer a different deal: accept a moderate score and keep a human in the loop for the doubtful clips.

An F1 score (the balance of precision and recall in one number), written as a value between 0 and 1 such as \(0.65\), climbs steeply in cost past the moderate range. Each extra point can demand disproportionately more data, deeper models, and fiddlier tuning, while a human reviewer clears the remaining doubt in seconds per clip.

The numbers behind this view are simple. Normal behavior covers about 90 percent of the footage, and the odd 10 percent is where human review adds value. The goal of automation is to clear away the routine 90 percent so people spend their time on the rest. Put concretely: out of 1,000 clips, the machine quietly passes about 900 routine ones and a person studies the remaining 100, instead of a person watching all 1,000.

Scope: the moderate-score deal holds where a human review step exists and false alarms are cheap to dismiss. Assumption: misses are not catastrophic. Where a miss means an undetected fall or a highway pile-up, the business accepts higher modeling cost and a lower threshold, because the price of silence dwarfs the price of extra review.

A common trap is reading 65 percent accuracy as failure. Against a final-exam standard it is failure; against a triage standard it is a large win, provided the 35 percent uncertain pile actually gets reviewed rather than auto-passed. A second trap is assuming the human loop is free: reviewer fatigue, shift length, and queue latency must be budgeted, or the flagged pile sits unwatched.

Hospital triage desks work the same way. A nurse at the door does not give a final diagnosis; the nurse sorts the waiting room into routine and urgent so doctors spend minutes where they matter. Anomaly detection at 60 to 70 percent is that nurse, not the surgeon.

Exam note: expect discussion-type questions on why a moderate score plus human review beats an expensive push for a perfect F1 score. Recap: anomaly detection pays in four domains, and the PCB story proves a modest screener plus human review triples throughput. The handoff: the next section explains why even that modest screener is hard to build.

13.3 Why the Problem Is Hard

13.3.1 Rare events and thin training data

What if the event you most need to catch is the one you have almost no footage of? That is the daily reality of anomaly detection.

Models learn from examples, and anomalies are rare by nature. A fall caught after three months of room surveillance gives almost no training clips for that event, perhaps one genuine fall against tens of thousands of hours of sitting, walking, and sleeping. From person to person, both the kind and the level of abnormality change. A fall detector would need clips of leaning left and falling, leaning right and falling, falling backward, falling forward, and falling sideways, and the available video holds far too few of each. Five directions times several body types times several room layouts quickly demands hundreds of fall clips that simply do not exist.

So the model must learn mostly from normal clips and still catch events it has barely seen. This single fact pushes the whole field toward one-class and unsupervised setups: learn the shape of normal very well, and treat departures as alarms, because the anomalous class can never be sampled properly.

Think of it like training a lifeguard who has watched ten thousand safe swims and only one real rescue. The lifeguard cannot learn rescues from one example; the lifeguard learns what safe swimming looks like so precisely that anything else draws the eye. The analogy breaks where video is harder: the lifeguard brings a lifetime of human priors, while our model brings none.

13.3.2 The meaning of normal shifts with context

Normal (the expected pattern for a fixed group in a fixed place) is not a fixed label. It changes with the group and the setting being described, so every definition must fix its granularity first: normal for whom, and normal where.

Senior citizens move slowly, and within a senior-monitoring setup that slow walk, roughly 0.4 to 0.7 m/s with short shuffling steps, is the normal pattern to learn. But set that same slow walk against a broad population of teenagers, middle-aged adults, and toddlers, where typical corridor speed runs 1.0 to 1.5 m/s, and the slow walk itself looks like an outlier. Without the framing sentence, the same movement reads as normal in one setup and anomalous in another, and no threshold can fix a framing error.

A concrete pair makes it stick. Clip A: an 80-year-old crossing a living room at 0.5 m/s. In the senior-monitoring model this scores near zero and passes. Clip B: the identical 0.5 m/s shuffle on a school running track during practice. Against the track model it scores high and flags. Same pixels of motion, opposite verdicts, because \(\mathcal{N}\) changed.

Picture two overlapping bell curves on a speed axis: the senior curve centered near 0.5 m/s, the general-population curve centered near 1.2 m/s. A measurement at 0.5 sits at the peak of one and deep in the tail of the other. The takeaway: the score has no meaning until the reference population is named.

13.3.3 Time makes it harder

Anomalous events do not share one fixed length, and video forces the model to judge across time. A fall is a short event, over in under a second: standing at frame 1, tilting at frame 10, floor contact near frame 20. Shoplifting is a long event, built from small moves spread over many minutes: enter, browse, circle back, glance, pocket, exit. Some setups must judge a clip against the last few frames, while others must judge it against weeks of history, such as a full month of shop footage. That long-range link between frames is hard to model because memory, compute, and spurious correlations all grow with window length.

At the same time, detection must be fast. A fall system should raise the alarm even before the person hits the floor, say within 300 to 500 milliseconds of the tilt onset, so someone nearby can step in and soften the harm. A shop system should flag the suspect before that person walks out the door, not from overnight batch review. Suppose a fall detector waits for a clean 2-second window: at 25 frames per second that is 50 frames of delay, and the person has already hit the floor. Low latency plus long memory is the heavy pair of demands. Sense-check: shortening the window cuts delay but blinds the model to slow-building patterns like shoplifting.

Picture a timeline with two markers: the event onset and the must-alarm point. For falls the gap is a fraction of a second; for shoplifting the gap is minutes but the evidence is diffuse. The takeaway: there is no single right window, so deployments often run a fast short-window scorer for abrupt events alongside a slow long-window scorer for brewing ones.

Real deployments feel this daily. Elder-care cameras that alarm only after impact arrive too late to help. Highway incident systems that need a full minute of stopped-vehicle evidence delay the tow truck by that same minute. Video anomaly teams pair frame-level motion cues such as optical flow with sequence memory for exactly this reason.

13.3.4 Annotation is painful

Labels are scarce because the definition itself is shaky. When even people disagree on what counts as normal, say whether lingering near a shelf for 40 seconds is browsing or casing, asking annotators to mark thousands of clips turns into a slow and costly chore. Each clip needs a start time, an end time, and a category, and the boundary seconds are genuinely ambiguous. Multiply by tens of thousands of clips and the budget breaks.

Scope: supervised training is only viable where the anomaly vocabulary is small and crisply defined, such as cracked versus intact boards. Assumption: annotators share one written normal-behavior spec. When the spec is missing, label noise swamps the rare anomaly class first, because there are too few anomaly labels to average the noise away.

A common trap is budgeting annotation per clip as if clips were still photos. Video labels cost multiples more: the annotator must watch, rewind, mark boundaries, and often debate the call. Teams that plan for image-label prices run out of budget a tenth of the way through.

Recap: four forces make the problem hard, which is why the field leans on normal-only training: rare events, shifting normal, time pressure, and painful labels. The handoff: the next section sharpens the vocabulary so anomaly, novelty, and outlier stop being blurred together.

13.4 Anomaly, Novelty, and Outlier

13.4.1 Three ideas that get mixed up

Three words sound alike in the corridor and mean different things in the lab. Mixing them up sends the wrong clips to the wrong pipeline.

The session separates three terms that people often blur together. An anomaly (a significant deviation from the learned normal behavior) is the thing the system must flag, like a vehicle driving against traffic flow. A novelty (something new that may later turn normal) is a pattern the model has never seen that could become routine, like the first wave of online classes. An outlier (a purely statistical departure with no real meaning) is sensor junk, such as a single frame with heavy noise or a sudden jump in brightness against neighboring frames.

The three differ in response, not just definition. An anomaly triggers an alert. A novelty triggers model updating, because the line of normal itself may have moved. An outlier triggers cleaning: averaging, pooling, or dropping the frame before scoring begins.

The work-from-home story makes novelty concrete. Before the pandemic, online weekend classes and working from home were rare treats, and an institute running online classes looked novel. After the pandemic, both became routine. The underlying distribution of behavior shifted, and what once looked novel turned into the new normal. City offices now hand out work-from-home days to ease road traffic, which shows how fully the once-strange pattern settled in. So a novelty detector must allow for the line itself to move over time, either by retraining \(\mathcal{N}\) or by maintaining a novelty buffer that graduates into normal after enough sightings.

Outliers in video look different and demand no such respect. A brightness spike from auto-exposure hunting, a compression glitch, a raindrop on the lens: each deviates statistically from its neighbors and means nothing about behavior. Most pipelines treat them as sensor junk and smooth single-frame spikes with temporal averaging or pooling before any anomaly score is computed, so the scorer never sees them.

A side-by-side contrast pins it down.

Lens Anomaly Novelty Outlier
Meaning Real behavior breaking the pattern New behavior that may become routine Meaningless statistical spike
Example Fence-climb entry, wrong-way car Work-from-home in 2019 versus 2022 One over-bright noisy frame
System action Flag and alert Watch, then absorb into normal Clean or drop pre-scoring
Time behavior Stays flaggable Decays into normal Isolated, no trend

When to pick which: if the clip shows meaningful behavior departing from \(\mathcal{N}\), call it anomaly; if it shows a coherent new behavior recurring across days, treat it as novelty and retrain; if it is a lone-frame spike with no behavioral content, treat it as outlier and filter it.

Think of it like a school attendance register. An anomaly is a student vaulting the fence. A novelty is the first year the school allows remote Fridays, strange at first, routine by term two. An outlier is a smudged ink mark on one row. Only the first calls the guard; the second rewrites the rulebook; the third just needs an eraser. The analogy breaks where video novelty is gradual: unlike a dated rule change, behavior drift has no memo, so the system must detect the drift itself.

A frequent trap is flagging every outlier as an anomaly, which floods operators with lightning glitches and exposure jumps. The reverse trap is dismissing a genuine novelty as noise: the first week of a new store layout looks strange every hour, and a system without a novelty path will alarm until someone retrains it.

Picture the work-from-home shift as two humps on a timeline: the office-presence hump tall in 2019 and short by 2021, the remote-work hump short in 2019 and tall by 2021. The crossing point is where the novelty graduated. The takeaway: normal is a moving target with a date stamp.

Recap: anomaly means flag it, novelty means possibly relearn it, outlier means clean it. The handoff: the next section sorts anomalies themselves into three flavors by the size of the pattern that carries them.

13.4.2 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Is an outlier the same thing as an anomaly?

A: No. An outlier is a purely statistical departure such as a noisy or over-bright frame, while an anomaly is a meaningful deviation from normal behavior. That is why outlier frames are often cleaned away with averaging or pooling instead of being flagged as events. The confusion is natural because both sit far from the bulk of the data, but only the anomaly carries behavioral meaning worth an alert.

13.5 Three Flavors of Video Anomaly

13.5.1 Point anomaly: one frame stands out

What if the whole story of strangeness fits inside a single frame? Then detection is cheap, fast, and usually not even worth an alarm.

A point anomaly (a single frame that breaks away from the rest) is the smallest flavor. A lone noisy frame or a frame flashing with too much light is the standard example. Detection is straightforward because the unit of analysis is one frame at a time. A simple frame-by-frame comparison, even a histogram matched against the previous few frames, is enough: compute the histogram distance between frame \(t\) and frames \(t-1\) through \(t-3\), and a spike at \(t\) marks the point. Latency stays low, so live use is practical.

The catch is that most such frames are sensor junk, so pipelines usually label them outliers and smooth them out rather than raising an event alarm. A median filter across three frames, or average pooling over a short window, erases the spike before the behavioral scorer runs. Genuine single-frame behavioral anomalies are rare precisely because behavior takes time.

Think of it like one misprinted page in a long novel. The eye catches it at once, and the fix is to reprint the page, not to rewrite the plot. The analogy breaks only where the misprint matters: a single frame capturing a muzzle flash or a crash instant is behaviorally decisive, and those are the rare point anomalies worth keeping.

13.5.2 Contextual anomaly: the setting decides

A contextual anomaly (a normal-looking action in the wrong setting) flips meaning when the backdrop changes. Running on a track is fine; running inside a reading hall breaks the expected pattern of that room. A car rolling in reverse or someone talking aloud where silence is the rule fits the same mold.

Scene understanding is what makes this flavor tricky. A bare background check is not enough. Running inside a hospital, a staple of screen dramas, reads as acceptable urgency, while running inside a reading hall reads as a violation. So the detector must grasp what the scene expects, hold a memory of past frame sequences, and compare the current sequence against them. Detection here compares a sequence of frames with other sequences, not one frame with the next: a chunk of \(T\) frames, say 3 seconds at 25 fps, is encoded as one unit and matched against the library of normal chunks for that scene.

This is the flavor most deployments actually want to catch: the event that should not happen, happening. Wrong-way driving, loitering at a gate, shouting in a silent hall: each is ordinary motion somewhere, anomalous here. So the model conditions on context explicitly, learning one \(\mathcal{N}\) per scene or feeding a scene label alongside the clip, because a single global normal cannot serve a hospital corridor and a running track at once.

Picture a two-panel sketch. Left panel: a track, horizontal axis distance, runners as streaks, one sprinting figure blends in. Right panel: a reading hall, desks in rows, the same sprinting figure stabs out as a lone streak across stillness. Axes identical, verdict opposite. The takeaway in one line: the scene, not the motion alone, decides.

Think of it like dress codes. A swimsuit at the pool is unremarkable; the same swimsuit in a boardroom is an incident. The cloth did not change, the room did. The analogy breaks where video is stricter: dress codes are written down, while scene expectations must be learned from footage, including the tricky hospital-versus-reading-hall distinction.

13.5.3 Collective anomaly: the pattern is the problem

A collective anomaly (pieces innocent alone but guilty together) is the hardest flavor. Each frame seems fine and the setting matches, yet the assembled pattern drifts off course.

Someone strolling in circles, or a shopper who keeps circling back to the same shelf, glancing left and right again and again, gives the idea. One glance sideways is ordinary shopping. A long chain of left-right-left-right glances, say 12 glances across 4 returns to one shelf in 6 minutes, paired with repeated returns to one spot starts to look like shoplifting. No single frame crosses \(\tau\); the pooled pattern across the whole window does.

This flavor is the hardest to catch because the window of judgment must be large. The detector has to hold many frames or even many small events together before the deviation shows itself. Point anomalies need one frame, contextual anomalies need one sequence, and collective anomalies need a whole pattern of sequences. Memory, alignment, and counting all enter: where was the person at minute 1 versus minute 6, how many glances, how many returns.

Score it concretely. Give each return-to-shelf plus glance-pair a small sub-score of \(0.15\). One visit scores \(0.15\) and passes under \(\tau = 0.5\). Four visits pool to \(0.60\) and fire. No single visit alarms, but the run of four does. Sense-check: a genuine shopper comparing two products makes two visits and stays under the line, while the circling pattern crosses it.

Scope: collective detection only works with windows long enough to contain the pattern, minutes for shoplifting, weeks for fraud drift. Assumption: the sub-events are reliably detected first. When glance or return detection is noisy, the pooled score inherits that noise multiplied by the count, so the front-end tracker must be solid.

Picture an event-timeline strip: time along the horizontal, shelf visits as ticks, glances as dots. Normal shopping shows two or three scattered ticks. The suspect strip shows dense repeating tick-dot clusters. The takeaway: rhythm, not any single mark, convicts.

13.5.4 A classroom exchange on collective behavior

Q: Point and contextual anomalies are clear, but can you give one more everyday example of a collective anomaly?

A: Take a comic whose act leans on strong language show after show: one harsh word means little, but the repeated reliance on that language across the whole act forms the pattern, and whether it counts as normal depends on the audience that chose that comic. Several students pressed on this example because the line felt thin, which shows how thin and setting-dependent the dividing line is: the same act is house style to one crowd and a violation to another. The professor kept the trigger and the resolution together: the doubt was about where pattern becomes violation, and the answer is that the audience and venue, the context, fix that line.

Q: So comedians get grouped by the language they use?

A: Not exactly grouping: the point is that for that act the repeated language is the house style, so judging it needs the full setting and domain knowledge rather than a bare boundary line. A classifier that clusters comics by word counts would miss the point; the judgment needs who booked the act, for whom, and where. This follow-up looked similar to the first but asked a genuinely different thing, how to compute the call, and the answer redirects from clustering to context.

A common trap is hunting collective anomalies with a point-anomaly window: scoring frame by frame guarantees the pattern never accumulates enough evidence. The opposite trap is stretching the window so far that everything looks suspicious. Match the window to the behavior: seconds for sprints, minutes for circling, shows for house style.

Recap: one frame, one setting, one pattern: point, contextual, collective, in rising order of window and difficulty. The handoff: the next section turns the taxonomy into a trainable setup with boundaries, phases, and a smile test.

13.6 How the Learning Problem Is Set Up

13.6.1 Classification with the boundary around normal

How do you train a classifier when one class barely exists? You draw the boundary around the class you have.

Anomaly detection can be framed as classification where the boundary is drawn around normal data. Picture normal clips as a cloud of points in some feature space, each point one clip embedded as a vector, with a hyperplane or curve wrapped around them. Anything that lands inside counts as normal, and anything that falls outside counts as anomalous. Training on normal data fixes that boundary, and testing checks each new clip against it. In symbols this is the same \(s(x) > \tau\) rule: \(s(x)\) is the distance from the clip point to the normal cloud, and \(\tau\) is the skin thickness of the wrapper.

The snag is data. Supervised training wants large piles of marked normal and abnormal clips, and those marked abnormal clips rarely exist. When a few abnormal examples are available, such as a car halting abruptly, a vehicle moving against the flow, or a sudden swerve from a mechanical fault, semi-supervised learning can use them as guides around a mainly normal training set: the guides pin down where the boundary should bulge. When even those guides are missing, the setup turns fully unsupervised: gather clips assumed to be normal, learn their distribution, and treat whatever departs from it as worth flagging.

A compact comparison helps.

Setup Training data Boundary source When used
Supervised Many normal plus many abnormal labels Both classes Rarely, labels missing
Semi-supervised Many normal plus a few abnormal guides Normal cloud nudged by guides Some known anomaly types
Unsupervised Normal clips only Normal distribution alone Default in this lecture

When to pick which: use supervised only where anomaly labels are plentiful, which in video they almost never are; use semi-supervised where a shortlist of anomaly types is known; default to unsupervised normal-only learning everywhere else.

Think of it like fencing a sheep pasture in open hills. You know where the sheep graze; you do not map the whole wilderness. So you fence the meadow tightly and treat anything outside the fence as worth a look. The analogy breaks where the meadow drifts: sheep pastures stay put, while normal behavior drifts with seasons and layouts, so the fence needs periodic re-surveying.

13.6.2 The two-phase unsupervised recipe

The unsupervised recipe from the session runs in two phases. In phase one, the model trains on normal clips only and learns their distribution or pattern: feature extraction plus training produces a trained model of normality \(\mathcal{N}\). In phase two, new clips that may be normal or abnormal pass through that model, and the departure of each new clip from the learned pattern becomes its anomaly score \(s(x)\), so no separate scoring module is needed. A tolerance threshold \(\tau\) on that score gives the final call, exactly the rule from 13.1.2.

Walk it as a procedure, since this is the recipe every later method instantiates.

Purpose: learn normal so well that strangeness scores itself.

Inputs and outputs: in goes a pile of normal clips plus a threshold-choice policy; out comes a trained \(\mathcal{N}\) and a scorer \(s(x)\) with cutoff \(\tau\).

Steps: first, extract features from each normal clip, such as motion, shape, or learned embeddings. Next, fit the normal distribution or boundary, by density, distance, or reconstruction. Then, for each test clip, compute its departure \(s(x)\). Finally, flag when \(s(x) > \tau\).

Complexity and cost: training touches only normal data, so it scales with routine footage volume; testing is one forward pass plus a comparison per clip, cheap enough for live scoring.

When to use and alternatives: use this recipe whenever anomaly labels are missing, which is the default; reach for semi-supervised guides only where a few anomaly types are known and labeled.

Picture the pipeline as a left-to-right flow: normal clips into a feature box, into a training box labeled \(\mathcal{N}\), then a split where fresh clips enter the same \(\mathcal{N}\) box and exit with score tags, then a diamond gate at \(\tau\) sorting pass versus flag. The takeaway: one model, two phases, no anomaly labels required.

13.6.3 The smile test as a worked example

Face-based stroke screening gives the idea a concrete face. In the smile test, people are asked to smile, and a healthy smile moves both sides of the lips symmetrically about the nose line. A stroke-affected smile turns asymmetric: one side lifts while the other stays flat. The session asks us to assume the camera captures the face well enough to read that symmetry, frontal view, even light, full mouth visible.

Training uses only healthy smiles, say around 10,000 short clips of under 30 seconds each, with many different people smiling at the camera. That is roughly 80 hours of smiling, all normal. The model learns how normal smiles look: the joint distribution of left-lift and right-lift, the symmetry axis, the natural wobble. At test time, fresh smiles arrive from healthy people and from stroke-affected people without labels. Each new smile earns a score from its departure from the learned pattern.

Score it with numbers. Measure left corner lift \(L\) and right corner lift \(R\) in millimeters from rest. Define asymmetry \(a = |L - R|\). Healthy training smiles show \(a\) near 0 to 1 mm, say mean 0.4 mm. A test smile with \(L = 8\) mm and \(R = 7.4\) mm gives \(a = 0.6\) mm and passes. A stroke-affected smile with \(L = 8\) mm and \(R = 1\) mm gives \(a = 7\) mm, far past a threshold of \(\tau = 2\) mm, and flags. The symmetric smile passes and the asymmetric one flags. Sense-check: a 7 mm side-to-side gap is visible across the room, so the flag matches human judgment.

Tolerance matters here too. A tiny smile where one lip corner twitches a bit more than the other, say \(a = 1.2\) mm, can still be healthy. The threshold must sit high enough, such as \(\tau = 2\) mm, to let such small natural asymmetries pass. Bedside screening tools set their cutoffs on large healthy cohorts for the same reason: calibrate on thousands of healthy faces before judging a new one.

The same recipe covers any normal pattern: a walking style, a traffic flow, a machine rhythm. Train on the normal version, learn its shape, and score each newcomer by its departure. Substitute gait period for lip lift or lane offset for asymmetry and the pipeline is unchanged.

Visualize the symmetry check as a face with a vertical nose-line axis, two arrows from mouth corners upward, and a double-headed gap arrow between their tips labeled \(a\). The takeaway: symmetry about the midline is the whole feature.

13.6.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: How is this deviation score different from plain outlier detection, since both study departures from a normal distribution?

A: The machinery overlaps, but here the departure is used directly as the anomaly call, so the threshold must be set with domain knowledge to swallow harmless outliers. The smile test asks for broad smiles and stroke cases show large departures, so \(\tau\) sits above the small natural wobble; highway traffic likewise sets its cutoff so only large meaningful departures count. The question is sharp because the math looks identical, and the answer is that the decision policy, not the formula, makes it anomaly detection: outlier pipelines filter the spike, anomaly pipelines flag on it.

Q: What if a test video is something else entirely, such as a person simply not smiling at all?

A: That clip scores a large departure, a neutral mouth sits far from every trained smile, and gets flagged as anomalous, and a later manual review pass sorts true cases from junk. A concrete number: the neutral face gives \(L = R = 0\) with a shape mismatch that dwarfs even stroke asymmetry, so \(s(x)\) lands deep in flag territory. That review pass is the standard post-processing step after the automatic flag, the same human-in-the-loop deal from the PCB story. The question matters because open-world test feeds always contain off-task clips, and the recipe handles them by flagging first and sorting after.

A common trap is demanding the scorer reject off-task clips silently. It cannot: a non-smile is genuinely far from smile-normal, so flagging is correct behavior and the review queue is the right destination. A second trap is tuning \(\tau\) onDuplicates of training smiles only; the cutoff must be validated on held-out healthy variety plus known stroke cases, or natural wobble triggers false alarms.

Recap: fence the normal cloud, score departures, threshold with domain sense, review the flags; the smile numbers show each step. The handoff: the next section fills the fence-building toolbox with classical, non-neural methods.

13.7 Classical Ways to Model Normal Behavior

13.7.1 Statistical models and the bell curve

Before any neural network enters, what can plain statistics already do? Enough to serve as every later baseline.

Statistical methods learn the distribution of normal behavior with no neural machinery at all, using tools such as histograms and mixture models. The bell curve picture guides the call. Learn the center and the spread of normal data, then treat points far out in the tails as anomalous or outlying.

Let \(\mu\) (mu) stand for the mean of the normal data, the center of the bell. Let \(\sigma\) (sigma) stand for its standard deviation, the typical spread around the center, so learning the model means learning these two numbers. The session uses the plus-minus-three-sigma gate: data inside the band counts as normal, and data outside it counts as outlying:

\[ \text{normal band} = [\mu - 3\sigma, \mu + 3\sigma] \]

New data earns a probability score under the learned curve, and that score doubles as the anomaly score \(s(x)\). Under a Gaussian curve this band holds about 99.7 percent of normal data, so only about 3 clips in 1,000 stray outside by chance.

Work it with numbers. Suppose normal walking speed in a corridor is \(\mu = 1.2\) m/s with \(\sigma = 0.2\) m/s. The band is \([1.2 - 0.6, 1.2 + 0.6] = [0.6, 1.8]\) m/s. A pedestrian at 1.3 m/s passes. A sprint at 3.5 m/s lands far outside and flags. A second feature works the same way: each axis gets its own band, and breaching any of them raises the score.

A Gaussian mixture model, or GMM, serves as the baseline in this family: it fits one bell curve per foreground pattern, such as a halted vehicle, a vehicle moving against the flow, or a vehicle crawling far below normal speed, all learned from traffic footage. Each component has its own \(\mu_k\) and \(\sigma_k\) plus a mixing weight, so multi-modal normal behavior, fast lane plus slow lane, gets one hump per mode instead of one stretched bell.

Think of it like grading on a curve where the teacher first finds the class average and typical spread, then questions any score three spreads away. The analogy breaks where video features are high-dimensional and correlated: one-dimensional bands per axis miss diagonal structure that a full mixture captures.

Picture the bell with center tick at \(\mu\) and shaded tails beyond \(\mu \pm 3\sigma\) in red. The horizontal axis is the feature, say speed in m/s; the vertical is density. The takeaway: almost everything routine sits in the white middle, and the red tails are the flag zone.

13.7.2 Distance-based models

Distance methods skip probabilities and work with closeness instead. A simple nearest-neighbor scheme trains on normal clips, which gather into one big cluster. Each new clip earns the distance to its nearest normal neighbor as its score.

Let \(x\) stand for a test clip embedded as a feature vector, and let \(\{x_i\}\) be the stored normal clips. Let \(d(\cdot, \cdot)\) stand for the distance in feature space, such as Euclidean distance. Then:

\[ s(x) = \min_{i} d(x, x_i) \]

A small \(s(x)\) means a familiar clip with a close neighbor, and a large \(s(x)\) means a stranger worth flagging. Comparing \(s(x) > \tau\) recovers the lecture master rule with distance as the score.

Concretely: a test clip 0.1 units from its nearest normal neighbor passes; one 2.5 units away with \(\tau = 1.0\) flags. The price is storage and search over all normal exemplars at test time, which is why deployments compress the normal set into cluster centers.

Trajectory analysis is the video-flavored version. Optical flow extracts the path of each moving object, meaning the frame-by-frame displacement field that traces where each pixel region travels, and all paths get clustered so the clusters stand for normal motion. A path that wanders away from every cluster breaks the expected flow and gets flagged. Standard clustering tools such as k-means or hierarchical grouping do the grouping step: k-means fixes \(k\) cluster centers and assigns each path to the nearest, while hierarchical grouping builds a merge tree cut at a chosen height.

Think of it like a library where every normal clip is a book on a shelf and a newcomer is shelved beside its closest match. A book on swimming shelved in the swimming aisle passes; one that fits no aisle is set aside as strange. The analogy breaks at scale: real libraries have aisles planned in advance, while the clusters must be discovered from the footage, including the painful choice of how many aisles exist.

13.7.3 Thresholds and the headaches they bring

Every classical baseline ends at the same gate: pick the cutoff above which a score means anomaly. Three routes were named. An ROC curve, meaning the true-alarm rate plotted against the false-alarm rate as \(\tau\) sweeps, can trade true alarms against false alarms by picking the knee of the curve. The plus-minus-three-sigma band can serve directly as the cutoff. Or the cutoff can move over time, tracking recent scores and adjusting itself as lighting or crowd density drifts.

Read an ROC choice with numbers. Suppose sweeping \(\tau\) gives operating points \((\text{false-alarm}, \text{true-alarm})\) of \((0.30, 0.98)\) at \(\tau = 0.3\), \((0.08, 0.90)\) at \(\tau = 0.6\), and \((0.01, 0.55)\) at \(\tau = 0.9\). The middle point keeps 90 percent of true catches while cutting false alarms from 30 to 8 percent. The knee near \(\tau = 0.6\) is the practical pick. Sense-check: pushing to \(\tau = 0.9\) nearly silences false alarms but loses almost half the real events.

Each route brings its own chore. Clustering needs the number of groups \(k\) fixed in advance, the dendrogram cut chosen for hierarchical grouping, and the distance \(d\) defined. Pick \(k\) too small and two behaviors blur into one blob; too large and one behavior shatters into slivers that flag each other. The cutoff itself needs its own study on held-out data, because a \(\tau\) tuned on training scores is optimistic.

Scope: ROC-based cutoffs need labeled anomaly examples to compute the true-alarm axis; without them only the sigma band or a percentile of normal scores is available. Assumption: the validation footage matches deployment conditions. When seasons, cameras, or layouts change, a frozen \(\tau\) drifts out of calibration and must be re-picked.

A common trap is treating threshold selection as a five-minute afterthought. In practice it is a separate study: split data, sweep \(\tau\), plot the trade, price false alarms against misses, and document the pick. Teams that skip this ship models whose accuracy numbers mean nothing at runtime.

Exam note: threshold selection methods and the pain of choosing group counts and cutoffs are favorite discussion prompts. Recap: statistics fencing with sigma bands plus distance-to-normal scoring covers the classical toolbox, and every path ends at a carefully priced threshold. The handoff: the next section rebuilds the same scoring idea with deep networks that learn their own features.

13.8 Autoencoders for Anomaly Detection

13.8.1 The encoder and the latent code

What if the model could learn its own features and its own scorer in one network? The autoencoder does exactly that by squeezing video down and rebuilding it.

An autoencoder is a deep network that squeezes a large input down to a small code and then rebuilds the input from that code. The input here is a video chunk: frames \(F_1, F_2,\) up to \(F_T\), each of height \(H\), width \(W\), and \(C\) color channels. The full input size \(T \times H \times W \times C\) runs very large, on the order of a million numbers, and the network compresses all of it into a short vector of \(D\) numbers with \(D\) far smaller. A concrete shape: \(T = 16\) frames of \(128 \times 128 \times 3\) is about 786,000 numbers in, squeezed to \(D = 1{,}024\) numbers, a compression of roughly 770 to 1.

A homely analogy from the session: calling a full course just mathematical squeezes everything into one word. Someone else might call it tough or concept-heavy. Each short label is a representation: not a category, just a compact stand-in for the whole thing. The encoder does the same for video, except its label is a list of numbers learned to preserve rebuildable content rather than a word chosen by a student.

Let \(x\) (the input clip) stand for a vector in the large \(N\)-dimensional space, where \(N = T \times H \times W \times C\). Let \(z\) (the compact code, also called the embedding or latent vector) stand for a vector in the small \(D\)-dimensional space with \(D\) far smaller than \(N\). Let \(F_{\theta}\) stand for the encoder mapping with parameters \(\theta\), meaning all encoder weights and biases. The encoding step is:

\[ z = F_{\theta}(x) \]

Training must learn the mapping \(F\), and backpropagation handles that once the objective is fixed. The verbal description from the session stays attached: the encoder takes the large input and learns a short code that stands in for it, with no labels needed.

Purpose in one line: distill each normal clip to the few numbers that suffice to rebuild it. Inputs and outputs: in goes the \(N\)-number clip, out comes the \(D\)-number code. When the code is well trained, two similar smile sequences \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) should ideally land near each other, though the plain autoencoder does not guarantee that neighborhood structure, which is the loose end picked up in 13.8.6.

Think of it like packing a large suitcase into a small carry-on summary note: destination, days, weather, events. A good note lets you repack something close to the original. The analogy breaks where the network note is unreadable: \(z\) is 1,024 floats with no words attached, useful to the decoder but opaque to people.

13.8.2 The decoder and the reconstruction objective

The decoder runs the trip in reverse. It takes the short code and tries to rebuild the original input from it.

Let \(G_{\phi}\) stand for the decoder mapping with parameters \(\phi\), meaning all decoder weights and biases. Let \(\hat{x}\) (x-hat) stand for the rebuilt clip in the same \(N\)-dimensional space as \(x\). The decoding step is:

\[ \hat{x} = G_{\phi}(z) \]

Chaining both steps gives the full rebuild directly from the input:

\[ \hat{x} = G_{\phi}(F_{\theta}(x)) \]

The inner term is the latent code and the outer term is the rebuild, so training needs nothing but the input clip itself: input goes in, code forms in the middle, rebuild comes out, and input versus rebuild supplies the teaching signal.

The training goal is that the rebuild matches the input, and the gap between them is the reconstruction error. Mean squared error or root mean squared error over pixels is enough, since the check runs pixel by pixel.

Let \(\text{MSE}\) stand for the mean squared error between rebuild and input, averaged over all \(N\) numbers:

\[ \text{MSE}(\hat{x}, x) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\hat{x}_{j} - x_{j})^{2} \]

The training objective is:

\[ \min_{\theta, \phi} \, \text{MSE}(\hat{x}, x) \]

Here \(\theta\) holds the encoder weights and \(\phi\) holds the decoder weights, and gradient descent tunes both at once by pushing the squared pixel gaps toward zero over normal training clips. When the code is right, the rebuild is close and the error is small; when the code is wrong, the error grows. The same gap that trains the network later serves as the anomaly score, which is the elegant turn of this design: \(s(x) = \text{MSE}(\hat{x}, x)\).

Derive the gradient path in words so no algebra is skipped. Each squared term \((\hat{x}_{j} - x_{j})^{2}\) differentiates to \(2(\hat{x}_{j} - x_{j})\) times the Jacobian of the decoder at \(z\), chained through the Jacobian of the encoder at \(x\). Backpropagation carries that product back through every layer of \(G_{\phi}\) then \(F_{\theta}\), updating \(\phi\) first and \(\theta\) behind it. Nothing about labels enters: the input is its own target.

Trace one pixel. Suppose input pixel \(x_{j} = 0.8\) on a 0-to-1 brightness scale and the current rebuild gives \(\hat{x}_{j} = 0.5\). The squared gap is \((0.5 - 0.8)^{2} = 0.09\). After more training the rebuild improves to \(0.78\) and the gap falls to \((0.78 - 0.80)^{2} = 0.0004\). Average that shrinkage over all \(N\) pixels and the MSE collapses from large to tiny. Training drives 0.09 down toward 0.0004 per pixel. Sense-check: a rebuild within 0.02 brightness on most pixels is visually indistinguishable, so tiny MSE means the code kept what mattered.

13.8.3 Network shapes: MLP, CNN, and the one hard rule

The encoder can be an ordinary multilayer perceptron, a funnel of dense layers with shrinking widths. The session sketches one such funnel: an input near 63,335 numbers stepping down through widths near 32,000 and 8,000 to 2,000 and finally a code of 1,024 numbers. Each step is a dense matrix multiply plus a nonlinearity, and the widths are design choices traded against memory and accuracy.

Because the input is really frames with spatial layout, convolutional layers fit better: pick kernels and stack them and the encoder becomes a convolutional encoder. A \(3 \times 3\) kernel with stride 2 halves each spatial map while doubling channels, repeating until the map is tiny, then a final dense layer emits the 1,024-number code. The decoder mirrors the trip upward with transposed convolutions or upsampling plus convolution, from the 1,024-number code back to the full 63,335-number output, through widening layers such as 4,000, 8,000, and beyond. Its exact widths are tuning choices.

Scope: MLP funnels suit flattened clips and quick baselines; convolutional stacks suit real frames where neighboring pixels belong together. Assumption: whatever the shape, the one hard rule holds: the decoder output size must equal the encoder input size, so the two can be compared pixel by pixel in the MSE. Break that shape match and the loss cannot even be computed.

Encoder and decoder are two separate networks with their own parameters, which is why they carry the two different symbols \(\theta\) and \(\phi\). Training updates both jointly, but at test time they run as a fixed pair: \(F_{\theta}\) compresses, \(G_{\phi}\) rebuilds, and the gap scores.

Count the funnel shrinkage. From 63,335 to 32,000 keeps about half; to 8,000 keeps about an eighth; to 2,000 keeps about 3 percent; to 1,024 keeps about 1.6 percent. The code keeps roughly one number per sixty input numbers. Sense-check: keeping 1.6 percent suffices only because normal video is highly redundant, with backgrounds static and motion smooth, which is exactly what the network exploits.

Picture the hourglass: wide bar at top labeled input \(N\), narrowing bars through 32k, 8k, 2k, a thin waist labeled \(z\) at \(D = 1{,}024\), then widening bars back to full width labeled \(\hat{x}\). The takeaway: the waist forces the network to keep only the reusable structure of normal clips.

13.8.4 Deployment, scores, and heatmaps

Deployment sends every incoming clip, normal or not, through the trained encoder and decoder pair and reads off the reconstruction error. A low error means the model knew how to rebuild the clip, so the clip is normal. A high error means the rebuild failed, so the clip is anomalous. Formally the anomaly score is the training loss reused:

\[ s(x) = \text{MSE}(\hat{x}, x) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\hat{x}_{j} - x_{j})^{2}, \quad \text{flag when } s(x) > \tau \]

Because the error is computed per pixel, it can be drawn as a heatmap the same size as the frame. Pixels with small errors shade toward blue, marking familiar content. Pixels with large errors shade toward red, yellow, or orange, marking the strange part. The map shows both when the anomaly fired and where inside the frame it sits, which is why operators like it: a red blob on an otherwise blue frame points at the intruder, the fallen person, or the cracked trace without watching the whole clip.

Read a heatmap with numbers. A normal corridor frame rebuilds with per-pixel squared errors near \(0.0005\), so the whole map stays blue and the frame score is \(s(x) = 0.0005\) under \(\tau = 0.01\). An intruder frame rebuilds the corridor fine but smears the person, giving squared errors near \(0.09\) over the 5 percent of pixels covering the figure and \(0.0005\) elsewhere, for a frame score near \(0.005 + 0.0005 \approx 0.0055\)... and a larger figure or worse smear pushes past \(\tau\). Blue everywhere passes; a red-yellow blob flags and localizes. Sense-check: the score is dominated by the strange region even when most pixels rebuild well, which is exactly the desired behavior.

One design question from the floor is answered here: the network runs frame by frame. Single-frame scoring catches point anomalies directly. Contextual anomalies need one more layer: score each of the \(T\) frames, pool the scores across the chunk, for example by taking the mean or the maximum, and flag the chunk when the pooled value passes the threshold. A chunk score of the max catches brief intrusions; a mean catches sustained drift. The choice follows the anomaly flavor from 13.5.

Think of the heatmap like a thermal camera for strangeness: blue is room temperature, red is hot. Operators scan a wall of blue frames and only open the ones with red blooms. The analogy breaks where heat is physical and error is learned: a red bloom means unfamiliar to this model, not hot in the world, so a new but harmless scene also blooms until retrained.

13.8.5 Overfitting and the memory fix

Autoencoders carry a known weakness: a strong decoder can rebuild even the anomalies. When the decoder generalizes too well, strange clips come back with small errors and slip through undetected. The session traces this to overfitting on normal data plus an over-powerful decoder: given enough capacity, \(G_{\phi}\) learns a near-identity shortcut that reconstructs anything, so \(s(x)\) stays small for friend and stranger alike.

A memory-augmented autoencoder, or MEM-AE, inserts a memory bank between the two halves. The encoder still produces its code \(z\), but the decoder never sees that raw code. Instead it receives a weighted mix of stored prototypes of normal behavior, call them \(m_1, \dots, m_K\), combined by addressing weights \(w_k\) computed from \(z\), and it must rebuild from that mix \(\hat{z} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k m_k\). Familiar clips match the stored prototypes well and rebuild cleanly. Strange clips match poorly, so their rebuilds fail and their errors stay large.

Trace it step by step. First the encoder emits \(z\) for the incoming clip. Next the memory computes similarity of \(z\) to each prototype \(m_k\) and normalizes to weights \(w_k\) summing to one. Then the mixed code \(\hat{z}\) goes to the decoder in place of \(z\). Finally the decoder rebuilds from \(\hat{z}\) only. A normal smile lands near a smile prototype, weight concentrates, rebuild succeeds. A cracked board or a fall pattern lands far from every prototype, weights spread thin, rebuild smears, error stays high.

Industrial inspection systems use memory-style bottlenecks so that novel defects cannot be painted over by an over-eager decoder: the decoder is only allowed to speak in sentences from the normal phrasebook, so anything unphraseable comes out garbled and gets flagged. The extra cost is the memory size \(K\) and the addressing computation, modest beside the encoder-decoder bulk.

Scope: the memory fix helps exactly the over-powerful-decoder failure; it does not fix bad features or mislabeled normal data. Assumption: the prototype bank genuinely spans normal variety. When \(K\) is too small or training missed a normal mode, such as night footage, legitimate clips match nothing and false-alarm.

A common trap is shrinking the latent code \(D\) to starve the decoder instead of adding memory. Starving helps a little but also degrades normal rebuilds, raising the noise floor for everyone. The memory bank is the surgical version: keep \(D\) expressive, but force traffic through memorized normal prototypes.

13.8.6 Why the latent space needs a constraint

Even with the memory fix, the plain autoencoder memorizes rather than organizes. Its latent space carries no rule about grouping, so similar clips need not land near each other. What operators want is neighborhood structure: two similar smile sequences \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) should sit side by side in code space, broad toothy smiles should cluster together, and tiny closed-lip smiles should form their own nearby group far from the toothy one. The plain autoencoder does not promise any of that, because the MSE only asks that each clip rebuild, never that similar clips co-locate.

Q: Training clips differ in many ways at once, such as face shape versus tooth visibility, so how does the encoder know which trait to group by?

A: The plain autoencoder cannot enforce that grouping, and adding an explicit constraint on the latent space that pulls similar clips together is exactly the job handed to the variational autoencoder taken up in the next session. The question is perceptive because faces vary along dozens of axes at once, and unguided MSE picks whichever axis rebuilds cheapest, often lighting or identity rather than the smile trait the task needs. The fix constrains \(z\) toward a shaped prior with grouped neighborhoods, so the trait the loss rewards becomes the axis the space organizes around.

Picture code space as a map. Today similar smiles are scattered houses with no streets. The wanted map has a toothy-smile district, a closed-lip district, and a neutral outskirts, with \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) as next-door neighbors. The takeaway: reconstruction alone draws no districts; only an added latent constraint zones the map.

A beginner trap is reading cluster proximity in a plain autoencoder as meaningful. Without a constraint, nearness in \(z\) is accident, and two codes a hair apart can decode to very different faces. Never argue from latent distances until the model that shaped them earns that reading.

Real teams feel this when they try to browse anomalies by code similarity and get incoherent neighborhoods. Search-by-example starts working only once the latent prior organizes the space, which is why the lecture hands the story to the variational autoencoder next.

Exam note: expect questions on why reconstruction error doubles as the anomaly score, how MEM-AE prototypes defeat overconfident decoders, and why the plain latent space cannot be trusted for grouping. Recap: squeeze, rebuild, score the gap, localize with heatmaps, pin the decoder to normal prototypes, and constrain the code map next. 13.1 What Counts as an Anomaly in Video

Must-know: Anomaly means significant deviation from learned normal behavior, decided by s(x) > tau

\[\text{anomaly}(x) = 1 \quad \text{if} \quad s(x) > \tau, \quad \text{else} \quad 0\]

Top pitfall: Treating s(x) > tau as a law of nature instead of a per-setting design choice

Self-check: What do the two words deviate and significantly contribute to the definition?
