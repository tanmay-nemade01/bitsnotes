Video Analysis

Video Retrieval: Embeddings, Indexing and Augmented Generation

Published: 2026-09-16
Level: postgraduate
Audience: Postgraduate students in Video Analysis

16.1 What Retrieval Means and the Five Parts of a Retrieval System

16.1.1 Two Kinds of Query

A retrieval task (finding the right items in a large store from a short request) starts with a query and ends with a short list of videos pulled from a large store. The two query forms are text-to-video (words in, videos out) and video-to-video (a clip in, similar clips out).

Why does one system need both forms? Because users arrive with different starting points. A person who wants to cook types how to make paneer butter masala and expects cooking videos that show each step. Here the input is text and the output is video. A person who already holds a video wants more like it. Two concrete uses show why the second form matters. One is checking for copied uploads: a cooking video is posted by one account, the same video appears under another account, and the owner wants to find every near copy so that copyright infringement can be handled. The other is gathering variants of one theme: a person wants sunset clips from many places or many days for comparison or study, so one sunset clip becomes the query and every similar sunset clip should come back.

Both forms point at the same store. The store is described as a data lake of videos, a large collection where every video is kept and maintained as the source that searches run against. The word lake signals size and mixed content: long and short clips, many topics, many uploaders, all sitting in one place.

Think of the store as a large library and each query as a slip handed to the desk. A text slip says "books about paneer butter masala." A video slip says "more books like this one I am holding." The desk must serve both slips from the same shelves. The mapping works when each slip is turned into the same kind of request the shelves understand. That shared request form is the similarity space met later in this lecture.

16.1.2 Five Parts That Must Work Together

A working retrieval system has five parts: (1) the data lake (the video collection), (2) the index (the searching mechanism built on that collection), (3) the query (text or video form), (4) similarity computation (a score linking query with stored items), and (5) ranking (sorting scores into a short relevant list, for example the top three or top five results in descending order of score).

Each part has one job. The collection holds the videos. The index makes them findable without scanning everything. The query states what is wanted. Similarity scores each candidate against the query. Ranking sorts those scores so that only the top few relevant links are returned in descending order. Similarity gives numbers; ranking turns those numbers into an ordered answer.

A textbook comparison makes the role of the second part concrete. To find something in a textbook, a reader can flip pages one by one, follow the table of contents, or use the index at the back. The index is fastest when it is well maintained. In the same way, the data lake needs an index, and the query and the index must share a similarity space, a common ground where a comparison score makes sense. Page-flipping is exact search over the full lake. The table of contents is a coarse grouping, like clip or topic buckets. The back index is the real target: a ready structure that jumps straight to the right entries.

Picture the score flow with small numbers. A query arrives. Similarity gives three candidates scores such as 0.91, 0.83, and 0.47. Ranking sorts them in descending order and returns the top two or top three links. Without ranking, the user would get an unordered pile of numbers. Without similarity, ranking would have nothing to sort.

16.1.3 What the Index Must Satisfy

Building the index is not only about listing items. The index must be searchable, so that a query can reach the right entries without scanning everything. It must be scalable, so that it keeps working when the collection grows very large. And it must accept changes in real time, because the data lake keeps changing while a printed index stays fixed. New videos arrive, old ones are removed, and the index has to track that movement. Similarity alone is also not enough. A system must move from similar to relevant, which is why ranking and relevance form their own part of the design.

Scope: the printed-index picture breaks at one point. A printed index is frozen at press time, while a video lake changes daily. Any design that assumes a fixed collection will fail in production. The index must support inserts and deletes without a full rebuild each hour. Assumption: the query and the index share one similarity space. When that shared space is missing, scores from two sides cannot be compared, and ranking has no valid input.

Imagine a chart with collection size on the horizontal axis and query time on the vertical axis. A full scan is a rising line: each new video adds work. A good index is a nearly flat curve: query time stays low while size grows by factors of ten. The landmark to watch is the point where the scan line leaves the page. That point is where exact search stops being usable and approximate search begins. The one-sentence takeaway is that the index buys flat query time at the price of upfront build work plus ongoing maintenance.

Two traps catch beginners here. First, treating similar as relevant. Two sunset clips can look alike while only one answers the question asked. Relevance needs the query intent, not pixels alone. Second, treating the index as a one-time build. A lake with daily uploads needs insert, delete, and rebuild paths from day one.

Recap: retrieval serves text queries and video queries from one lake through five parts, with the index doing the heavy lifting. The bridge to the next step is representation: the index can only be fast when each video is stored as a compact vector that supports nearest neighbor search.

Typing words into a video tab of a web search engine and getting video results, then picking one video and receiving more videos on the same dish, is retrieval working behind a familiar page. The first page is text-to-video. The follow-on row is video-to-video. Both rows come from the same five-part design.

16.1.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Where do the five parts fit, and why is ranking listed separately from similarity?

A: The collection holds the videos, the index makes them findable, the query states what is wanted, similarity scores each candidate against the query, and ranking sorts those scores so that only the top few relevant links are returned. Similarity gives numbers; ranking turns those numbers into an ordered answer. Ranking stays separate because sorting, cut-off choice such as top three or top five, and relevance filtering are design decisions of their own.

16.2 Embedding Space and Encoder Design Choices

16.2.1 Fixed Length Vectors and the Similarity Idea

An embedding (a fixed length vector that stands for one input) turns variable length video into a list of numbers with a set size. A clip, a frame, or a whole video is mapped to that fixed size no matter how long the input was, and that vector is what gets compared.

Video is long, variable in length, and hard to compare directly. Pixel-by-pixel comparison fails because two clips of the same dish never share the same length, lighting, or framing. A clip, a frame, or a whole video is mapped to a fixed length vector, a list of numbers with a set size that stays the same no matter how long the input was. That vector is what gets compared.

The comparison idea is borrowed from grouping by distance. In K-means clustering, points that are alike end up in one cluster, close together, while points that are unlike end up far apart in different clusters. The embedding space for retrieval is built on the same promise: all videos with similar content sit close together, and videos with distinct content sit far apart. All the paneer butter masala videos occupy one region, all the sunset videos occupy another region, and the two regions stay apart because the content is different. When that layout holds, search becomes a nearest neighbor task. Given a query vector, the system looks for the stored vectors nearest to it, which can be done with simple tools such as K-nearest neighbors instead of a complex search routine.

Let \(z \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) be the embedding of one item, where \(d\) is the fixed dimension. Retrieval assumes that a small distance in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) means similar content and a large distance means distinct content, so that nearest neighbor search returns relevant items.

Think of each video as a house and its embedding as the street address written in numbers. Houses on the same street share nearby addresses. Cooking videos share one street, sunset videos share another street, and the streets stay apart. A query is a visitor asking for the nearest houses to a given address. The picture breaks when the encoder is weak: then houses from different streets get mixed addresses, and nearness means nothing. Good training is what keeps each street together.

Imagine a two-dimensional sketch with the first embedding axis left to right and the second axis bottom to top. Paneer butter masala points form a tight cloud on the left. Sunset points form a tight cloud on the right. A query point lands inside the left cloud, and its three nearest dots are all cooking videos. The takeaway is that geometry carries meaning only when training placed similar items close together.

Worked sketch: take \(d = 2\) for drawing. Suppose three stored vectors are \(z_{1} = [0.9, 0.1]\), \(z_{2} = [0.8, 0.2]\), and \(z_{3} = [-0.8, 0.3]\). A cooking query lands at \(z_{q} = [0.85, 0.15]\). Euclidean gaps are about 0.07 to \(z_{1}\), 0.07 to \(z_{2}\), and over 1.6 to \(z_{3}\). The two nearest items are both cooking videos, and the sunset item sits far away. Sense-check: nearness follows content here because training grouped each theme into its own region.

16.2.2 Four Encoder Options

Frames go into an encoder, and the encoder produces the embedding, written here as \(z\). The symbol \(z\) means the numeric vector for one input item, with \(z_{q}\) for the query item and \(z_{v}\) for a stored video item. The subscript \(q\) marks query, the subscript \(v\) marks video.

The designer picks what the encoder should pay attention to: (1) spatial content alone, which is about what appears in the picture, (2) temporal content alone, which is about how things move, (3) spatial plus temporal together, or (4) attention-based weighting on top. Each option maps to families of networks studied earlier, and picking one is a design choice.

A person running against a person jogging shows why the choice matters. The two look alike in a still frame, so spatial content alone struggles. Motion carries most of the meaning: stride rate, arm swing, and pace over time. A temporal or joint encoder sees that difference, while a pure spatial encoder misses it. A SlowFast network is given as one concrete pick: frames enter a SlowFast encoder and a single embedding vector comes out. The slow path reads scene shape at low frame rate, the fast path reads quick motion at high frame rate, and the fused output \(z\) carries both.

Option Sees Misses Good for
Spatial only Objects, scenes, colors Motion order Dishes, places, sunsets
Temporal only Movement patterns Fine appearance Running against jogging
Spatial plus temporal Both Little, at higher cost Most clip tasks
Attention on top Key moments with weights Little, at highest cost Long clips with dull parts

Pick spatial when the question is what is visible. Pick temporal or joint when the question is how it moves. Pick attention when only a few seconds inside a long clip carry the answer.

16.2.3 Granularity Is Separate From Architecture

A common doubt is whether networks such as I3D or 3D CNNs force video level output. They do not. The architecture and the granularity are two separate decisions. The same family of encoders can take a short clip of a few seconds, say thirty seconds, and return one vector for it. It can take a whole video and return one vector for that. Or, when each frame is fed in turn through a spatial or temporal encoder, it can return one vector per frame. So \(v_{1}\) may mean the first frame, the first short clip, or the first full video, depending on the granularity the designer picked. A thirty second clip works well as one input unit and yields one vector with the chosen dimension.

Scope: one vector per input unit is the rule; what counts as a unit is the design decision. The same I3D or 3D CNN weights can serve frame, clip, or video duty when the input window changes. Assumption: the encoder input window matches the stored unit. A query cut into five-second clips cannot be compared directly against an index of full-video vectors without re-encoding or pooling.

Two traps sit here. First, reading the network name as the output level: I3D does not mean video level by itself. Second, mixing units across query and index: a frame query against a video index compares unlike things, and scores lose meaning.

Recap: embeddings place similar videos close together so search becomes nearest neighbor lookup, encoders choose what to see, and granularity chooses the unit size. The bridge is normalization: raw vectors need a shared scale before distances can be trusted.

16.2.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Does an encoder such as I3D or 3D CNN only produce video level vectors, and how would frame level output arise?

A: The architecture does not fix the granularity. Feed it a clip of a few seconds and it returns one vector for that clip. Feed it a full video and it returns one vector for that video. Feed it single frames one by one through a spatial or temporal path and each frame yields its own vector. One vector per input unit is the rule; what counts as a unit is the design decision.

Some students asked the same granularity question in different words, so it is merged here into one canonical answer.

Q: How should purpose guide the choice between frame, clip, and video level?

A: Start from the purpose seen from above. For copyright or duplicate checks, clip or video level is usually enough. Frame level is rarely needed for retrieval because neighboring frames repeat so much. Most practical work starts at clip level, and frame level is kept for fine tasks such as surgery or repair retrieval where small steps must not be lost.

16.3 L2 Normalization and the Similarity Measure

16.3.1 Mathematical Formulation

Why normalize at all? Raw embeddings carry two facts mixed together: direction (what the content is) and length (how strong the response was). Retrieval mostly wants direction, so each vector is scaled to length one before comparison.

Once an embedding \(z\) exists, it is L2 normalized before any comparison. L2 normalization (dividing a vector by its own length to get a unit vector) means the result has length one. In words from the discussion, each embedding is converted into unit vector form so that later comparison becomes simple. For a vector \(z\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\), where \(d\) is the dimension such as \(d = 128\), with entries stored as numbers at 8 or 16 bit precision depending on the system, the normalized form is the vector divided by its Euclidean length:

\[ \hat{z} = \frac{z}{\|z\|_{2}} \]

where \(z \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the raw embedding vector, \(\|z\|_{2}\) is its Euclidean length, a positive scalar, and \(\hat{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the unit length version with \(\|\hat{z}\|_{2} = 1\). The query embedding becomes \(\hat{z}_{q}\) and each stored video embedding becomes \(\hat{z}_{v_{1}}, \hat{z}_{v_{2}}, \hat{z}_{v_{3}}\), and so on, with the numeric subscript marking video one, video two, video three.

The Euclidean length itself is built step by step from entries \(z_{1}, \ldots, z_{d}\):

\[ \|z\|_{2} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d} z_{i}^{2}} \]

so the \(j\)-th entry of the normalized vector is \(\hat{z}_{j} = z_{j} / \|z\|_{2}\). Dividing each entry by the same positive number keeps direction fixed while forcing the total length to one.

Comparison then uses angle rather than raw distance. Euclidean distance is the simplest option, but cosine similarity is preferred because it compares the angle between vectors and stays agnostic to magnitude once vectors share the same scale. The verbal form given is that cosine similarity computes the angle between the vector spaces. Its formula is:

\[ \mathrm{cos}(z_{q}, z_{v}) = \frac{z_{q}^{\top} z_{v}}{\|z_{q}\|_{2} \, \|z_{v}\|_{2}} \]

where \(z_{q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the query vector, \(z_{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) is one stored video vector, \(z_{q}^{\top} z_{v}\) is their dot product, a scalar sum of elementwise products, and the denominator is the product of their two lengths. The dot product itself expands as:

\[ z_{q}^{\top} z_{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{d} z_{q,i} \, z_{v,i} \]

When both vectors are already L2 normalized, each length equals one, the denominator becomes one, and the whole expression collapses to a dot product:

\[ \mathrm{cos}(\hat{z}_{q}, \hat{z}_{v}) = \hat{z}_{q}^{\top} \hat{z}_{v} \]

where \(\hat{z}_{q}\) and \(\hat{z}_{v}\) are the L2 normalized query and video vectors. The short derivation is one line: start from the cosine form, set \(\|\hat{z}_{q}\|_{2} = 1\) and \(\|\hat{z}_{v}\|_{2} = 1\), and the denominator turns into \(1 \times 1 = 1\). That is why normalization is placed in the pipeline before search: it removes the denominator and leaves a dot product of two vectors, which is fast to compute across a large index. In short, embed with one of the four architecture choices to get \(z\), L2 normalize to get \(\hat{z}\), and compare with a dot product.

Think of two arrows from the same start point. One arrow is long, one is short, but both point the same way. Raw dot product would favor the long arrow. Normalization first trims both arrows to unit length, so only the angle between them decides the score. The picture breaks for the zero vector, which has no direction; in code that case needs a guard because division by zero is undefined.

Scope: cosine scoring fits when direction carries meaning and magnitude is noise from lighting, clip length, or network gain. Assumption: no stored vector is exactly zero, and all vectors live in the same \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) with the same encoder recipe. When magnitudes carry signal, such as energy or count, cosine throws that signal away and a magnitude-aware score is needed.

Picture cosine against angle: at angle zero the score is 1, at ninety degrees it is 0, at opposite directions it is minus 1. The horizontal axis is the angle in degrees, the vertical axis is the score from minus 1 to 1, and the curve falls smoothly like a cosine wave from left to right. The landmark is ninety degrees: orthogonal content scores zero even when both vectors are strong.

Two traps recur. First, normalizing only one side. When the query is normalized but the index is not, the denominator survives and ranking mixes angle with stored-vector length. Second, storing at low precision and then normalizing twice with rounding drift. Pick 8 or 16 bit storage once, normalize once, and keep that form everywhere.

16.3.2 Worked Sketch With Dimensions

Worked numbers in \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\): let \(z_{q} = [3, 4]\) and \(z_{v} = [1, 0]\). Lengths are \(\|z_{q}\|_{2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5\) and \(\|z_{v}\|_{2} = 1\). Normalized forms are \(\hat{z}_{q} = [0.6, 0.8]\) and \(\hat{z}_{v} = [1, 0]\). The dot product is \(0.6 \times 1 + 0.8 \times 0 = \mathbf{0.6}\). The full cosine gives the same value: \((3 \times 1 + 4 \times 0)/(5 \times 1) = \mathbf{0.6}\). Sense-check: the angle has cosine 0.6, about 53 degrees, which matches a partly aligned pair.

Worked sketch at \(d = 128\): the query side holds one vector \(\hat{z}_{q} \in \mathbb{R}^{128}\). The index side holds many vectors \(\hat{z}_{v_{1}}, \hat{z}_{v_{2}}, \ldots\), each in \(\mathbb{R}^{128}\). The system scores \(\hat{z}_{q}^{\top} \hat{z}_{v_{1}}\), then \(\hat{z}_{q}^{\top} \hat{z}_{v_{2}}\), and so on, one dot product per stored item, and sorts the scores from high to low. A 128 dimension unit vector per item is a typical operating point when storage precision and speed must be balanced.

Recap: L2 normalization scales each embedding to unit length so cosine similarity becomes a plain dot product. The bridge is granularity: once scoring is cheap, the open question is which video unit each vector should cover.

16.4 Granularity, Detail, Cost and Pooling

16.4.1 Three Levels and Their Trade Offs

Why not always use the finest level? Finer units keep more detail but each unit needs its own vector, so storage and search cost grow. Granularity (the video unit that gets one vector) is a three-way trade between detail kept and cost paid.

Granularity means the unit that gets one vector. At video level, one vector stands for an entire video. That is compact but temporal detail gets lost, because steps inside a long recording blur together. At clip level, one vector stands for a shorter segment, so short motions stay visible, for example the garnishing step in a paneer butter masala recording, a sunset passage, a lion running, or a lion roaring. At frame level, every single frame gets its own vector. That is the finest grade and captures each moment, but the index must then store a vector for each frame, which raises storage and computation cost. The choice depends on what is being retrieved and why. The stated optimal choice for most work is clip level, because it keeps temporal information while lowering indexing and storage cost relative to frame level, without losing as much detail as video level.

Worked vacation case: a vacation collection holds hours of footage with only one short sunset passage of about twenty seconds. At video level, one vector must cover swimming, meals, driving, and that sunset together, so the sunset signal is diluted. At clip level with thirty-second units, one clip vector covers the sunset passage almost alone and matches a sunset query with a high dot product. At frame level, hundreds of near-identical sunset frames each match well but cost hundreds of vectors for one passage. Sense-check: clip level keeps the passage findable at a small fraction of frame level cost, which is why it is the usual pick.

Level One vector covers Detail kept Cost Good for
Video Whole recording Low, steps blur Lowest Duplicate check of full uploads
Clip Short segment Mid, steps visible Mid Most retrieval, garnishing or sunset passage
Frame Single image Highest, each moment Highest Surgery or repair steps

Think of summarizing a book. Video level is one paragraph for the whole book. Clip level is one paragraph per chapter. Frame level is one paragraph per sentence. Chapter summaries are the usual pick because they keep the plot while staying short.

Picture storage cost on the vertical axis against detail kept on the horizontal axis. Video level sits bottom-left, frame level sits top-right, and clip level sits in the bend of the curve where detail rises fast while cost stays modest. The takeaway is to move right only while the task truly needs the extra detail.

16.4.2 When Frame Level Is Worth It

Frame level is discussed in theory but used rarely in practice, because consecutive frames carry heavy redundancy, with very little change from one frame to the next. It earns its keep when fine steps must not be lost. Two examples are retrieving similar surgery procedures, where minute procedural moves matter, and retrieving similar repair procedures for a mechanical device or gadget, where small steps cannot be missed. In such cases the designer still processes frame level features through a spatial or temporal encoder, then aggregates the per frame vectors into one vector with a pooling step so that detail is kept without paying frame level storage forever.

Pooling (merging many frame vectors into one clip or video vector) lets a system read at frame detail but store at clip cost. Frame vectors \(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{T}\) enter pooling, and one vector \(z\) leaves for the index.

A surgery example shows the logic. One stitch has five small moves: needle entry, pull-through, loop, knot, cut. A clip vector may blur the knot into the pull. Frame vectors keep each move, and pooling then blends them into one vector that still carries the knot signal. The same holds for a gadget repair where one missed screw step changes the answer.

16.4.3 Pooling Choices

Pooling is the aggregation that turns many frame vectors into one. Mean pooling (averaging frame vectors) suits cases where order does not matter, where the sequence is insignificant. Attention pooling (weighting frames by importance) suits cases where order and key moments matter, which is most of the time when fine detail was the reason for going to frame level. Max pooling (keeping only the maximum value along each dimension) behaves like averaging in spirit but with less computation, taking the strongest response and dropping the rest. The rule is simple: when sequence can be ignored, mean pooling is acceptable; when sequence carries meaning, attention pooling is the better pick.

Scope: mean pooling fits unordered sets such as a pile of sunset stills. Attention pooling fits ordered steps such as stitch then knot. Max pooling fits peak detection such as the brightest flare in a clip, but it drops the rest of the story. Assumption: pooled vectors are still L2 normalized after pooling, or cosine scores will mix pooled magnitude with angle.

A tiny number sketch fixes the difference. Three frame scalars \([0, 10, 0]\) mean a flash in the middle frame. Mean gives 3.3, max gives 10, attention with weights \([0.1, 0.8, 0.1]\) gives 8.0. Mean dilutes the flash, max keeps only the peak, attention keeps the peak while remembering the rest. Pick the blend that matches the question.

Two traps follow beginners. First, using frame level for everything because it feels safest, then running out of memory on the index. Second, using mean pooling on ordered steps such as a recipe, where garnish-then-serve and serve-then-garnish average to the same vector but mean different things.

Exam note: expect questions that ask which granularity fits a stated purpose, and which pooling fits a stated need about order. Copyright checks point to clip or video level, surgery or fine repair retrieval points to frame level plus aggregation, order insignificant points to mean pooling, order important points to attention pooling.

16.5 Unimodal Retrieval With Video Queries

16.5.1 Near Duplicates, Copyright and Recommendations

Why start with video-to-video before text? Because both sides share one encoder family, so no translation step is needed. Unimodal retrieval (query and stored items in the same modality) is the simplest working system and the base on which harder cross-modal search builds.

In unimodal retrieval the query and the stored items share one modality. A video query is encoded with the same family of video encoders used for the index, so comparison stays inside one space and stays easy. Three uses dominate. One is normal video search ending in similar video suggestions. One is recommendation: watch one cooking video and the next visit surfaces more videos on the same dish, because the engine retrieved items whose content vectors sit nearby. One is duplicate removal and copyright handling. The recommendation rows on video platforms such as YouTube and streaming services such as Netflix, and the video tab on a web search page, are everyday faces of this mechanism.

One encoder family serves both sides: the query clip and each stored clip pass through the same video encoder recipe, are L2 normalized the same way, and are compared with the same dot product. No shared-space projector is needed because there is only one space.

Near duplicate handling deserves care. Copies are often edited slightly through overlay changes, compression changes, or speed changes. A logo is pasted in a corner, quality is lowered on re-upload, or playback runs ten percent faster. Three defenses are named. First, add signatures or fingerprints that survive small edits. Second, align temporal content so that shifted or re-timed copies still match. Third, pass candidates through simple transformations to test whether a suspected copy is a near duplicate. Detailed treatment of these three is deferred until later in the flow, after the multimodal part is in place.

Think of fingerprints as a song chorus detector that still fires when the track is played slightly faster with crowd noise on top. A fragile hash would break; a strong fingerprint bends without breaking. Temporal alignment is like sliding two audio tracks until their beats line up before comparing.

Scope: unimodal matching answers "more like this" but cannot answer a typed question with no example clip. When the user has only words, the system must move to the shared-space design of the next section. Assumption: query and index share encoder weights or at least the same training recipe. Mixing encoder families across sides without alignment breaks the score scale.

16.5.2 Level and Scope

Unimodal comparison can run at video level or clip level, matching the granularity discussion. Frame level stays rare for the redundancy reason already noted. The purpose decides: duplicate and copyright checks often sit at clip or video level, while recommendation naturally follows whatever unit the platform stores.

Picture a platform storing one vector per clip. A watched cooking clip retrieves its five nearest clip vectors, and the interface groups them by parent video for the recommendation row. The visual is a query dot with five nearby dots in the embedding plane, each labeled with its parent title. The takeaway is that recommendation is nearest neighbor search with a nicer front end.

Recap: unimodal retrieval compares video with video in one space for search, recommendation, and copyright work, with fingerprints and alignment as defenses against light edits. The bridge is the harder case: typed words against stored video, which needs a shared space.

16.6 Shared Space, CLIP and Contrastive Training

16.6.1 Why Text and Video Cannot Be Compared Directly

A user with only words, such as a person riding a bicycle at night, has no clip to show. Why can't the system just compare the text vector with the video vector? Because the two encoders speak different languages, and raw scores across languages carry no meaning.

A text query such as a person riding a bicycle at night is natural for a user who does not hold a matching video. The problem is that a text encoder and a video encoder speak different languages. The example given pairs BERT on the text side with VideoMAE on the video side. One embedding lives in one space, the other in another space, and the two cannot be compared, in the same way that output in Hindi cannot be matched directly with output in Telugu, or Greek with Latin. The fix is a shared embedding space: both sides must be translated into one common language, Latin to Latin or Hindi to Hindi in the analogy, so that a dot product between a text vector and a video vector carries meaning. In symbols, with \(z_{t}\) for the text side and \(z_{v}\) for the video side, the goal is a space where \(z_{t}^{\top} z_{v}\) is a valid similarity score. Without that shared space, text to video matching cannot work.

Let \(z_{t} \in \mathbb{R}^{D}\) and \(z_{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{D}\) be the text and video vectors after projection into one shared space of dimension \(D\). Retrieval is valid only when both vectors share \(\mathbb{R}^{D}\) and the same normalization, so \(z_{t}^{\top} z_{v}\) is a true angle score.

A produce-market picture helps. The text seller prices in one currency, the video seller prices in another, and the numbers cannot be compared until both convert to a common currency. The shared space is that common currency. The analogy breaks at one point: currency conversion is fixed arithmetic, while embedding translation must be learned from paired data.

16.6.2 CLIP Architecture and the Projection Step

CLIP is a contrastive language image pre trained model that compares natural language with images and maps them together. Its parts are: an image encoder for frames, a text encoder for captions, and one small projector per side that translates both outputs into the shared space.

For video work the starting point drops back to frames. Each frame passes through an image encoder, typically a vision transformer called ViT or a ResNet, which yields image features. The text passes through a text encoder, with BERT named as the example family, which yields text features. Call the image side output \(i\) and the text side output \(t\). These are still in different spaces, so each side goes through a projector, a shallow network, usually a two or three layer MLP, a multilayer perceptron with a small hidden part. The projector converts \(i\) into \(z_{i}\) and \(t\) into \(z_{t}\), both in one shared space of dimension \(D\), written as \(z^{D}\). The numbers used in the discussion make the plumbing concrete: the image path may start at 512 dimensions, the text path at 128 dimensions, and both project to 256 dimensions. Inputs and outputs everywhere at this stage are vectors, single modality data, which is easier to handle than raw pixels plus raw words. Pre trained models supply the encoders so that heavy training is avoided, and only the projection heads are trained to perform the translation into the shared space.

Worked plumbing: an image feature \(i \in \mathbb{R}^{512}\) enters a two layer projector \(512 \to 384 \to 256\) and leaves as \(z_{i} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}\). A text feature \(t \in \mathbb{R}^{128}\) enters a two layer projector \(128 \to 192 \to 256\) and leaves as \(z_{t} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}\). Both outputs are L2 normalized, so the match score is the dot product \(z_{i}^{\top} z_{t}\), a scalar in \([-1, 1]\). Sense-check: two inputs of different sizes now share one 256 dimension space, so the score is valid.

A personal story makes the translation goal vivid. A visitor in Hyderabad needs stage lighting changed but shares no language with the light engineer, so a student interprets. The visitor does not ask for word by word translation. The visitor asks for the job to be done: the light dims, the power changes, the cue lands. A short request may need five sentences in the other language to carry the intent. Retrieval wants the same thing. The projection is not asked to restate a frame as exact text. It is asked to place the frame vector and the text vector so close together in the shared space that a query such as sunset videos returns sunset videos. The job done matters more than literal wording, which is why projection goes to a new shared space rather than forcing video into text space alone. Projecting everything into text space would be costly and would miss the point, since the aim is matching vectors, not generating captions.

Freezing the big encoders and training only the small heads is a cost choice. Full retraining of ViT plus BERT on video pairs would need far more data and compute. Tuning two or three layer MLPs needs far less, because each head holds only a small stack of matrices while the frozen backs supply strong features.

16.6.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Should the whole video go through an MLP that translates it into text space so that text to text matching can follow?

A: No. The design does not project to text space alone, because that is costly and the goal is not to produce text. The goal is that the vector from text and the vector from the clip or video sit near each other in one shared space so that they match. Both sides project into that common space instead.

16.6.4 Contrastive Training With Positive and Negative Pairs

How do the small projectors learn the translation? By comparing and contrasting: each matched pair is pulled together while mismatched pairs are pushed apart, one hard negative at a time.

The projection heads learn through contrastive training, which compares and contrasts, as the name says. Training collects positive examples against negative examples. A sunset video with its own caption is a positive pair. A beach volleyball clip with no sun in frame is not a sunset, so pairing a sunset caption with that clip is a negative pair. With \(n\) videos and \(n\) texts, every combination forms an \(n\) by \(n\) similarity matrix. Videos run along one side, texts along the other. The diagonal holds the matched pairs, video one with text one, video two with text two, video three with text three, and those scores should be high, with values such as 0.97 and 0.78 given as illustrations of strong matches. Every off diagonal cell pairs video one with text two, video one with text three, and so on, and those scores should stay near zero or very low. Similarity is symmetric: the score of video one with text one equals the score of text one with video one, order does not matter, so only one triangle of the matrix needs separate attention.

Training stays balanced by pairing each positive with a negative. From the many off diagonal options for one item, the most confusing or most optimal negative is picked, so that each video or each text sees one positive against one hard negative, a one to one balance. The loss follows the same symmetry. The image to text loss is a softmax over the pairings in one direction, the text to image loss is a softmax over the pairings in the other direction, and the final contrastive loss, also called InfoNCE, averages the two:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{L}_{i \to t} + \mathcal{L}_{t \to i}\right) \]

where \(\mathcal{L}_{i \to t}\) is the softmax based loss from image to text, \(\mathcal{L}_{t \to i}\) is the softmax based loss from text to image, and \(\mathcal{L}\) is the symmetric contrastive loss, a scalar.

A three-by-three number sketch shows the target. Before training the matrix looks flat, with every cell near 0.3. After training the diagonal reads 0.97, 0.85, 0.78 while off diagonal cells sit near 0.02 to 0.10. Each row softmax then peaks at the diagonal cell, each column softmax peaks likewise, and the mean of the two directions keeps both retrieval directions strong: image to text and text to image.

Worked row softmax: fix video one with text scores \([0.97, 0.05, 0.10]\) before softmax. Exponentials are about \([2.64, 1.05, 1.11]\), summing to about 4.80. The positive probability is \(2.64 / 4.80 \approx \mathbf{0.55}\), already the clear winner, and training pushes it higher by raising the diagonal score and lowering the rest. Sense-check: a high diagonal with near-zero negatives gives a peaked softmax, which is exactly the training goal.

After training, diagonal entries stand out as aligned and off diagonal entries stay non aligned. At inference time the stored side is ready in advance: each video is split into frames, each frame is embedded into the shared space, and those vectors wait in the store. At run time only the text query is encoded into a query vector \(z_{q}\), compared with stored vectors \(z_{v}\), and the top \(K\) vectors come back.

Scope: contrastive heads align what the frozen encoders can already see. When the frozen image encoder misses motion or a fresh domain, no pairing loss can invent the missing signal, so light tuning of more layers is needed. Assumption: each batch holds true negatives. When captions repeat across items, such as many clips captioned sunset, off diagonal cells are not true negatives and the loss needs care.

16.6.5 Zero Shot Behavior

A striking property of this family is zero shot classification. A model trained on a very large image caption collection, with four hundred million pairs named as the scale, can handle new images and new words without fresh training. The trick is textual prompting: each class is turned into a generic text prompt, prompts are embedded, the image is embedded, and the image is assigned to the prompt it matches best. So a query such as a person riding a unicycle can succeed even when the words cycle or unicycle never appeared as a training class, because the shared space supports broad vocabulary matching.

Think of the shared space as a map learned from four hundred million signposts. A new word such as unicycle still lands near cycle, wheel, and person riding signs, so its address is sensible on arrival. The picture breaks for words with no neighbors on the map, where placement becomes a guess.

16.6.6 From CLIP to CLIP4Clip and Its Limits

CLIP as described handles images, while retrieval needs videos or clips. CLIP4Clip, written as contrastive clip for video, closes the gap in a simple way. Each frame of a video or clip passes through the image CLIP encoder to give per frame vectors, call them \(z_{f}\) per frame, and then a pooling step merges those frame vectors into one aggregate video or clip vector. The pre trained image part is reused, and only the aggregation is tuned on a video dataset, which is fine tuning rather than full training. Three limits remain. First, the frozen image part cannot fully learn temporal detail, so motion heavy distinctions suffer and tuning is needed to keep temporal information. Second, the frozen model does not scale to every domain on its own, so fresh domains need tuning too. Third, certain language fails: negation such as fetch videos with no sunset or which do not have sunset works poorly, and counting questions such as how many dogs are barking work poorly. Light tuning helps bridge each of these gaps.

Picture CLIP4Clip as a pipeline: frames \(f_{1}, \ldots, f_{T}\) enter the frozen image encoder, per frame vectors \(z_{f}\) leave, pooling blends them into one clip vector \(z_{v}\), and that vector meets the text vector \(z_{t}\) in the shared space. The only newly trained block is the blending step plus small adapters.

Exam note: expect a question that asks why two projection heads are trained instead of forcing video into text space, and what InfoNCE averages. The answer is cost plus matching intent for the first part, and the mean of image to text and text to image softmax losses for the second.

16.7 Indexing, Scale and Approximate Search

16.7.1 Cost of Exact Search and the Recall Latency Memory Trade Off

Why can't the system just score every stored video? Because at web scale the per-query bill grows too large. Exact search is simple but slow, so the design trades a little result quality for large gains in speed and memory.

Assume a data lake of \(n\) videos with \(n = 10^{8}\), each stored as a vector of dimension \(d = 512\). Scoring one query against everything costs on the order of \(n \times d\) operations:

\[ \mathrm{cost} = n \times d \]

where \(n\) is the number of stored videos and \(d\) is the vector dimension. With \(10^{8} \times 512\) the bill is huge: about \(5.12 \times 10^{10}\) multiply-add steps per query, far beyond what an interactive page can wait for. Exact search that touches every entry cannot serve users.

A dictionary analogy shows the way out. To find opal in a printed dictionary, a reader jumps to O, then P, then A, then L, instead of reading page by page. To find scandalous, the reader reduces it to its base form scandal and then navigates, which is already an approximate step that still lands in the right neighborhood. In both cases the search space shrinks letter by letter while most pages are ignored. Retrieval needs the same shortcut.

The trade off is stated as recall against latency against memory: keep result quality high, answer fast, and use little memory. Recall (share of relevant items actually returned) measures quality. Latency (answer time) measures speed. Memory (index size in RAM) measures cost. That means arranging the search space so that only a small part is visited, accepting approximate search instead of exact search, and cutting the number of operations per query.

Picture three corners of a triangle labeled recall, latency, and memory. Pushing toward one corner pulls away from the others. Exact scan sits at the recall corner with poor latency and memory. A tiny compressed index sits at the memory corner with weaker recall. Production picks a point inside the triangle that users accept.

16.7.2 Offline Index, Online Query and Tree Structures

The practical split is offline against online. The index over stored vectors is built once in advance and kept ready: that is the offline part. The user query is encoded at run time and searched against the ready index: that is the online part.

The index over stored vectors is built once in advance and kept ready. That is the offline part. The user query is unknown ahead of time: one day the system is built around sunset examples and the user asks for sunrise, and it must still work. So the query is encoded at run time with a matching encoder, the same encoding recipe used for the index, then searched against the ready index with similarity sorted in descending order, and the returned identifiers are mapped back to actual videos. That is the online part.

One classical structure for the offline side is a tree such as a B tree or B plus tree, possibly combined with hashing, where each internal node summarizes what sits below it so that search starts at the root and keeps eliminating wrong branches until it reaches the leaf holding the wanted vector. The design choice among trees, hash tables, or hybrids stays open. A static prebuilt tree has one weakness: like a printed book index, it cannot easily accept new words or drop old ones, while the video lake changes all the time as videos are added and removed.

Think of the offline build as printing the dictionary and the online query as looking up one word. Printing is slow and done once. Lookup is fast and done millions of times. The lookup still works for a word the printers never saw as an example, such as sunrise after a sunset-built index, because the navigation rules generalize.

16.7.3 ANN Methods: IVF, HNSW and Compression

Because exact scan is out of reach and lazy per query learning is too slow for users who expect instant answers, the field uses approximate nearest neighbor search, abbreviated ANN and read as approximate nearest neighbor search, not neural search. KNN is the closest analogy for understanding it, but everything heavy is precomputed.

Three mechanism families are named. The baseline is a flat structure that stores vectors unchanged. It does not scale and serves only as the reference for judging other methods. The first real family is the inverted file index, IVF. The embedding space is split into Voronoi cells or Voronoi clusters, the same idea met in K-means, so that each cell holds similar points. A query probes only the nearest cells, which narrows search to the region with highest recall. The second family is graph based, with hierarchical navigable small world, HNSW, as the example. Nodes link to neighbors, and fewer hops mean more similar items while more hops mean farther items. Trees are graphs of this kind too, running from coarse levels down to fine levels. The third family is compression through quantization: vectors are shrunk so that they use less space, at the price of some lost detail, which is acceptable because matching is already approximate.

A Voronoi picture helps. Scatter stored points on a plane, mark one centroid per group, and draw a boundary halfway between neighboring centroids. Each cell holds the points nearest its centroid. A query lands in one cell, and search visits that cell plus one or two neighbors instead of the whole plane. A graph picture differs: each video is a node with links to close neighbors, and search walks from a start node toward ever-closer nodes, hopping across the graph.

A graph index works well but can be memory heavy when the whole structure must sit in memory for a very large collection. A system holding on the order of a hundred million 512 dimension vectors must use one of these approximate families; a flat scan is kept only for small tests and baselines.

16.7.4 Product Quantization and the Codebook

Product quantization, PQ, shows how compression works. A long vector is split into subvectors, written as \(SV_{1}, SV_{2}, SV_{3}\), and each subvector is quantized against a codebook, a reference book that maps ranges of values to short codes.

The textbook style example uses the number 173. Level A buckets values coarsely: 100 to 200 maps to code A1, 200 to 300 to A2, and so on. The value 173 falls in A1, as does 158, so one level cannot separate them. The residual remains: with base 100 the leftover is 73. That residual goes to level B, which buckets more finely, for example 70 to 79 maps to B7. The leftover after that, namely 3, goes to level C, which pins down the exact tail as C3. The stored code for 173 is then the concatenation A1, B7, C3. Forward mapping turns numbers into codes, reverse mapping turns codes back into approximate numbers by consulting the prebuilt book.

Worked codebook trace for 173: level A sees 173 in range 100 to 200 and emits A1 with residual \(173 - 100 = 73\). Level B sees residual 73 in range 70 to 79 and emits B7 with next residual \(73 - 70 = 3\). Level C sees tail 3 and emits C3. Stored code is A1, B7, C3. Reverse lookup reads A1 as base 100, B7 as plus 70, C3 as plus 3, rebuilding about 173. A neighbor such as 158 maps to A1, B5 or B6, C8, so it shares the coarse code but differs below. Sense-check: coarse plus residual codes separate close values while storing only a few bytes.

A second telling uses a five digit style number and bucket edges such as 0 to 10000 in A1 and \(10^{5}\) to \(10^{6}\) in A2: any value in a bucket maps to that bucket code, the base is subtracted to leave a residual, the residual maps to a B code, the next residual maps onward, and a fixed count of parts, for example four subvectors, gives the final short code. Only those few bytes are stored per item. In practice IVF and PQ are combined: IVF says where to look, by naming the approximate cells, and PQ says what is stored there, in compressed code form. The scan then runs fast and uses far less memory.

Picture IVF plus PQ as a post office: IVF picks the right delivery zone, PQ shrinks each parcel so the van carries far more of them. Zone choice keeps recall high, shrinking keeps memory low, and both together cut latency.

16.7.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Can a graph database hold the index, and what mapping is still needed?

A: Yes, a graph store can hold the structure, but the index alone is not enough. Search must return an identifier, and that identifier must map back to the video file. A plain store is also slower than a searchable structure, which is why a search oriented index is preferred and the identifier to video mapping is kept alongside it, for example in a JSON file.

Several students asked about codebooks in different words, so the repeats are merged into one canonical answer here.

Q: How does a codebook work when one level cannot separate two values?

A: The first level assigns a coarse bucket such as A1 for both 173 and 158. The base value is subtracted to leave a residual, here 73, which is bucketed at the next level as B7, and the remaining tail is bucketed after that as C3. The concatenated codes A1, B7, C3 identify the value to a close estimate, and the same book maps the codes back when needed.

16.7.6 FAISS as a Wrapper and a Concrete Flat Example

Facebook AI similarity search, FAISS, is the de facto standard wrapper. It is not itself a new index so much as a shell that builds an index from the corpus of \(z\) vectors, queries it with query vectors, searches partitions in parallel, and maps results back to files efficiently. Any underlying index can sit inside it: flat, PQ, HNSW, or others. The caller speaks only to the FAISS index. It returns distances \(D\), which carry similarity scores for ordering, and identifiers, which map to the top \(K\) videos. FAISS stores indexes and search structures, not the videos themselves, so identifier to video mapping lives outside it, in the example through a JSON file that pairs file names with identifiers.

Worked flat run: take 10000 clips, each as a 512 dimension L2 normalized vector \(z \in \mathbb{R}^{512}\). A flat index costs \(N \times D = 10^{4} \times 512 = \mathbf{5.12 \times 10^{6}}\) operations per query, which is fine at this size, with a JSON file holding the file name to identifier map. When a query arrives, the same text encoder maps it to \(z_{q}\), L2 normalization is applied, the index is searched for that \(z_{q}\) with \(K = 5\), cosine similarity is ranked in descending order with dot product as the implementation because vectors are normalized, the top identifiers come back, and the JSON map turns them into actual videos. Sense-check: five million multiply-adds run in milliseconds on modern hardware, while the hundred-million scale needs IVF or PQ.

16.7.7 Keeping the Index Fresh

A lake that grows and shrinks forces maintenance. For inserts, each new video is encoded with the same encoder, normalized, and added. In IVF the centroids stay fixed while new points arrive, so the Voronoi regions stay where they were even as the data distribution shifts, and quality degrades over time. Graph inserts are cheap, since adding a link is a small operation, but graphs bring their own difficulties.

Scope: for deletes, removing a vector from the structure is expensive, so the system uses tombstoning: the vector stays in the index but its pointer to the actual video is removed from the mapping table, leaving a grave marker that points nowhere. Many deletes leave many graves behind. Assumption: top \(K\) fetch uses oversampling plus compaction on a schedule. Without that, some of the \(K\) hits point at graves and the user sees fewer than \(K\) real videos.

Many deletes waste space and can break top \(K\) behavior, because some of the \(K\) hits point at graves, so the system must fetch extra candidates and compact the index regularly. Drift in IVF has one cure: periodic re indexing, which also clears tombstones. Two moments force a full re index: the encoder changes because a better one appeared, or the distribution shifts under a fixed IVF layout. Between rebuilds, production setups keep a day file and a night file. The night file is the frozen static index from the previous night. During the day, new arrivals are encoded into a small day delta file. Overnight, a scheduled maintenance folds the day delta into the night file, so the next morning starts from a fresh frozen index.

Picture the day file as a small notebook beside a large printed catalog. New titles go in the notebook during opening hours. Each night the staff reprints the pages that changed and starts fresh. The takeaway is that freshness comes from a cheap daily patch plus a regular full rebuild.

Recap: exact search costs \(n \times d\) and fails at scale, so IVF narrows where to look, PQ shrinks what is stored, FAISS wraps both, and day plus night files with tombstone compaction keep the index fresh.

16.8 Retrieval Augmented Generation and Evaluation

16.8.1 Why RAG

A language model alone can write fluent answers that are wrong. Retrieval augmented generation, RAG, fixes this by fetching evidence first and then writing: retrieve the segments relevant to the question from an index, use them as augmented evidence in a better prompt, and generate the answer from that evidence.

RAG stands for retrieval augmented generation: a generation step that draws power from a retrieval step, or a retrieval step embedded into generation. Four pains motivate it. First, private corpora stay unseen by public models: a large personal collection of dance performance videos cannot be answered from public pre training. Second, models freeze at a training cutoff and do not refresh every day. Third, models hallucinate: asked how many dogs are barking in a video with no dogs, a model may still answer five dogs barking, because it is trained to produce some answer. Fourth, retraining a heavy model for each small need is far too costly. The RAG answer is to avoid retraining and instead retrieve the segments relevant to the question from an index, use them as augmented evidence in a better prompt, and generate the answer from that evidence. For video question answering this bridge matters even more, because language models reason over text while videos are multimodal, so retrieval connects the two sides.

Think of RAG as an open-book exam. The closed-book model must answer from memory and may invent facts. The open-book model first pulls the right pages, keeps them on the desk, and then writes with sources in view. Hallucination drops because the answer must match the pages.

16.8.2 Two Video RAG Paths

Video RAG first cuts video into searchable chunks or clips that carry both visual and spoken word parts. Only the segments relevant to the question are retrieved, then fed to a pre trained language model that writes the answer. The retrieval half stays exactly as built so far: maintain a video index, accept a user query, return top segments as evidence. RAG then makes the language side reason better, giving stronger generation and grounded answers, so the dog counting question returns a value supported by evidence instead of a hallucinated count.

Two paths handle the modality gap. The cheaper and most common path transcribes and retrieves as text. Speech becomes written text with a tool such as Whisper, each frame or segment gets tags or captions from a model such as CLIP, timestamps are attached, and any text oriented model such as GPT-4, Llama, or Mistral can serve as the generator. The richer path uses visual tokens. Frames pass through a projector into the same token space the language model uses for text, which is the vision language model approach, also called a VLM path: embed the visuals, retrieve in that space, and let the model read visual tokens directly.

Path What is stored Generator reads Good for
Text transcribe Whisper text plus CLIP tags Words plus timestamps Speech, lyrics, music videos where words carry the answer
Visual tokens Projected frame tokens Image plus text tokens Answers that need visual richness

This path fits speech, lyrics, and music videos where words carry the answer, but visual clues can be lost. The choice follows the application: text path for cheap speech heavy tasks, visual token path for answers that need visual richness.

16.8.3 Chunking and Timestamps

Video is heavy, so the full recording never goes to retrieval or to the model at once. A long duration is cut into chunks, for example one chunk per ten seconds, and each chunk carries its timestamp. A chunk from 3 minutes 42 seconds is stored with that stamp, retrieved with that stamp, and shown to the model with that stamp, so the answer can point at an exact part and hallucination drops. The retriever in these setups is often CLIP with per frame captioning, or CLIP4Clip at video level. The vector store is often FAISS. Speech to text uses Whisper or a similar engine by default. The generator is any proprietary or open source language model. An orchestration tool such as LangChain or LlamaIndex ties the pieces into one pipeline, which sits outside the scope of this discussion.

Worked chunk trace: a forty-minute dance archive is cut into ten-second chunks, giving about 240 chunks. A question about a spin move retrieves the top three chunks with stamps 03:42, 14:05, and 31:50. The prompt shows the model those three stamped segments plus their CLIP captions. The answer cites 03:42 as the match. Sense-check: a ten second chunk with its timestamp is the unit that lets a long recording be searched and cited without rewatching the whole file.

16.8.4 Evaluation With Recall, Precision, MAP and nDCG

Retrieval is judged on its top \(K\) list, whether the query was text or video. Recall asks how many of the relevant items in the whole corpus appeared in the top \(K\). Precision asks what fraction of the retrieved \(K\) items was relevant. The denominator is the key difference:

\[ \mathrm{Recall} = \frac{\mathrm{relevant\ retrieved}}{\mathrm{all\ relevant\ in\ corpus}} \]

\[ \mathrm{Precision} = \frac{\mathrm{relevant\ retrieved}}{\mathrm{all\ retrieved\ (K)}} \]

where the numerators count retrieved items judged relevant by users, and the denominators differ as shown.

A worked example fixes the idea. Suppose the corpus holds 10 relevant videos. The system retrieves 5. Recall is \(5 / 10 = 0.5\), or fifty percent. Of those 5 retrieved, suppose 3 are relevant and 2 are not. Precision is \(3 / 5 = 0.6\), or sixty percent. The bike example in the discussion follows the same pattern: five retrieved with three relevant gives reduced precision, while a corpus with ten relevant and only five retrieved gives fifty percent recall.

Worked scores: suppose the corpus holds 10 relevant videos and the system retrieves 5 of them, so recall is 5 / 10 = 0.5, or fifty percent. Of those 5 retrieved, suppose 3 are relevant and 2 are not, so precision is 3 / 5 = 0.6, or sixty percent. Sense-check: recall rises only when more of the corpus relevant set is found; precision rises only when fewer of the \(K\) slots are wasted.

Mean average precision, MAP, averages precision across \(K\) values and is the go to choice when ordering must be judged. A list with the relevant item at rank one scores higher than the same item at rank five. For graded relevance, cumulative gain and normalized cumulative gain are used: a highly relevant hit counts more than a marginally relevant one, and the normalized form scales the sum to \([0, 1]\) against the ideal order. All of these rest on human judged relevance: semantic quality means the user says a video is relevant and the measure checks whether it was retrieved. Index recall is a different cut: given what the user called relevant, did the search structure actually return it. Because recall touches both semantic quality and index quality, it is the main go to measure, with precision, MAP, and related scores layered on top.

Scope: recall and precision judge the top \(K\) list, not the whole corpus order. MAP judges order across cut-offs. Graded gain judges partial relevance. Assumption: relevance labels come from users, not from the index itself. When labels are missing or noisy, every score above inherits that noise.

Picture a ranked list of five slots with relevant marks at positions 1, 3, and 4. Precision at 5 is 3/5. Average precision averages precision at each relevant rank: at 1 it is 1/1, at 3 it is 2/3, at 4 it is 3/4, giving about 0.81. The takeaway is that early relevant hits lift the average far more than late ones.
