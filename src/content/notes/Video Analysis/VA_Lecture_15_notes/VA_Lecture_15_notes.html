15.1 Dynamic Summarization and Video Skimming

15.1.1 Core idea and contrast with static summaries

A photo album tells you what happened. A trailer makes you feel how it happened. Which one does a video summary need to be?

A dynamic summary (a short video that still moves, with motion and sound kept) preserves the temporal order of events. A static summary (a storyboard or montage of picked still frames) freezes selected moments into pictures. The still form is cheap to browse but loses pace, rhythm, speech flow, and cause-and-effect order. The moving form costs more watch time but keeps action, dialogue continuity, and event sequence visible.

A video skim (a compact moving summary built by selecting short segments and joining them) is the main practical route to a dynamic summary. Building a skim raises two linked questions that structure this whole lecture. First, how do we select the exact segments to keep? Second, how do we merge the selected segments with a smooth transition so the result watches as one piece rather than a pile of clips?

Think of a skim like a movie trailer cut by a careful assistant. The trailer does not show every scene, but the scenes it shows still move, still speak, and still follow story order. A storyboard of the same film would show the same plot points as stills yet lose the chase speed, the joke timing, and the speech rhythm. That gap is why sports recaps, news digests, and course revision cuts all prefer skims when the viewer must feel the event order.

Intuition: a static summary is a list of landmarks on a map, while a dynamic skim is a short drive through the same town. The map shows where things are, but only the drive shows how far apart they feel, how fast the road runs, and which turn follows which. The analogy breaks in one place: a drive takes real time, so a skim must spend its time budget wisely, while a map can be scanned at any speed.

Picture the choice as a chart. The horizontal axis runs from short to long watch time, and the vertical axis runs from low to high temporal fidelity. Static storyboards sit at the bottom: almost no watch time, almost no motion fidelity. Full video sits at the top right: full fidelity at full cost. A skim sits in the middle: a short watch time with most of the motion fidelity kept. The one-sentence takeaway is that skims trade a little time for most of the motion meaning.

15.1.2 Segment scoring with collective importance

Selection rests on an importance score (a number, usually in \([0, 1]\), that says how much a frame or segment matters). For single frames the score blends sight, sound, and motion cues, much as static keyframe selection does. For segments the score is collective: either the segment gets its own direct score from a segment-level model, or the per-frame scores inside it are pooled into one segment score.

Formalize: pooled segment score. Let a segment \(S\) contain \(N\) frames with per-frame importance scores \(p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_N\), where each \(p_n\) is a scalar in \([0, 1]\) and larger means more important. The pooled segment score \(P(S)\) by plain averaging is:

\[ P(S) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} p_n \]

Here \(N\) is the frame count inside the segment, \(p_n\) is the importance of frame \(n\), and \(P(S)\) is the collective segment score. The plain average keeps one strong frame from hiding two weak ones and keeps one weak frame from hiding two strong ones. When sound is absent, each \(p_n\) is built only from the cues that exist, and the same average applies.

Selection then keeps the top segments, where the count follows the time budget. A ten minute or fifteen minute summary budget decides how many segments fit. Suppose five candidate segments each run three minutes and the budget is ten minutes. All five need fifteen minutes and cannot fit, so the selector ranks by \(P(S)\), keeps the top three (nine minutes total), and either drops the rest or compresses them. Length and importance are decided together rather than in isolation: a slightly weaker but much shorter segment can beat a strong but long one once time is counted.

Scope: pooled averaging applies when every frame inside the segment already carries a comparable importance score on the same scale. Assumption: frame scores are calibrated, so \(0.9\) in one segment means the same as \(0.9\) in another. When scores come from different detectors with different scales, normalize first or the average mixes apples and oranges. The average also smooths bursts: a segment with one brilliant second inside nine dull seconds scores low, which is correct for coverage but wrong when the task is pure excitement retrieval.

Visually, plot each candidate segment as a bar. The bar width is duration in minutes and the bar height is \(P(S)\). The budget is a fixed total width, say ten minutes. The task is to pack the tallest bars into that width. The takeaway is that skimming is a knapsack problem in disguise: value is importance, weight is time.

Short segments with one spike can fool a maximum-pooling rule, while long flat segments can fool a sum rule. Plain averaging sits between them, but it still undervalues a single brilliant moment buried in dull footage. A common trap is to rank segments before checking durations, then discover the top five do not fit the budget. Always rank under the budget constraint, not before it.

Worked example 1: budget fit. Five candidate segments each run \(3\) minutes, so all five need \(15\) minutes. The budget allows \(10\) minutes. Ranked pooled scores are \(0.92\), \(0.85\), \(0.81\), \(0.60\), and \(0.45\). Keep the top three: \(3 + 3 + 3 = 9\) minutes, total value \(0.92 + 0.85 + 0.81 = 2.58\). One minute of budget remains. When a fourth segment must stay, run its weakest minute at double speed so it costs \(0.5\) minutes, fitting inside the leftover minute. Answer: keep top three (nine minutes); compress rather than drop the fourth. Sense-check: nine minutes fits inside ten, and no kept minute was cut mid-sentence.

Worked example 2: pooling. Frames scored \(0.9\), \(0.7\), and \(0.8\) pool as:

\[ P(S) = \frac{0.9 + 0.7 + 0.8}{3} = \frac{2.4}{3} = 0.8 \]

Add the frame scores inside the segment (\(2.4\)) and divide by the frame count (\(3\)) to get \(0.8\). Sense-check: \(0.8\) lies between the minimum \(0.7\) and maximum \(0.9\), as any average must.

15.1.3 Playback speed and human involvement

By default many teams play every kept segment at the same speed. A richer option varies speed with content: less central parts run fast, for instance at double speed \((2\times)\), while central parts run at normal speed \((1\times)\). Fast-forwarding lets more ground fit the budget without dropping segments outright.

Variable speed needs judgment about what deserves full speed and what tolerates compression, so dynamic work involves people more than still-frame extraction does. Speech tolerates about \(1.2\times\) to \(1.5\times\) before comprehension drops, while calm scenery tolerates \(2\times\) or more. Most deployed dynamic pipelines are hybrid for this reason: the algorithm proposes segments and speeds, and a person adjusts which segments stay and at what speed they play. This professor analogy of fast-forwarding the dull drive and slowing down at the viewpoint captures the whole idea: spend full speed where it matters, compress where it does not.

Q: How do we decide how many segments to keep?

A: The time budget decides. Rank segments by collective importance \(P(S)\) and keep as many top segments as fit inside ten or fifteen minutes. Several students asked this in different words, and the answer is the same each time: count is not fixed, budget is fixed. When more ground must stay, raise playback speed on weaker parts instead of cutting them.

Exam note: expect a conceptual comparison of static and dynamic summaries, with segment scoring by pooled averaging and the time budget as the decision point. Be ready to compute \(P(S)\) for three frames and to explain why a skim preserves rhythm that a storyboard loses.

Recap and bridge: a skim keeps motion and order by ranking collective segment scores under a time budget, using speed as a second knob. Next, those kept segments must be joined without jarring jumps, which is where transitions and audio sync enter.

Sports recap reels, news digests, and course revision cuts all use skims so viewers feel the event order instead of flipping through stills. In video analysis at large, this is the entry point to the full pipeline: score, select under budget, join cleanly, and only then compress further with learning-based models.

15.2 Segment Transitions and Audio Sync

15.2.1 Joining segments with transitions

Cutting the best scenes together is only half the job. Why does a pile of great clips still watch badly?

Once segments are chosen they must be joined. The simplest join is a cut (a straight jump with no smoothing at all). Softer joins add a dissolve (one picture melting into the next over a few frames) or a fade (the picture dimming to black and back in). The same family of moves used between presentation pages applies between video segments: the editor picks per joint whether a hard cut or a soft blend serves the story better.

Think of joins like punctuation. A cut is a full stop between two sentences: sharp, fast, good for action. A dissolve is a comma or a semicolon: a soft glide, good for time passing or place changing. A fade is a chapter break: the screen goes dark and a new block begins. Picking the wrong mark does not change the words, but it changes how the reader feels the flow.

Join vocabulary. A cut keeps every kept frame and jumps directly from the last frame of segment \(k\) to the first frame of segment \(k+1\). A dissolve of length \(F\) frames blends the tail of segment \(k\) with the head of segment \(k+1\) as a weighted mix that slides from one to the other. A fade passes through black (or white) between the two. Hard cuts preserve duration exactly; soft blends consume a few frames of overlap and need that overlap reserved at selection time.

Picture a timeline with two kept blocks separated by a removed middle block. A cut snaps the two block edges together with a visible edge. A short dissolve draws a small cross-faded bridge, perhaps \(6\) to \(12\) frames, where both pictures are half-visible. The takeaway is that the bridge hides the time that was removed, at the cost of a brief double image.

15.2.2 Audio sync and the mid-sentence trap

When the source has no sound, joining is purely visual. When sound is present, the sound must stay lined up with the picture. This bites hardest in news and documentary material, where the picture can change while a sentence continues. Cutting out a dead minute, for instance a pause spent fixing the microphone, is only safe at a sentence or idea boundary.

Scope: every cut in a talking video must be checked against the spoken line, not just the pictures. Assumption: sentence and idea boundaries can be found from captions or speech pauses. When no speech-to-text captions exist, run speech detection first; cutting blind on video activity alone is unsafe. A cut in the middle of a sentence is a clear failure signal: the viewer hears half a thought and loses trust in the skim. A cut in the middle of an idea is subtler but still harmful, because the viewer cannot reconstruct the full point even when each audible sentence is whole.

Imagine the audio waveform under the video timeline. Sentences appear as dense speech lumps separated by short silences. Safe cut points sit in the silences at sentence ends. A cut drawn through the middle of a lump slices the waveform mid-word, and the viewer hears a chopped syllable. The one-sentence takeaway is to cut in the gaps, never through the lumps.

Beginners watch the pictures and forget the soundtrack. A visually dull minute with important speech looks removable but is not. Another trap is trimming inside an idea: each sentence survives, yet the connecting explanation is gone, so the conclusion dangles. Always replay the joined audio before locking the skim.

Q: Can we cut wherever the picture looks dull?

A: No. When speech is present, the cut must respect sentence and idea boundaries. A mid-sentence cut breaks the line and advertises poor work, so audio awareness is part of every join decision. The professor flagged this as the classic giveaway of a careless skim: one half-sentence tells the viewer the editor never listened.

15.2.3 Worked Examples

Worked example 1: dead-minute removal. A sixty second pause for microphone setup sits between two complete explanations. Sentence one ends at time \(t = 120\) s, the pause runs \(120\) to \(180\) s, and sentence two starts at \(180\) s. The safe cut removes exactly \([120, 180]\) s at the pause edges and joins explanation to explanation with a short \(8\)-frame dissolve. Both sentences stay whole, and the dissolve hides the missing minute. Answer: cut pause edges only; keep both sentences intact. Sense-check: replay shows no chopped word at the joint.

Worked example 2: unsafe cut. A forty second sentence runs \(t = 0\) to \(40\) s. Removing the middle twenty seconds \([10, 30]\) s shortens the segment to twenty seconds but leaves half a sentence: the subject without its verb. The fix is to widen the cut to the full sentence boundary \([0, 40]\) s (drop the whole sentence) or keep the sentence and trim a different, complete sentence elsewhere. Answer: never cut inside \([0, 40]\) s; cut at \(0\) or \(40\) s. Sense-check: any kept audio must parse as a complete thought.

Exam note: audio sync faults, especially mid-sentence cuts, are a favorite short-answer trap. Be ready to spot the fault in a described cut and to state the boundary fix in one line.

Recap and bridge: joins are punctuation plus audio discipline: cut, dissolve, or fade per joint, always at sentence or idea edges. Once clean joining is understood, the next question is how the segment scores themselves can be learned instead of hand-set.

Documentary editors and news producers check each join against the spoken line before locking the skim. In broadcast practice this audio-first check is what separates a watchable digest from a clip pile.

15.3 Supervised and Unsupervised Skim Learning

15.3.1 Learning setup and supervision paths

What if the machine could learn what matters instead of us hand-writing the importance rule?

Skim generation can be framed as learning from examples. A supervised setup (training with paired full videos and their target skims, or at least per-frame or per-segment importance scores to train against) learns a map from input frames to importance. The learner takes frames in and predicts importance out, and the target scores teach it what matters. The snag is annotation scarcity: hand-scored videos and hand-built skims are rare, and pulling dense scores out of an existing skim is itself hard work, because a binary keep-or-drop skim does not say how important each kept second was.

An unsupervised setup (grouping without any hand labels) needs no annotations. Simple grouping tools such as k-means (an algorithm that clusters items into \(K\) groups by closeness, written \(K\) for the group count, e.g. \(K = 5\)) or hierarchical clustering do the job, provided the closeness measure reflects importance rather than raw pixels. Grouping raw pixels clusters by color and texture, which is useless for skims; grouping importance features clusters by what matters. The open problems then shift to picking \(K\), adapting it per video rather than fixing one \(K\) for all videos, and checking the result against human taste, which still differs from algorithm taste.

Two supervision paths. Supervised: input frames \(X\) map to predicted importance \(\hat{p}\) trained against hand labels \(p\), with label cost high and ranking quality high when labels are plentiful. Unsupervised: frames map to groups by distance in importance-feature space with no labels, with label cost zero and ranking quality uncertain until a person checks it. The feature choice matters more than the clustering routine itself.

Picture label cost on the horizontal axis and ranking trust on the vertical axis. Unsupervised sits bottom-left: free but untrusted. Supervised sits top-right: trusted but costly. The empty middle is where practical systems live, which motivates semi-supervised and weakly supervised training later.

Scope: supervised training applies when at least a small set of densely scored videos or paired skims exists in the target domain. Assumption: hand scores generalize across videos in that domain. When the new footage comes from a different domain (say a model trained on news applied to sport), the assumption breaks and scores mislead. Unsupervised grouping applies anywhere, but its assumption that closeness in feature space equals closeness in importance must be validated per video.

A common trap is training a supervised model on a tiny labeled set and trusting it everywhere. With twenty labeled videos the model memorizes them and stumbles on the twenty-first. The opposite trap is running k-means on raw pixels and expecting story-aware groups. Both failures come from the same root: the wrong input representation for the supervision level.

15.3.2 Why hybrids dominate

Scores learned without people often rank different moments highly than viewers would. Because the machine view and the viewer view diverge, deployed systems keep a person in the loop to confirm or repair the machine ranking. Supervised models suffer the same gap from the other side: with enough labels they learn fast, but labels are costly and viewer taste still drifts by show and by viewer. That tension motivates the middle ground covered later: semi-supervised and weakly supervised training, plus personalized ranking.

Q: Why not always train supervised skim models?

A: Labels are the bottleneck. Paired full videos with target skims or dense importance scores are scarce and costly to make, so unsupervised grouping plus human review often ships first. Several students pressed this point, and the professor answer stayed constant: annotation cost decides, not model cleverness.

Exam note: be ready to contrast supervised label cost against unsupervised ranking risk in one paragraph. Name k-means and the feature-versus-routine point: importance features in, story-agnostic pixels out.

Recap and bridge: supervision is a cost-versus-trust trade, and hybrids rule because neither extreme is affordable and trustworthy at once. Next, one concrete unsupervised route makes the feature point tangible: clustering over importance triplets.

Archival news summarizers start unsupervised with k-means over importance features, then add hand labels only for the highest-value shows. That staged spend, free first pass plus costly labels only where value justifies it, is the standard industry pattern.

15.4 Clustering with Triplet Importance Scores

15.4.1 Mathematical Formulation

K-means is only as smart as the numbers you feed it. What should a frame look like as a number?

Each frame is described by three cue scores: a visual score, an audio score, and a motion score. Write the visual score of frame \(i\) as \(s_i^{v}\), the audio score as \(s_i^{a}\), and the motion score as \(s_i^{m}\), where each of \(s_i^{v}\), \(s_i^{a}\), and \(s_i^{m}\) is a scalar in \([0, 1]\) with larger meaning more important. The frame then becomes the triplet \((s_i^{v}, s_i^{a}, s_i^{m})\). Think of the triplet like a three-subject report card: vision, sound, and movement each get a mark, and the frame identity is the three marks together.

A simple pooled frame score averages the three cues:

\[ s_i = \frac{s_i^{v} + s_i^{a} + s_i^{m}}{3} \]

Here \(s_i\) is the pooled frame importance, a scalar in \([0, 1]\). The verbal rule is "add the three cue scores for the frame and divide by three." When sound is absent, the average runs over the cues that exist, for instance \((s_i^{v} + s_i^{m})/2\) for a silent clip.

Formalize: Euclidean distance over the triplet. Closeness between two frames \(i\) and \(j\) uses Euclidean distance over the triplet. The verbal rule is "take the per-cue gaps between frame \(i\) and frame \(j\), square each gap, add the squares, and take the square root":

\[ d(i, j) = \sqrt{(s_i^{v} - s_j^{v})^{2} + (s_i^{a} - s_j^{a})^{2} + (s_i^{m} - s_j^{m})^{2}} \]

Here \(d(i, j)\) is a scalar gap with \(d(i, j) \ge 0\), small when the two frames matter in the same mix of ways. This is the ordinary Euclidean distance equation applied to the new triplet feature vector rather than to pixels. Self-checks: distance is zero when triplets match exactly; it is symmetric with \(d(i,j) = d(j,i)\); and each output lies in \([0, \sqrt{3}]\) since each cue gap lies in \([-1, 1]\). A tiny spot-check: triplets \((1, 0, 0)\) and \((0, 1, 0)\) give \(d = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\), large as expected for opposite strengths.

K-means then clusters these triplet points: it picks \(K\) centers, assigns each frame to its nearest center by \(d(i, j)\), moves each center to its group mean, and repeats. Feature choice matters more than the clustering routine itself, which is the professor intuition to carry forward: the same k-means on pixels versus on triplets gives garbage versus story-aware groups.

Scope: the triplet average assumes the three cues share one scale and deserve equal weight. Assumption: each cue score is calibrated to \([0, 1]\) with larger meaning more important. When one cue is noisier than the others, use a weighted average instead of \(1/3\) each. When sound is missing, average only over present cues rather than inserting zero, or silence drags every score down unfairly.

Picture the triplets as points in a unit cube. Each axis runs \(0\) to \(1\) for one cue. Exciting multimodal moments sit near the \((1, 1, 1)\) corner; dull moments sit near \((0, 0, 0)\). K-means draws boundaries through this cube, and Euclidean distance is the ruler. The takeaway is geometric: close in the cube means similar in importance mix, regardless of what the pixels look like.

Beginners run Euclidean distance on raw pixels and wonder why day and night shots of the same event never group. Pixels measure appearance, not importance. Another trap is fixing \(K\) once for all videos: a quiet lecture needs few groups while a football match needs many, so adapt \(K\) per video and check groups against viewer judgment.

15.4.2 Worked Examples

Worked example 1: distance between two frames. Frame \(i\) has triplet \((0.9, 0.2, 0.6)\) and frame \(j\) has \((0.7, 0.4, 0.6)\). Step 1, gaps: visual \(0.9 - 0.7 = 0.2\), audio \(0.2 - 0.4 = -0.2\), motion \(0.6 - 0.6 = 0.0\). Step 2, squares: \(0.04\), \(0.04\), \(0.00\). Step 3, sum: \(0.04 + 0.04 + 0.00 = 0.08\). Step 4, root:

\[ d(i, j) = \sqrt{0.08} \approx 0.283 \]

Answer: \(0.283\). The pair is close, so k-means may place them in one group. Sense-check: two of three cues nearly agree, so a small distance below \(0.3\) is plausible.

Worked example 2: pooling then grouping. Frames \(A = (0.8, 0.8, 0.7)\) and \(B = (0.85, 0.75, 0.8)\) pool to \(s_A = (0.8 + 0.8 + 0.7)/3 = 2.3/3 \approx 0.767\) and \(s_B = (0.85 + 0.75 + 0.8)/3 = 2.4/3 = 0.80\). Their gap is:

\[ d(A, B) = \sqrt{(0.05)^{2} + (0.05)^{2} + (0.10)^{2}} = \sqrt{0.015} \approx 0.122 \]

A third frame \(C = (0.1, 0.2, 0.1)\) sits at distance over \(1.0\) from both, so it forms its own group. Answer: \(A\) and \(B\) group together near \(0.77\) to \(0.80\); \(C\) separates. Sense-check: the pooled values confirm the geometric closeness.

15.4.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Must we use Euclidean distance on raw pixels for k-means?

A: No. Pixels mislead because two shots of the same event can look totally different while mattering equally, and two similar-looking shots can matter very differently. Use the importance triplet \((s_i^{v}, s_i^{a}, s_i^{m})\) or the pooled importance \(s_i\) as the feature, and use the Euclidean gap between those scores as the grouping criterion. Feature choice matters more than the clustering routine itself.

Exam note: expect a numerical on triplet Euclidean distance with all intermediate squares shown. Write gaps, squares, sum, and root on separate lines; markers are lost when steps are skipped.

Recap and bridge: triplets turn each frame into an importance point, Euclidean distance groups similar points, and k-means proposes the skim groups. Graphs offer a second unsupervised route that replaces centers with hubs, which comes next.

Rush-review tools cluster meeting footage by pooled audio-visual-motion scores before a person picks groups for the skim. The same triplet idea appears across video analysis whenever appearance must be replaced by importance as the grouping signal.

15.5 Graph-Based Frame Similarity and Centrality

15.5.1 Mathematical Formulation

What if important frames are the popular ones, the frames that look like many others?

A frame similarity graph (a network whose nodes are frames and whose links join look-alike frames) offers another unsupervised route. Start from per-frame scores that blend the available cues (visual plus audio plus motion, or visual plus audio when motion is missing). Put down one node per frame, for instance eight nodes for an eight-frame toy video. Draw an undirected link between two frames when their similarity is high, where high means above a chosen threshold \(\tau\), for instance \(\tau = 0.8\). Links run both ways: when frame \(i\) resembles frame \(j\), frame \(j\) resembles frame \(i\), so the graph is undirected rather than directed.

Formalize: similarity graph and centrality. Let similarity \(w(i, j)\) be a scalar in \([0, 1]\) built from the blended frame scores, with larger meaning more alike (for instance \(w(i,j) = 1 / (1 + d(i,j))\) from the triplet distance \(d(i,j)\)). Draw an undirected edge between \(i\) and \(j\) when \(w(i, j) \ge \tau\). A centrality score (a number saying how well connected or central a node is, for instance its degree or its random-walk score) then ranks nodes. The top-central nodes and the frames linked to those hubs join the skim. Graph construction is the hard step; ranking after that is routine.

Think of each frame as a person at a party and each link as a mutual friendship. The most connected people are the hubs everyone resembles. Picking hubs plus their close friends gives a guest list that represents the whole party. The analogy breaks where time matters: at a real party friends gather in one room, while in video two look-alike frames can sit an hour apart and must not be merged blindly.

Once the graph stands, standard network ranking applies. Degree centrality counts links; fancier scores weigh who the links connect to. Either way the output is a ranked list of frames, and the skim keeps the top of that list up to the budget.

Scope: centrality ranking applies after a trustworthy similarity graph exists. Assumption: the similarity \(w(i,j)\) reflects shared importance, not just shared color. When similarity is built on raw appearance, hubs become the most repetitive shots (long static takes) rather than the most important moments. Threshold \(\tau\) is the control knob: too low and everything links into one mush; too high and the graph fractures into isolated nodes with no hubs.

Picture eight nodes in a row in time order, with curved links jumping between look-alikes. Two nodes sprout many links: the hubs. The takeaway is visual: hubs are dense stars in the link pattern, and the skim is the hubs plus their neighbors.

A classic trap is tuning the ranker while the graph is wrong. No centrality score rescues a graph built on the wrong features. Another trap is reading directed meaning into undirected links: resemblance has no arrow, so algorithms that assume direction misfire here.

15.5.2 Ordering problem and temporal attention

Graphs forget order, which creates a real failure. Picture a news show: an anchor speaks for the first twenty frames, the show cuts to a landmark for one hundred frames, then returns to the same anchor. Frame one and frame one hundred twenty look nearly identical and link strongly, even though they sit far apart in time. A pure look-alike graph merges them and scrambles the story order, keeping one anchor shot where two distinct appearances belong.

The repair folds time into the features. A spatial attention weight (emphasis on where to look inside the frame, such as the anchor face versus the studio wall) plus a temporal attention weight (emphasis on when to look, including timestamp gaps between frames) separates same-looking frames that occur at different times. Concretely, multiply or append a time penalty so \(w(1, 121)\) drops when \(|t_1 - t_{121}|\) is large. So the graph keeps its power while the order of events survives, and the skim places one anchor shot at the opening and one at the close instead of merging both ends.

15.5.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: When frames link by similarity, how does the final skim keep story order?

A: Plain similarity alone cannot keep order, because distant look-alike frames link together and collapse into one hub. The fix builds timestamp and temporal attention into the frame features before links are drawn, so far-apart twins no longer count as identical. The skim then keeps both appearances in their true positions.

The same confusion returns in a second form, so the professor answered it once more from the symmetry side.

Q: Why is the graph undirected?

A: Resemblance runs both ways. When frame \(i\) resembles frame \(j\), frame \(j\) resembles frame \(i\) by the same token, so each link is bidirectional. There is no direction of liking here, unlike a follower graph, which is why undirected ranking is the right tool.

15.5.4 Worked Examples

Worked example 1: anchor twin. Frames \(1\) through \(20\) show the anchor, frames \(21\) through \(120\) show the landmark, and frame \(121\) returns to the anchor. Look-alike score gives \(w(1, 121) = 0.95\), above \(\tau = 0.8\), so a plain graph links them. Adding a timestamp gap penalty multiplies by \(0.5\) for gaps over \(100\) frames, giving \(0.475 < 0.8\), so the link drops. Answer: with time folded in, the skim keeps one opening anchor shot and one closing anchor shot in order. Sense-check: the story needs both ends, which the repaired graph preserves.

Worked example 2: threshold choice. With eight frames there are \(8 \times 7 / 2 = 28\) pairs. With \(\tau = 0.8\), twelve pairs link and two hubs emerge with degrees \(6\) and \(5\). With \(\tau = 0.95\), only three pairs link, degrees collapse to \(1\) or \(0\), and the ranking fractures into noise. Answer: tune \(\tau\) until hubs match viewer judgment, here near \(0.8\). Sense-check: a good \(\tau\) leaves a few clear stars, not one mush and not dust.

Exam note: the anchor-returns ordering trap is a classic explain-why question. State the failure (distant twins merge), the cause (graphs forget time), and the fix (timestamp plus temporal attention in features before linking).

Recap and bridge: similarity graphs find hubs, but only time-aware similarities keep story order. Next, sequence models add a different pressure: picks must be both important and varied.

News digest builders use centrality on similarity graphs to pull anchor and field shots in story order. The same time-aware graph idea returns wherever repeated settings must not collapse across a long timeline.

15.6 Saliency, Autoencoders and Sequence Loss Design

15.6.1 Diversity versus importance

The ten most exciting seconds of a match might all be the same goal from ten angles. Is that a good summary?

A good skim is both important and varied. News, documentary, and game coverage all need range: not just goals but crowd shots, sideline moments, and turning points. Picking the ten highest scores without a variety pressure returns near-duplicates of one peak.

An autoencoder (a network that squeezes input down to a compact code and rebuilds the input from that code) helps here because its compact code keeps the most telling and most varied aspects of the footage. Frames that rebuild well yet differ from each other score on both coverage and variety. Think of the code like a small suitcase: only the most useful and most different clothes fit, so packing forces both importance and range. The analogy breaks in that a suitcase holds objects while a code holds patterns, but the forcing effect is the same.

15.6.2 Mathematical Formulation

Sequence models for skims adjust the usual training loss. The verbal rule is "keep the rebuild error between predicted and actual values, then add diversity and representativeness terms with balancing weights."

Formalize: three-term sequence loss. Write rebuild loss as \(L_{rec}\), diversity loss as \(L_{div}\), and representativeness loss as \(L_{rep}\), each a scalar with lower meaning better. With balance weights \(\lambda_{div} \ge 0\) and \(\lambda_{rep} \ge 0\) the training objective is:

\[ L = L_{rec} + \lambda_{div} L_{div} + \lambda_{rep} L_{rep} \]

Here \(L_{rec}\) penalizes poor reconstruction of frames, \(L_{div}\) penalizes picking near-duplicate frames, and \(L_{rep}\) penalizes leaving some video region uncovered. The verbal reading is "total loss equals rebuild loss plus weighted diversity loss plus weighted representativeness loss." The weights act as regularizers in the speed-limiter sense: raise \(\lambda_{div}\) and the model spreads picks apart; raise \(\lambda_{rep}\) and it covers each part of the video. Setting both weights to zero recovers plain reconstruction; raising them trades fidelity for range and coverage.

Generation uses a bidirectional LSTM (a sequence model reading frames forward and backward, so each decision sees past and future context) in a many-to-many shape: frames \(I_1, I_2, \ldots, I_T\) enter in order and per-frame outputs \(Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_T\) leave in the same order, with each \(Y_t\) mapped to a keep score for the summary. Here \(T\) is the frame count, \(I_t\) is frame \(t\), and \(Y_t\) is its keep score in \([0, 1]\). The top scores up to the budget mark the kept segments.

Scope: the three-term loss applies when training a sequence scorer that must balance fidelity, variety, and coverage. Assumption: each loss term is on a comparable scale, or the weights \(\lambda_{div}\) and \(\lambda_{rep}\) absorb the scale gap. When \(L_{div}\) is naturally ten times larger than \(L_{rec}\), a weight of \(0.5\) still dominates; normalize terms first. Raising diversity too far yields varied but unimportant picks, while raising representativeness too far yields even but dull coverage.

Picture total loss as three stacked bars per training step: a rebuild block, a diversity block scaled by \(\lambda_{div}\), and a coverage block scaled by \(\lambda_{rep}\). Turning the \(\lambda_{div}\) knob visibly stretches the diversity block and pushes picks apart on the timeline. The takeaway is that the weights are the director controls for taste: more spread versus more coverage.

Beginners set both weights by guess and train once. Tune them on a validation set and watch which failure appears: duplicates mean \(\lambda_{div}\) is too low, while gaps in coverage mean \(\lambda_{rep}\) is too low. Another trap is reading \(Y_t\) as a hard decision; it is a score, and the budget plus threshold turns scores into keeps.

15.6.3 Worked Examples

Worked example 1: loss balance. With \(L_{rec} = 0.40\), \(L_{div} = 0.30\), \(L_{rep} = 0.20\), \(\lambda_{div} = 0.5\), and \(\lambda_{rep} = 0.5\), total loss is:

\[ L = 0.40 + 0.5 \times 0.30 + 0.5 \times 0.20 = 0.40 + 0.15 + 0.10 = 0.65 \]

Raising \(\lambda_{div}\) to \(1.0\) lifts the total to \(0.40 + 0.30 + 0.10 = 0.80\) and pushes the model toward more varied picks. Answer: \(0.65\), rising to \(0.80\) with stronger diversity pressure. Sense-check: each term is non-negative and the weighted sum exceeds \(L_{rec}\) alone, as required when both weights are positive.

Worked example 2: many-to-many scoring. Eight frames \(I_1\) through \(I_8\) enter the bidirectional model and eight scores leave, say \(Y = [0.91, 0.20, 0.85, 0.30, 0.88, 0.15, 0.40, 0.35]\). The top three scores are \(0.91\) at \(t = 1\), \(0.88\) at \(t = 5\), and \(0.85\) at \(t = 3\). Answer: keep frames \(1\), \(5\), and \(3\) in timeline order \(1, 3, 5\). Sense-check: three keeps from eight inputs matches the budget, and keeps spread across the timeline rather than bunching.

15.6.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why is skim building called hybrid even with strong models?

A: Machine ranking and viewer taste diverge. Frames the model rates highly may leave viewers cold, because the loss rewards reconstruction and spread while viewers reward story and feeling. So a person reviews and repairs the machine shortlist before release, which keeps the pipeline hybrid no matter how strong the sequence model is.

Exam note: be ready to write the three-term loss and explain each weight in words. Name what rises when each \(\lambda\) rises: diversity spreads picks, representativeness covers regions.

Recap and bridge: sequence loss trades rebuild quality against variety and coverage under two weight knobs, with a bidirectional reader scoring every frame. When even that machinery starves for labels, the middle-ground training regimes take over.

Match-day editors let the model propose varied candidate moments, then a person locks the aired cut. That propose-then-lock loop is the deployed form of the three-term loss: the machine optimizes range, the human certifies taste.

15.7 Semi-Supervised and Weakly Supervised Training

15.7.1 Middle ground between labels and no labels

Full hand labels cost too much and no labels trust too little. What lives between them?

When full hand labels are scarce but some labels exist, two middle paths help. Semi-supervised learning (mixing a small labeled set with a large unlabeled set) trains on the labeled part and predicts on the unlabeled part, or mixes models with stand-in merging of predictions, or creates pseudo-labels (machine-made stand-in labels for the unlabeled part, kept only when confident) and then trains a supervised model on labels plus pseudo-labels. Transformer pipelines often use the first move: a small supervised warm start, then prediction over the large pool.

Weakly supervised learning (training on rough labels that come free with the video) uses titles, tags, comments, or category names. These hints are noisy and vague: a tag may name the event without marking its timestamp, and a comment may praise a moment without locating it. So the model must tolerate mismatch, for instance by treating tags as bag-level hints rather than frame-level truth. Even so, weak hints beat no hints across large collections, because thousands of noisy tags outweigh dozens of exact labels for representation learning.

Two middle grounds. Semi-supervised mixes a few exact hand labels with many unlabeled videos and predicts or pseudo-labels the rest: exact but scarce plus plentiful but unscored. Weakly supervised reuses free but rough hints such as titles, tags, comments, and category names: plentiful but noisy, with no timestamps. The first needs confidence filtering on pseudo-labels; the second needs noise tolerance on weak tags.

Picture a spectrum from zero labels to dense labels. Unsupervised sits at zero, weakly supervised a step up with free rough tags, semi-supervised further up with a few exact labels plus a large pool, and fully supervised at the far end. Cost rises along the spectrum while noise falls. The takeaway is to pick the cheapest point that still trains: start weak, add a few exact labels, and only go fully supervised for the highest-value shows.

Scope: semi-supervised applies when a small trusted labeled set plus a large same-domain unlabeled pool both exist. Assumption: confident pseudo-labels are mostly correct. When the base model is weak, confident mistakes reinforce themselves, so filter hard and re-check on held-out hand labels. Weak supervision applies when free metadata exists at scale; its assumption is that tag noise averages out, which breaks for rare events with few tags.

A common trap is keeping every pseudo-label: low-confidence machine guesses become training truth and the model drifts. Keep only the most confident slice and retrain. The weak-label trap is treating a video-level tag as a frame-level label, so every frame of a goal-tagged video is marked goal. Use bag-level training instead, letting the model find which frames carry the tag.

15.7.2 Benchmark datasets and domain shift

Two public collections anchor tests. TVSum suits news, documentary, sport, and instructional clips with shot-level importance scores, while SumMe offers several hand-made summaries per video from different people, which supports cross-checking against diverse viewer taste. Both give benchmark numbers, yet a model tuned on them may stumble on new footage because the new domain differs in pace, camera work, and audio mix. So teams report benchmark scores and then test domain transfer to their own videos before trusting the model: a news-tuned scorer rarely transfers untouched to cricket highlights.

15.7.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: What is the difference between semi-supervised and weakly supervised skim training?

A: Semi-supervised mixes a few exact hand labels with many unlabeled videos and predicts or pseudo-labels the rest, filtering by confidence. Weakly supervised reuses free but rough hints such as titles, tags, comments, and category names, and builds noise tolerance because those hints lack exact timestamps. One is exact-but-scarce plus unlabeled; the other is free-but-noisy.

15.7.4 Worked Examples

Worked example 1: pseudo-label loop. Train on \(200\) hand-scored clips. Predict scores on \(2000\) unscored clips. Keep the most confident \(1000\) as pseudo-labels (top half by confidence). Retrain on \(200 + 1000 = 1200\) clips. Answer: the labeled set grows sixfold at the cost of some noise. Sense-check: held-out hand labels must improve after retraining, or the confidence bar was set too low.

Worked example 2: weak tag use. Videos tagged goal supply rough positive hints at video level. The model learns goal-like audio-visual patterns (cheer plus replay plus score graphic) from thousands of such videos, then a small hand-scored set of \(50\) videos sharpens exact start and end boundaries. Answer: weak tags teach the look and sound; few exact labels teach the edges. Sense-check: boundary error should fall after the sharpening stage.

Exam note: contrast exact-but-scarce labels with free-but-noisy tags in one short paragraph. Name TVSum and SumMe, plus the domain-transfer warning: benchmark scores do not guarantee transfer.

Recap and bridge: middle-ground training buys labels cheaply through confidence or through free metadata, always validated by transfer tests. Next, the goal of training itself splits: fair summaries versus exciting highlights, each with its own domain cues.

Large video archives bootstrap with tags and titles, then refine with a small curated labeled pack. That bootstrap-then-refine order is the practical recipe whenever annotation budgets are tight.

15.8 Summary versus Highlight and Domain Cues

15.8.1 Core distinction

Should a recap cover everything fairly or replay only the moments viewers love?

A summary (a preview of the whole video) values representativeness (fair coverage of everything, where each part of the source appears in proportion) and ships at a fixed size, such as a ten minute cut or fifteen percent of the source length. A highlight (a reel of the most engaging moments) chases viewer satisfaction (engagement, excitement, and memorable moments) and ships at variable length, because satisfaction has no fixed runtime: a dull game yields a short reel while a classic yields a long one.

Summary versus highlight. Summary: goal is fair coverage, length fixed by budget or compression share (say \(15\%\)), success measured by coverage of all parts. Highlight: goal is excitement and replay value, length variable with the moments found, success measured by viewer response. A sport summary covers both sides evenly even when one side lost; a sport highlight leans toward the moments viewers replay, usually goals, cards, and turning points from the winning side.

Think of a summary like balanced news reporting and a highlight like a fan scrapbook. The reporter must cover every quarter of the game; the fan keeps only the pages worth re-reading. The analogy breaks on evaluation: coverage can be checked against the source, while satisfaction can only be checked against viewers.

Picture length on the horizontal axis and excitement on the vertical axis. Summaries sit on a vertical line at the fixed budget: same length whatever happened. Highlights scatter along the axis: short for dull games, long for classics. The takeaway is that fixed size signals coverage duty while variable size signals excitement duty.

Scope: use a summary when the viewer missed the video and needs the whole story fast. Assumption: fair coverage is definable per domain, which holds for sport and news but strains for open-ended vlogs. Use a highlight when the viewer saw the event or wants only thrills; its assumption is that engagement signals (replays, cheers) mark the right moments, which fails for instructional content where the driest minute may be the exam hint.

Beginners treat the two as synonyms and then argue about length. Length is the symptom, goal is the cause: fixed length follows from coverage duty, variable length follows from excitement duty. Another trap is judging a highlight by coverage ("it skipped the first half") or a summary by thrills ("it kept dull parts"): each fails the other test by design.

15.8.2 Sports cues are multimodal

Sport is rule-based, so key moments have known shapes: scores, fouls, red and yellow cards. Three cue families combine. Visual cues include the ball path, the goal or basket, replays, and the visible scoreboard. Audio cues include crowd cheer for a home score and groans or boos for a miss. Text cues include scrolling tickers and on-screen score graphics. When all three fire at once, replay plus cheer plus score graphic, the segment is almost certainly a keeper, because three independent witnesses agree.

15.8.3 Surveillance cues are anomaly cues

Surveillance sits at the other extreme: no fixed rules, only deviation from normal. Watched events include left-behind objects, stationary people or vehicles, traffic violations, and crowds scattering after an alarm such as fire. Events are rare against hours of calm, so data is heavily imbalanced: thousands of normal hours against seconds of incident. Needs differ too. Live or near-live online processing matters more than in sport, where few viewers demand highlights in real time when they could watch the game itself. Privacy handling matters because subjects are watched for safety rather than broadcast with consent, so faces and plates need care that sport footage never needs.

15.8.4 Meeting and instructional cues

Meeting and instructional videos are an active research and product area: many tools now draft a textual recap right after the call ends. Minutes need who said what and when, plus decisions, owners, and deadlines. Key cues include page or slide change from one presented page to the next, writing captured by text recognition on boards, and above all speech: exam hints or action items often live only in the spoken line, never on any page. So speech analysis carries more weight here than in sport, where audio-visual cues alone often suffice. Missing one spoken sentence can lose the only record of a deadline.

15.8.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: How does a sport summary differ from a sport highlight in size and goal?

A: A summary aims at fair coverage at a fixed size such as ten minutes or a set compression share, covering both sides evenly. A highlight aims at viewer satisfaction and engagement, so its length varies with the exciting moments found, leaning toward replayed moments. Fixed size signals coverage; variable size signals excitement.

Exam note: the summary-versus-highlight split by goal and length is highly testable. State both halves in one line each, then map sport, surveillance, and meeting cues: multimodal agreement, anomaly deviation, and speech-plus-slide change.

Recap and bridge: coverage duty means fixed summaries, excitement duty means variable highlights, and each domain supplies its own cue family. Next, sport itself splits further: each sport scores differently and fuses cues at a different stage.

Broadcast sport desks air fixed-length previews plus variable-length exciting-moment reels; safety teams scan surveillance streams for rare anomalies; meeting tools draft minutes with speakers, decisions, and deadlines. Each product is the same skim machinery pointed at a different cue family.

15.9 Sport-Specific Scoring and Multimodal Fusion

15.9.1 Goal shapes across sports

A goal is a goal, right? Why does cricket refuse to behave like football?

Scoring shapes differ by sport. Football and basketball score through one fixed target, a goal mouth or a basket, and the basket is the easier target to spot because it is small and distinct, while a goal mouth filmed in close-up without all posts is hard to read. Tennis scores two ways: a clean winning shot, or an opponent error that gifts the point, with no single target to watch, so the scorer must read rally shape rather than target entry. Cricket is the hardest: whether a boundary matters depends on the chase and the final result, and the winning side reshapes which innings deserves emphasis. A team-A win lifts team-A moments above team-B moments for highlight purposes, while a neutral summary still covers both sides evenly. In cricket the textual score line often outweighs sound and pictures when deciding what enters the highlight, because only the score context says whether a four changed the game.

Sport-to-cue map. Football and basketball: fixed-target visual cues (goal mouth, basket) plus aligned cheer and replay. Tennis: rally-shape cues with two point types (winner versus forced error) and no fixed target. Cricket: context-heavy cues where the score line leads and audio-visual cues confirm. The same cheer means different things per sport, which is why one scorer never fits all sports.

Picture each sport as a detector shape. Football draws a rectangle to find: the goal mouth. Basketball draws a small ring: the basket. Tennis draws a rally curve with two endings. Cricket draws a score-context curve where identical strokes score differently by match state. The takeaway is that the visual target shrinks and the context weight grows along that list.

Scope: fixed-target detectors apply to football and basketball where goals pass through a known object. Assumption: the target is visible at decision time. Close-up filming that crops the posts breaks the assumption, so the detector must fall back to replay plus crowd plus score graphic. Tennis and cricket never satisfy the fixed-target assumption, so they must start from rally and score context instead.

A common trap is training one goal detector on football and aiming it at cricket boundaries. The visual pattern transfers but the meaning does not: a boundary in a lost cause is not a highlight. Another trap is reading tennis errors as winners; the point counts the same on the board but the highlight value differs, and viewers remember winners.

15.9.2 Context dependence with worked numbers

Importance depends on what came before. An eight-goal football game supplies eight strong segments; cards add variety, with red above yellow and the guilty player identity adjusting weight. A nil-nil game supplies no goals, so tries, blocks, and saves rise in value. The model must learn that tries rank below goals and red cards rank above yellow cards without being told the rule each time, which means training data must show both high-score and scoreless games.

Worked example 1: high-score game. Five goals for one side and three for the other give eight core segments. Add one red card as a ninth pick for variety. Yellow cards join only when space remains or a star player is involved, since routine yellows add little replay value. Answer: eight goals plus one red card form the nine-piece core. Sense-check: variety enters only after all top-tier moments are kept.

Worked example 2: scoreless game. Zero goals means the candidate pool becomes ten tries and six strong blocks. Rank by crowd plus motion response and keep the top five tries and top two blocks for a seven-piece reel. Answer: tries and blocks replace goals one-for-one under the same budget. Sense-check: the reel stays exciting even with no goals because the next-best cue family fills the gap.

15.9.3 Fusion choices

Cue fusion follows three patterns. Early fusion (merging audio, visual, and text features first and feeding one joint vector to the model) suits football and basketball because goal cues align in time: replay, cheer, and score graphic arrive together. Timestamp alignment is the make-or-break step: misaligned features drop goals even when every cue fired. Late fusion (training separate per-cue models, scoring each cue stream with its own timestamps, then merging the score lists) suits cricket because the textual score line leads while sound and pictures follow at their own delays. Hybrid fusion (merging at several stages at once) is tuned per domain and even per sub-domain, since football and cricket want different merge points.

Comparison in one table: early fusion merges features first, needs tight alignment, and fits synchronized cues; late fusion merges scores last, tolerates loose timing, and fits leader-follower cues; hybrid fuses at several stages and fits mixed domains at higher tuning cost. Pick early when cues arrive together, late when one stream leads, hybrid when the domain mixes both patterns.

Q: When should fusion happen early rather than late?

A: Fuse early when cues arrive together, as with a goal where replay, cheer, and score graphic align in time, so one joint vector sees the full moment. Fuse late when one stream leads, as with cricket where the score line decides and the other streams confirm at their own timestamps, so each stream is scored separately first. Timestamp alignment decides: tight alignment allows early fusion, loose timing forces late fusion.

Exam note: map each sport to its fusion pattern with the alignment reason stated. Football and basketball go early (synchronized cues); cricket goes late (score line leads); mixed domains go hybrid.

Recap and bridge: sport shapes decide the target, context decides the value, and timing decides the fusion stage. The last step is personal: whose excitement counts, and how the full line from raw video to shipped file runs.

Goal detectors fuse replay, crowd sound, and score graphics jointly for football; cricket highlight stacks score-text models above audio-visual confirmers. That pairing is the deployed memory hook for early versus late.

15.10 Personalized Highlights, Transformers, LLM Pipelines and End-to-End System

15.10.1 Personalized and user-centric highlights

Two fans watch the same match and want different reels. Whose taste should the cut follow?

A general highlight serves every viewer the same cut. A personalized highlight (a cut per viewer, learned from that viewer history) serves each viewer their own cut, learned from explicit feedback (likes, dislikes, and ratings on past highlights, where the viewer states taste directly) and implicit feedback (whether the viewer finished the clip, quit early, or replayed it, where behavior reveals taste without words). The task then blends highlight building with recommendation: predict the moments this viewer will value from how they treated past moments.

Think of explicit feedback like a restaurant rating card and implicit feedback like watching which dishes a guest finishes or reorders. The card states taste; the plate reveals it. Both feed the next menu, but the plate never lies about appetite. The analogy breaks where taste shifts: a viewer who loved goals last season may chase saves this season, so the model must keep learning rather than freezing a profile.

Personalization inputs. Explicit signals: likes, dislikes, star ratings per highlight. Implicit signals: completion (finished means valued), early quit (means bored or misled), replay (means loved). The model maps viewer history plus candidate moments to per-viewer keep scores, turning one general ranking into many personal rankings.

Picture each viewer as a weighting over moment types: goals, saves, crowd shots, analysis. Replays push their type weight up; skips push it down. The takeaway is that personalization reweights a shared scorer per viewer rather than training one model per viewer from scratch.

Scope: personalization applies when per-viewer history exists at usable volume. Assumption: past watch behavior predicts future taste. New viewers with no history need a general cut first (the cold start), and drifting tastes need recent history weighted above old history. Implicit signals also carry noise: a quit may mean a phone call, not boredom, so aggregate over many sessions before concluding.

A common trap is reading every replay as love and every quit as hate. Replays can mean confusion ("what just happened?") and quits can mean interruption. Combine explicit and implicit signals, and down-weight single events. Another trap is pure personalization with no general fallback: the first session for a new viewer must still ship something good.

15.10.2 Hierarchical attention over video structure

Video has a natural ladder: frames group into shots (continuous runs between boundaries such as indoor to outdoor cuts), shots group into scenes (larger blocks such as pre-match versus post-match), and scenes group into the full video. Instructional single-camera sessions may show only flat frames with no real shots, while news and documentary show the full ladder.

Attention then runs at two levels: frame-level attention ranks frames inside each shot, and shot-level attention ranks shots against each other. A transformer block with multi-head attention, feed-forward layers, residual links, and positional codes carries timestamps through the stack, so order survives and deeper stacks learn finer taste. In the football illustration, shot-level attention lifts true goals above tries and idle running, even when a try outscores a goal at frame level, because the shot context corrects the frame ranking.

Imagine grading essays inside folders. Frame attention grades sentences inside each essay; shot attention grades essays against each other. A brilliant sentence in a weak essay still loses to a strong essay, which is exactly how a dazzling try loses to a true goal once shot context is weighed.

15.10.3 Language model routes

Two practical routes use modern models. Route one feeds frames to a pre-trained video language model such as VideoLLaMA or VideoChat and either takes its summary directly end to end or fine-tunes it on local clips and reads frame scores out. Route two captions each frame with an image caption model such as BLIP, pools frame captions into shot captions with timestamps, and feeds that text to a text language model with retrieval support that returns both a textual recap and the timestamps of the top frames; a small window around each timestamp (say two seconds each side) then maps back to video segments. A text query with retrieved video pieces, for instance asking how to prepare a fresh dish from its raw base and getting the matching steps, extends the same machinery toward query-driven recap, with clip scoring deferred to the retrieval module.

Pick route one when video-language models cover the domain and compute allows; pick route two when text models plus captions are cheaper or when a searchable textual recap is itself a deliverable. Both routes still end at timestamps plus margins, which is where the video pieces come from.

15.10.4 Full pipeline and post-processing

The end-to-end line runs in order: raw multimodal video (sight plus sound plus text) passes cleaning such as resizing, noise removal, and stabilization; shot detection splits the stream into shots; feature extraction pulls spatial, temporal, audio, and textual cues under the chosen early, late, or hybrid merge; the summarization model scores and keeps the top \(K\) segments where \(K\) follows the length rule; post-processing smooths joints, removes blur, jitter, and flicker artifacts, and formats the file container such as MP4; the final cut ships and its index feeds later search and generation stages.

Most of post-processing is automatic: soft transitions such as fades and dissolves, plus removal of blur, jitter, and flicker, run as code. People step in for sense-level transitions: title cards such as few moments later or one year later that join two hard cuts with a meaning bridge rather than a visual blend. Visual blending is routine code; a meaning bridge with words on screen still needs a person to write the bridge and place the two surrounding cuts.

Q: Is post-processing automatic or does it need people?

A: Most of it is automatic: soft transitions such as fades and dissolves, plus removal of blur, jitter, and flicker, run as code. People step in for sense-level transitions, title cards such as few moments later or one year later that join two hard cuts with a meaning bridge rather than a visual blend. Several students asked this alongside the stitching question, and the split is the same: pixels automatic, meaning manual.

Visual blending between kept frames is routine code: a fade or dissolve is a fixed pixel mix. A second student question pressed whether full stitching matches manual editing, and the answer holds the same line.

Q: Can stitching between frames be automated like manual editing?

A: Visual blending can. A fade or dissolve between kept frames is routine code. A meaning bridge with words on screen still needs a person to write the bridge and place the two surrounding cuts, because only a person knows what the jump means.

A third practical question asked how to start from open pre-trained sport or news models on an isolated machine without network access.

Q: How hard is it to start from open pre-trained sport or news models on an isolated machine?

A: Start from a pre-trained video model, run a short clip through it for a baseline recap, and fine-tune on local clips. The pipeline above shows where each choice lands: features and merge pattern first, then scoring and top \(K\), then post-processing. Even offline, the same staged order applies, with local clips replacing any cloud data.

15.10.5 Worked Examples

Worked example 1: top \(K\) from timestamps. A text model returns frame timestamps 00:04:12, 00:21:03, and 00:55:47 with keep scores \(0.93\), \(0.88\), and \(0.85\). Adding a two second margin each side gives pieces [00:04:10, 00:04:14], [00:21:01, 00:21:05], and [00:55:45, 00:55:49], twelve seconds total. Join the three with dissolves into the final skim. Answer: three timestamped pieces plus margins form the skim. Sense-check: pieces stay in time order and total duration fits a short budget.

Worked example 2: feedback update. A viewer replays two goal pieces and skips two crowd pieces. History weights shift to goals \(0.9\) and crowd \(0.2\). The next personalized cut for that viewer keeps both goal pieces, drops one crowd piece, and shortens the other to half length. Answer: goals stay, crowd shrinks. Sense-check: the next reel matches revealed taste, not the general ranking.

Exam note: reproduce the pipeline order and place top \(K\), fusion choice, and post-processing correctly. The order is clean, split into shots, extract and merge features, score and keep top \(K\), smooth and format, ship and index.

Recap and bridge: personal taste reweights shared scores, hierarchical attention respects the frame-shot-scene ladder, language models supply timestamps, and the pipeline turns raw video into a shipped, searchable cut. This closes the lecture arc from segment scoring to deployed system.

Recap products chain BLIP captions with a text model for searchable summaries; editing helpers auto-apply dissolves while editors write meaning bridges. Personalized feeds blend explicit ratings with implicit watch, quit, and replay signals on top of the same line.

15.4 Clustering with Triplet Importance Scores

Must-know: Cluster frames by Euclidean distance over the (visual, audio, motion) importance triplet, not pixels.

\[ d(i, j) = \sqrt{(s_i^{v} - s_j^{v})^{2} + (s_i^{a} - s_j^{a})^{2} + (s_i^{m} - s_j^{m})^{2}} \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Running Euclidean distance on raw pixels instead of importance triplets; fixing K for all videos
