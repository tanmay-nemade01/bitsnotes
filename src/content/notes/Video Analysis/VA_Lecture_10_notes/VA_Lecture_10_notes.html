10.1 Autoencoder Foundations for Unsupervised Learning

An autoencoder (a network that learns to copy its input through a narrow middle layer) answers a simple question: what can we learn from data that has no labels at all? If the only task is "rebuild what you saw, through a code far smaller than the input," the code is forced to keep what matters.

An embedding (a short vector that keeps the main structure of the input, written \(z\)) is the product of this training. We feed data in, squeeze it to a small code, rebuild from that code, and lower the rebuild error. When the rebuild is good, the code has captured the shape of the data. That code then serves later classifiers, search systems, and video encoders in this lecture.

Think of packing for a long trip with one small bag. You cannot carry the whole wardrobe, so you pick items that mix and match into many outfits. The bag is the bottleneck. The outfits you can still assemble are the rebuilds. A good packer learns what is versatile. An autoencoder learns the same lesson with numbers.

10.1.1 Mathematical Formulation

An encoder (the first half of the network that makes the data smaller) maps an input vector \(x\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{N}\) to a bottleneck vector \(z\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\). Here \(N\) is the number of input features, \(\mathbb{R}^{N}\) means a real-valued vector of length \(N\), \(d\) is the code size with \(d\) much smaller than \(N\), and \(z\) is the embedding. A decoder (the second half that builds the data back up) maps \(z\) back to a rebuild \(\hat{x}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{N}\), where the hat means predicted or rebuilt. Written as maps:

\[ z = f_{enc}(x) \]

\[ \hat{x} = f_{dec}(z) = f_{dec}(f_{enc}(x)) \]

Here \(f_{enc}\) is the encoder map from \(\mathbb{R}^{N}\) to \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) and \(f_{dec}\) is the decoder map from \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) to \(\mathbb{R}^{N}\).

In plain words before the equation: actual minus predicted gives the error at each entry, square each gap so big misses hurt more than small ones, add them up, and divide by the count so clips of different sizes stay comparable. The rebuild form is:

\[ L_{MSE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_{i} - \hat{x}_{i})^{2} \]

Here \(x_{i}\) is the \(i\)-th entry of the true input, \(\hat{x}_{i}\) is the \(i\)-th entry of the rebuild, \(N\) is the count of entries, \(L_{MSE}\) is the mean squared error, and the sum runs over \(i\) from \(1\) to \(N\). When this loss trains an autoencoder we call it reconstruction error (how far the rebuild is from the source).

Training aims for:

\[ \hat{x} \approx x \]

Here \(\approx\) means about equal. As training lowers \(L_{MSE}\), the gap between \(x\) and \(\hat{x}\) shrinks entry by entry.

The shape rule is strict: if the first layer has \(N\) units, the last layer must also have \(N\) units, because \(x\) and \(\hat{x}\) are compared entry by entry. The middle layers shrink step by step. A pattern stated in the session is \(N\), then below \(N/2\), then below \(N/4\), then below \(N/8\), down to a bottleneck (the smallest layer that holds the embedding), after which the network stops shrinking and starts growing again. Only the ends are fixed by the loss; the hidden widths, depths, and the bottleneck size \(d\) are design choices.

A tiny numeric check makes the loss concrete. Suppose \(N = 3\) with \(x = [2, 4, 6]\) and \(\hat{x} = [1, 4, 8]\). Then the gaps are \(1\), \(0\), and \(-2\). Squaring gives \(1\), \(0\), and \(4\). The sum is \(5\) and the mean is \(5/3 \approx 1.67\). So \(L_{MSE} \approx 1.67\). The third entry dominates because squaring punishes its gap of \(2\) four times as hard as a gap of \(1\).

Picture the loss surface for one weight: the horizontal axis is the weight value and the vertical axis is \(L_{MSE}\). The curve is a bowl with one low point where the rebuild matches the input. The takeaway is that every training step rolls the weights downhill toward that low point, and the bottleneck decides how close to the bottom the network can ever get.

Scope: This squared-error form fits continuous values such as pixels and marks. It assumes gaps in every entry count equally and that large gaps deserve quadratic penalty. Assumption: Entries are on comparable scales. If one feature ranges over thousands while another ranges over fractions, the large feature swamps the loss unless inputs are normalized first.

10.1.2 Worked Examples and Intuition

Take a tiny image of size 3 by 3, which is 9 pixels and so 9 features. Set \(N = 9\). The input layer has 9 units and the output layer also has 9 units. Build the encoder as 9 to 4 to 2: the first hidden layer holds 4 units (near \(N/2\)) and the bottleneck holds 2 numbers (near \(N/4\)). The embedding size is 2, far below 9. Build the decoder growing back, for one variant as 2 to 5 to 9. Every unit in one layer joins every unit in the next. From the 2-number code the network must rebuild all 9 pixels, so those 2 numbers must encode edges, brightness, and rough layout. Sense-check: 2 numbers cannot store 9 independent pixels, so training succeeds only when the 9 pixels share structure the code can exploit.

A one-dimensional story shows the use. Suppose we collect past marks in a video analysis course with no labels. We want two things: to grasp the mark spread and to make new synthetic marks that follow the same spread. Feed the marks in, squeeze to a small code, rebuild the marks, and lower the rebuild error. The small code then holds the shape of the spread — where marks cluster, how wide they scatter, whether there are two humps. From that code we can learn the spread and draw new samples. The same idea scales to frames: the code holds what repeats across frames so later heads can classify with few labels.

The session's copy analogy earns its place here. If a person copies handwriting, small shifts appear in loops and slants. If a decoder copies a money note, fine print blurs. The gap between source and copy is the signal, and shrinking that gap forces the middle code to keep what matters. Where the analogy breaks: a human copier can look back at the source at test time, but the decoder sees only the code — there is no peeking.

Variants serve distinct needs. One variant mirrors the decoder shape to the encoder shape for symmetry. One variant ties the weights so decoder weights form the transpose of encoder weights, which halves the parameters and acts as a regularizer. One variant uses convolution layers in encoder and decoder for images so nearby pixels share weights. For now the plain multilayer perceptron form is enough. A multilayer perceptron (a stack of fully joined layers with nonlinear units, with no convolution and no recurrent links) is the running example. Depth and width stay with the builder.

A narrow bottleneck keeps only strong structure but may starve the rebuild. A wide bottleneck rebuilds easily yet may learn a near-copy with no useful compression. A missing nonlinearity is a classic trap: a fully linear autoencoder with squared error merely recovers a principal-component subspace, so depth without activation adds no new expressive power.

10.1.3 Design Choices and Variants

Loss choice stays simple even though learning has no labels: learning without labels still needs an objective function, and mean squared error is that objective here. Actual is the input \(x\). Predicted is the rebuild \(\hat{x}\). Feed \(x\) in, run all steps, get \(\hat{x}\) out, take the gap, square, sum, average, and update weights to lower it. Because the target comes from the input itself, this is self-supervised rather than unsupervised in the strictest sense — the supervision signal is free.

Choose depth and width deliberately. Start with the \(N\) to below \(N/2\) to below \(N/4\) shrink as a baseline, then shrink the bottleneck until the rebuild just starts to degrade; that knee marks the smallest code that still captures the data. Prefer tied or convolutional weights when data is scarce, since fewer free parameters mean less overfitting. Keep the plain fully joined design while learning the core, then move to convolutional or transformer encoders for real video.

Civil engineers compress bridge sensor streams the same way: hundreds of strain readings per second squeeze through a small code, and a spike in rebuild error flags damage. Streaming services compress watch-history vectors into short codes for recommendation. In video analysis, teams pre-train the bottleneck code on unlabeled frames and reuse it as input to later classifiers.

Recap: An autoencoder copies its input through a narrow code, and the squared rebuild gap trains it. The code is the prize; the rebuild is only the teacher. Bridge: With this copying machine in hand, the next step hides most of the input and forces the machine to reason about time — the masked video pipeline. Exam note: State the first-layer equals last-layer rule, write the mean squared form with symbol meanings, and explain why the bottleneck code is the product of training.

10.2 Masked Sequence Modeling and VideoMAE Pipeline

What if the network may look at the past but never at the future? When we are at word two of a sentence, we know words one and two but not word three. A masked autoencoder (an autoencoder trained on partly hidden input) turns that everyday reading limit into a training task, and VideoMAE (video masked autoencoder, with V for video, M for masked, A for autoencoder, and E for encoder in the naming used here) applies it to video tubes.

The core shift from 10.1 is that future steps stay hidden while past and nearby steps stay seen. The network must guess hidden motion from visible context, which forces it to learn time flow rather than single-frame texture.

10.2.1 Mathematical Formulation

Write a video clip as an ordered list \(x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots, x_{T}\), where each \(x_{t}\) is the token for time \(t\) and \(T\) is the count of steps. When the model sits at \(x_{2}\), it sees data from \(x_{1}\) to \(x_{2}\) only, and later steps stay masked. Positional order is therefore part of the input, not an afterthought.

For video the token is not a single number. The token is a tube (a small space-time block cut from the clip). Each tube has width, height, and time depth. Let a tube be \(u\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{h \times w \times \tau}\), where \(h\) is height in pixels, \(w\) is width in pixels, \(\tau\) is time depth in frames, and \(\mathbb{R}^{h \times w \times \tau}\) means a three-way array of that size. Let the rebuild be \(\hat{u}\) of the same size, so input tube and output tube match entry by entry.

In plain words: we rebuild pixel values in each frame, tube by tube, and the squared gap is summed over every tensor entry. The tube loss form is:

\[ L_{tube} = \sum_{p} (u_{p} - \hat{u}_{p})^{2} \]

Here \(u_{p}\) is the true pixel value at tensor position \(p\), \(\hat{u}_{p}\) is the rebuilt pixel value at the same position, and the sum runs over all positions \(p\) in the tube. The full clip loss sums this term over tubes, optionally divided by the count of masked entries to keep the scale steady across masking rates.

Frame size must match on both ends. If the input frame is 640 by 480, the rebuild frame must also be 640 by 480. If the input is a tube, the output is a tube of the same shape. The only hard link between encoder and decoder is this size match — the decoder need not mirror the encoder layer by layer, which is why a heavy transformer encoder can pair with a light decoder.

Picture the masking grid: the horizontal axis is space (tube position across the frame) and the vertical axis is time (tube position across frames). Shaded cells are seen tubes; blank cells are hidden. The takeaway is that the encoder reads a sparse scatter of shaded cells while the decoder must fill the whole sheet.

Scope: This loss assumes pixel rebuild is a good teacher for motion. It works when hidden tubes are predictable from visible context, which holds for natural video with smooth motion. Assumption: Masked positions are the only ones scored; seen tubes are not penalized, so the network cannot earn credit by copying visible pixels.

10.2.2 Worked Pipeline and Intuition

Run the full pipeline on a short clip. First, tubelet extraction (cutting the clip into small 3D patches) turns the clip into a grid of tubes. Next, tube masking (hiding a large share of those patches, about 90 percent) keeps roughly 1 tube in 10. Send only the seen tubes into a Vision Transformer encoder. A Vision Transformer encoder (a transformer that reads image or video patches with self-attention) builds features from seen parts only, so compute drops nearly tenfold. Add learned mask tokens to the encoder output. A mask token (a stand-in vector that marks a hidden spot) tells the decoder where to fill. Send the mix into a light decoder — it need not match the Vision Transformer design; any light decoder works. The decoder outputs rebuilt pixels, and the squared gap on hidden tubes is the training signal. Sense-check: if the rebuild of a hidden running figure looks sharp, the encoder must have encoded leg motion, not just background color.

Think of reading with most words blacked out. To fill the gaps the reader must grasp time flow, not just single frames — grammar, story, and cause carry the guess. That pressure is the point. The network must learn how pixels move and change to guess hidden tubes. Where the analogy breaks: a reader uses a lifetime of language, while the network starts from random weights and earns its grammar purely from pixel gaps.

Sports archives use this pipeline to pre-train on thousands of unlabeled match hours, then fine-tune a small head for goal or foul labels. Wildlife camera teams do the same for rare-animal clips where labels are few but raw footage is plenty.

Recap: Cut the clip into tubes, hide most of them, encode only the seen few, then rebuild hidden pixels with a light decoder. Bridge: The next step counts exactly how few tubes the encoder sees and explains why video tolerates such extreme hiding. Exam note: List the order clip to tubelets to masking to encoder to mask tokens to decoder to pixels, and state that the encoder sees only seen tubes.

10.2.3 Student Questions and Practical Notes

Q: Can we change the build so the network rebuilds tokens from the encoder instead of rebuilding pixels?

A: Hold the full story first. To get an error we need a target, and here the target is pixels. The decoder must stay because the rebuild error needs images: training runs on the pixel rebuild error, which is mean squared error. After that base is set we can debate token targets — predicting features instead of pixels is a real research variant. But without the image target the error has no ground, and the encoder gets no signal. Several students raised versions of this mix-up, so remember the split: tokens are the input cut, pixels are the rebuild target, and the decoder exists to make the pixel target so the encoder learns.

10.3 Tubelets, Ninety Percent Masking and Temporal Redundancy

Why can we throw away 9 tubes out of 10 and still learn? A tubelet (a small 3D patch with width, height, and frame depth) is the unit of masking, and temporal redundancy (repeat of nearly the same pixels across time) is why heavy masking works. Video holds much repeat — walls, grass, and studio desks barely change — so hiding most tubes still leaves enough clues.

10.3.1 Mathematical Formulation

Let frame height be \(H\), frame width be \(W\), and clip length be \(F\). Let tubelet height be \(h\), tubelet width be \(w\), and tubelet time depth be \(\tau\). The count of tubelets is about \((F / \tau) \times (H / h) \times (W / w)\), where each ratio is rounded down to whole patches. Each factor counts how many tubelets fit along one axis, and the product counts the 3D grid.

The session works an example with 16 frames and 14 by 14 spatial patches with a time depth of 2. First compute the time factor: \(16 / 2 = 8\). Then the count form is:

\[ N_{tok} = 8 \times 14 \times 14 \]

Here \(N_{tok}\) is the total count of tubelet tokens. Step by step: \(14 \times 14 = 196\) spatial patches per time slab, and \(8 \times 196 = 1568\). So:

\[ N_{tok} = 1568 \]

In words: eight time slabs times fourteen by fourteen spatial patches give one thousand five hundred sixty eight tokens. This matches the direct reading of the session numbers, and the arithmetic checks out exactly.

With masking rate \(r = 0.90\), where \(r\) is the share hidden, the hidden count is:

\[ N_{hide} = 0.90 \times 1568 = 1411.2 \approx 1411 \]

Here \(N_{hide}\) is the count of hidden tokens, about one thousand four hundred eleven (rounded down to a whole tube). The seen count is:

\[ N_{seen} = 1568 - 1411 = 157 \]

Here \(N_{seen}\) is the count of tokens the encoder sees, about one hundred fifty seven. So the encoder sees only 157 tubes while 1411 stay hidden — roughly 1 in 10.

A second tubelet size stated in the session is 4 frames by 16 pixels by 16 pixels. In symbols a tubelet is \(u\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{16 \times 16 \times 4}\), where 16 is the spatial size and 4 is the time depth.

Picture a bar chart of the 1568 tubes: a thin shaded slice of 157 seen tubes against a tall bar of 1411 hidden tubes. The takeaway is that compute follows the thin slice while the learning target covers the tall bar.

Scope: The 90 percent rate fits slow, redundant footage where neighbors predict each other. Assumption: Masking is uniform over the grid so hidden tubes are scattered, not wiped out in one corner. If masking clumps, whole moving objects vanish and the rebuild becomes guesswork.

10.3.2 Worked Examples and Intuition

Picture a cartoon chase with a cat and a mouse. In one frame the cat is left and the mouse is right. Most of the frame is still background: wall, floor, and sky change little from frame to frame. News clips and nature clips behave the same way — the anchor's desk stays fixed while only lips move, and a forest stream keeps its banks while only water ripples. Many tubelets stay static and only a few tubes hold motion. With 157 seen tubes scattered at random, several still land on the cat, the mouse, and their motion trails, which is enough to infer the chase. Sense-check: 157 clues for 1411 gaps sounds thin, but because background repeats, most gaps are answered by "same as neighbor."

That is why 90 percent masking forces time reasoning. If most static tubes are hidden, the network cannot just copy neighbors. It must learn what moves, why a pixel value changes, and how past frames shape next frames. The hidden code must hold time context, not just space texture.

A second check uses frame gaps. When a person stands still, frame gaps stay low. When the person starts to run, gaps spike. When the person keeps running, gaps stay high but less spiky than at onset. When the person stops, gaps spike again. Heavy masking pushes the network to learn these onset, steady, and offset patterns from very few seen tubes — the same peak shapes that later drive start and end detection in 10.7.

Do not confuse spatial masking with temporal masking: hiding random patches in one frame teaches texture, while hiding whole tubes across frames teaches motion. A common trap is lowering the rate for "safety" — at 50 percent the network coasts by interpolation and learns weak time features. Another trap is keeping 90 percent on fast, dense motion (a basketball scramble), where nearly every tube changes and the guess becomes noise.

Sports video, street video, and studio news video all show strong time repeat, so teams use high masking both to cut encoder compute nearly tenfold and to push time learning.

Recap: 1568 tubes split into 1411 hidden and 157 seen, and redundancy makes that split learnable. Bridge: Next we train on this hiding game with no labels at all, then keep only the encoder for real tasks. Exam note: Compute hidden and seen counts from a masking rate, explain temporal redundancy with a still-background example, and link 90 percent masking to pressure for time reasoning.

10.3.3 Masking Rate Notes

The rate is not magic. Ninety percent fits video because repeat is high. Images need less masking — masked image models typically hide about 75 percent of patches — because a single frame holds less repeat. If motion is fast and dense, a lower rate such as 75 to 80 percent may fit better since little repeats. If the view is fixed and motion is sparse, a higher rate can still work. The choice should follow the data, not habit: measure motion density first, then set the rate.

10.4 Training Without Labels, Reuse of Encoder and Variational Contrast

Can a network learn from clips nobody labeled? Yes — if the task is rebuilding hidden pixels, the pixels themselves are the teacher. Training uses unlabeled clips; testing may still use labels for scoring, but encoder learning sees no labels. After training we throw the decoder away for recognition tasks and keep the encoder. A variational autoencoder (an autoencoder whose code must follow a set normal shape) adds a second loss that forces that shape.

10.4.1 Mathematical Formulation

Let \(x\) be an unlabeled clip and \(\hat{x}\) its pixel rebuild. The rebuild loss is the mean squared form from 10.1: actual minus predicted, squared and averaged. Both encoder and decoder update together to lower it.

For the variational form, let the encoder output distribution be \(Q\), where \(Q\) means what the network learns for the code, with mean \(\mu\) and spread \(\sigma\). Here \(\mu\) is the average and \(\sigma\) is the spread. Let the reference be \(P = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), where \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) means standard normal with mean zero and spread one. In words: we force the bottleneck to follow a standard normal with mean zero and unit spread, and we compare the learned spread to that reference.

The gap between spreads uses Kullback-Leibler divergence (a score for how far one spread is from another), written \(D_{KL}(Q \| P)\), where \(Q\) is the learned spread, \(P\) is the reference spread, and \(\|\) means divergence from \(Q\) to \(P\). Total variational loss is:

\[ L_{VAE} = L_{recon} + L_{KL} \]

Here \(L_{recon}\) is the rebuild loss, \(L_{KL}\) is the spread-match loss \(D_{KL}(Q \| P)\), and \(L_{VAE}\) is the sum used for training. No \(L_{KL}\) term appears for a plain autoencoder.

For a Gaussian code the spread term has a closed form per dimension. With learned mean \(\mu\) and learned spread \(\sigma\):

\[ L_{KL} = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j} \left(1 + \log(\sigma_{j}^{2}) - \mu_{j}^{2} - \sigma_{j}^{2}\right) \]

Here \(j\) runs over code dimensions and \(\log\) is the natural log. A quick spot-check: if \(\mu_{j} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{j} = 1\), each term gives \(1 + 0 - 0 - 1 = 0\), so a code that already matches the standard normal pays nothing. If \(\sigma_{j}\) shrinks toward zero, \(\log(\sigma_{j}^{2})\) dives negative and the penalty grows — the loss resists collapsing the code to a point.

Scope: The normal-shape demand suits generation, where we later draw random codes and decode them. Assumption: Code dimensions are treated as independent Gaussians. If the true data shape is strongly multimodal (two far-apart clusters), one Gaussian prior squeezes them together and rebuilds blur.

10.4.2 Worked Training Story and Intuition

Train for many passes, with two thousand epochs given as one training example. Each pass feeds unlabeled clips, masks most tubes, rebuilds pixels, and lowers the rebuild error. Both encoder and decoder update together during training — picture two partners where one codes and one decodes, and the coder must write so the decoder can rebuild. After training ends, remove the decoder for recognition use. Deployment is then simple: send a full clip into the kept Vision Transformer encoder, get the code out, and feed that code into a small head for the task at hand. The head may be a linear classifier, a shallow network, a support vector machine, or a nearest-neighbor voter. A classification head (a small layer set that maps codes to labels) turns codes into action scores. A support vector machine (a margin-based classifier) and K nearest neighbors (a vote among close training points) are named as valid light choices. Fine-tune that small head on task data to get results. Sense-check: if the frozen encoder plus a linear head already separates actions, the self-supervised code captured real motion structure.

The plain autoencoder keeps any code that rebuilds well. The variational form is stricter: it demands that the code look like a normal spread with learned \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\). The session's cooking story makes the split plain. Plain autoencoder says cook an edible lunch — lentils or mixed rice, either is fine. Variational autoencoder says cook lentils exactly as taught, same pot, same rinse count of five, no shift allowed. One asks for a good meal; the other asks for a good meal made in one fixed way. Where the analogy breaks: a fixed recipe limits the cook, while the Gaussian prior enables the sampler — the strictness is what makes drawing new samples safe.

Side use flips the parts. For making new data, keep the decoder and drop the encoder. Learn the spread of marks or faces, then feed spread samples through the kept decoder to make new synthetic marks or faces. Low rebuild loss plus low spread-match loss means the maker does a good job. This is the base idea behind data-making models: sample \(z\) from \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), decode it, and get a fresh face or mark sheet.

A frequent trap is fine-tuning the whole encoder on tiny labeled data and wiping out the pre-trained features — use a small learning rate or freeze early layers first. Another trap is judging pre-training by rebuild sharpness alone: blurry rebuilds can still yield strong codes, because classification needs structure, not pixel perfection.

Archive teams pre-train a video encoder on large unlabeled clip pools, then attach a small task head for action labels, tags, or search codes. Face-synthesis teams train the variational form on portrait pools and sample new faces from the decoder.

Recap: Rebuild loss teaches the code; the optional spread-match loss shapes it for sampling. Keep the encoder for recognition, keep the decoder for generation. Bridge: With a trained encoder in hand, the next choice is which architecture and pre-trained weights to build on. Exam note: State what is kept and what is dropped for each use, write the two-term variational loss, and name \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\).

10.4.3 Student Questions and Practical Notes

Q: If the network can rebuild images well, does that not already mean the spread is right, so why add a second loss?

A: A good rebuild alone allows any spread shape — scattered islands, thin threads, or holes the sampler will fall into. Here we demand a normal shape. By the central limit idea, many pooled effects drift toward a normal shape, so that demand is safe. The encoder learns \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) for the data, and we compare learned \(Q\) with reference \(P = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) that has mean zero and spread one. The compare score is Kullback-Leibler divergence. This extra step checks that codes follow the wanted shape. With no shape demand — pure recognition use — no second loss is needed.

Q: Why keep talking about tokens when the loss needs images?

A: Tokens are the cut and images are the target. The decoder turns codes plus mask tokens back into pixels so the error has a target. Training needs that image error, and after training the decoder can go for recognition use. Keep the two vocabularies apart: masking speaks in tokens, scoring speaks in pixels.

10.5 Choosing Architectures, Pre-trained Models and Use in Practice

Three transformer families recur, and no family wins for all tasks: which one should you pick? A TimeSformer (a transformer with divided space-time attention) splits attention over space and time to save compute. ViViT (video vision transformer) is another video transformer design. VideoMAE is the masked autoencoder path above. Data, labels, speed needs, and privacy rules drive the pick — not popularity.

10.5.1 Decision Quantities

Ask how much labeled data is at hand, not just how much raw data is at hand. Ask what flow data is at hand for motion tasks. Ask what compute and memory are at hand. Ask whether fast inference matters. Ask whether data can leave the owner. In the session's framing these collapse to three checks — how much labeled data we have, whether we have flow, and how much resource we have — plus whether a pre-trained model fits the setting.

Optical flow guides the action-recognition pick. Optical flow (per-pixel motion between frames) feeds two-stream nets: one stream reads shape, the other reads motion. If flow is plenty, two-stream networks fit. If no flow is at hand, learn motion with a SlowFast network or with transformers. A SlowFast network (a two-path net with a slow path for shape and a fast path for motion) is named as the default snippet backbone in the later detection pipeline.

Pre-training guides the low-label pick. Kinetics (a large action video set used for pre-training) is the common source, and most pre-trained video models train on Kinetics. With little labeled task data, start from a Kinetics pre-trained model and fine-tune on task data. Without that option, start from a TimeSformer default and improve with masked autoencoder ideas as needed.

Situation Pick Why
Little labeled data, open setting Kinetics pre-trained TimeSformer or VideoMAE encoder, then fine-tune Reuses motion features learned at scale
Rich optical flow available Two-stream net Flow already encodes motion cleanly
No flow, need motion SlowFast or space-time transformer Learns motion from raw frames
Strict privacy or tiny device Owned light net trained in-house No outside weights or data leave the owner

The one-line rule: match the tool to the constraint — labels, flow, compute, and privacy — and re-check whenever a constraint changes.

Scope: Pre-trained weights transfer best when the source and target look alike (sports to sports beats movies to X-ray). Assumption: Fine-tuning data follows the same label meaning as the new task. A Kinetics "kick" and a football-analytics "kick" may differ, so verify label definitions before trusting transfer scores.

10.5.2 Worked Academic and Industry Stories

For academic work with open data, pre-trained models fit well: the net shape is known, training is known, and others can repeat the result. Use the pre-trained net on task data and report — that repeat path is the point of open research.

For industry work with private data, the path splits. Privacy and secrecy may block outside weights or outside data sharing. The group may hold its own shape as patentable work and may not share it fully. Then a pre-trained model gives only a baseline. The team builds a custom shape and tries to close the gap.

A numeric story from the session makes the trade plain. A pre-trained TimeSformer on Kinetics gives about 89 percent on the reference task. The team cannot ship that pre-trained net. A custom lighter net gives about 78 percent. The team then works to lift the custom net toward the 86 percent level seen in other pre-trained runs. The 89 versus 86 gap across runs is ordinary run-to-run shift — different splits, seeds, and checkpoints move scores by a point or two — but the lesson holds: a lighter private net may ship at 78 percent if owners accept that score to save resources and protect data. Sense-check: an 11-point gap for a much smaller owned net is plausible, and closing part of it with distillation or masked pre-training on owned clips is the standard next step.

Decisions outside team control also bite: resource caps, data access rules, and ship dates shape the pick. So learn both paths — how to use a pre-trained net well, and what sits inside each shape so a custom shape can be built when needed.

Do not ship a lab score as a product promise: open-benchmark accuracy rarely survives private data, odd lighting, and rushed camera work. A second trap is picking VideoMAE for its fame on a task with ample labels, where a plain strong backbone trains faster and scores as well.

Kinetics pre-training, SlowFast backbones, TimeSformer and VideoMAE encoders, and two-stream flow nets recur in product baselines, while private video owners often replace open weights with owned light nets for privacy and cost.

Recap: Labels, flow, resources, and privacy pick the architecture — pre-trained for open low-label work, owned nets for private deployment. Bridge: Once single-clip naming is settled, the harder job is finding many actions inside one long video. Exam note: List the three checks of labeled data, flow, and resources, and contrast open research use of pre-trained nets with private industry use as baseline only.

10.5.3 Practical Notes

Do not pick VideoMAE blindly even if it is widely used. First check labeled data size, annotation state, and speed needs. With ample labels and compute, a plain strong backbone may do. With few labels, masked pre-training plus fine-tuning often pays. With strict privacy, plan for owned training from the start — budget the labeling, the compute, and the extra tuning time before promising dates.

10.6 From Single-Action Clips to Multi-Action Detection

Naming one action in a 30-second clip is easy. Finding every goal, card, and clash in a 90-minute match — with start and end times — is the real job. Action recognition (naming the one action in a short trimmed clip) differs from temporal action detection (finding all actions with start and end times in a long untrimmed clip). Trimmed means one clip holds one action; untrimmed means one long video holds many actions.

10.6.1 Mathematical Formulation

Let an untrimmed video have \(L\) frames written \(I_{1}, \dots, I_{L}\), where \(I_{t}\) is the frame at time \(t\) and \(L\) is the total frame count. The session feeds these \(L\) frames from \(I_{1}\) to \(I_{L}\) as input to a black box.

The output is a set of triplets \((T_{s}^{i}, T_{e}^{i}, Y^{i})\) for \(i = 1, \dots, n\), where \(T_{s}^{i}\) is the start time of action \(i\), \(T_{e}^{i}\) is the end time of action \(i\), \(Y^{i}\) is the class label of action \(i\), \(i\) is the action index, and \(n\) is the total count of actions. Both \(n\) and each time pair are unknown before inference — the network must decide how many actions exist as well as where each one lies.

Two label examples are stated. From 101 to 103 the action is goal. From 36 to 39 the action is yellow card. In symbols \((T_{s}, T_{e}, Y) = (101, 103, \text{goal})\) and \((36, 39, \text{yellow card})\). Some clips hold three actions, some hold five — the count varies by clip, so fixed-output classifiers cannot solve this task.

Picture the timeline: the horizontal axis is time from \(1\) to \(L\), and colored bars mark each predicted span with its label. Gaps between bars are background. The takeaway is that detection outputs a variable-length set of labeled bars, not one label per clip.

Scope: Times may be frame indices or seconds, but the choice must stay consistent between truth and prediction or overlap scores become meaningless. Assumption: Spans are contiguous — one start and one end per action. Split actions (a dribble interrupted by a cut) need one span each, not a single gappy span.

10.6.2 Worked Examples and Intuition

Take football over 45 plus 45 minutes, about one and a half hours of footage. One 30-second clip cannot hold the whole game. The full video holds running, goals, penalty kicks, yellow cards, red cards, jumping, dribbling, kicking, heading, and even on-field clashes. A trimmed setup that forces one action per clip misses this mix. An untrimmed setup must name each action and mark its span — for instance, a goal at frames 101 to 103 and a yellow card at 36 to 39 inside the same long recording.

The high-level pipeline has three steps. First, cut the long video into short windows and get snippet features. A snippet (a short frame window) of 16 frames is the running example: send each 16-frame window into a recognition backbone such as SlowFast to get features and draft proposals. Second, join window outputs into candidate segments across time. Third, label each candidate and score it, then remove overlaps.

A stride example shows overlap. Start with frames \(F_{1}\) to \(F_{16}\) and get proposals \(P_{1}, P_{2}, P_{3}\), where \(F_{k}\) is frame \(k\) and \(P_{j}\) is draft proposal \(j\). Slide by stride 8 to frames \(F_{9}\) to \(F_{24}\) and repeat. Because windows overlap by 8 frames, \(P_{3}\) may appear again from the second window. The join step must merge repeats into clean candidate spans, and the score step keeps strong spans and drops the rest. With stride 8 and window 16, a 90-minute match at 25 frames per second yields about 16,000 windows — so merging and suppression are not optional extras.

A common trap is scoring windows alone and skipping the join: the same goal then appears five times under slightly different bounds. Another trap is fixing \(n\) in advance — long videos vary from zero to dozens of actions, so the pipeline must output a set, not a vector of fixed length.

Full-game football analysis, long news shows, and long nature films need this untrimmed framing; short-clip naming alone cannot mark when a goal starts and ends.

Recap: Detection outputs triplets of start, end, and label with unknown count \(n\), built from 16-frame windows at stride 8 through features to candidates to labels plus suppression. Bridge: The next step scores candidate bounds explicitly with start, end, and action curves. Exam note: Write the triplet form with \(L\), \(T_{s}\), \(T_{e}\), \(Y\), \(i\), and \(n\), and explain why \(n\) is unknown and why start and end times are part of the output.

10.6.3 Overlap Handling Preview

Overlap comes from sliding windows and from multi-scale pyramids. A simple fix is non-maximum suppression (keep the best span and drop the rest that overlap it strongly). A softer fix decays scores of overlapping spans instead of hard removal. Details follow in 10.8. The pipeline stays the same while backbones and scoring choices vary, giving proposal-based versus anchor-free designs.

10.7 Proposal Method with Start End and Action Scores

How do we turn rough window proposals into tight bounds? A boundary-sensitive network (a proposal method that scores start, end, and action curves) changes step two of the pipeline: instead of trusting window edges, it scores every instant as a possible start, end, or action middle. A temporal evaluation module (the scoring part of that network) takes snippet features and outputs three probability curves over time. Proposals form by pairing start peaks with end peaks and checking the middle.

10.7.1 Mathematical Formulation

Let snippet features be \(F\), where \(F\) means space-time codes from a backbone such as TimeSformer. The session sends backbone features through several convolution layers to get probability scores for action, start, and end at each time step.

Write the curves as \(P_{S}(t)\), \(P_{E}(t)\), and \(P_{A}(t)\), where \(P_{S}\) is start probability at time \(t\), \(P_{E}\) is end probability at time \(t\), \(P_{A}\) is action probability at time \(t\), and \(t\) is time. All three share time on the x-axis, and peaks mark likely bounds.

Pair a start peak at \(T_{s}\) with an end peak at \(T_{e}\). The center time is:

\[ T_{c} = \frac{T_{s} + T_{e}}{2} \]

Here \(T_{c}\) is the middle time, \(T_{s}\) is start time, and \(T_{e}\) is end time. In words: take the center of the paired span and check the action there. A numeric check: with \(T_{s} = 36\) and \(T_{e} = 40\), the center is \((36 + 40)/2 = 38\), which should sit inside the yellow-card action.

The check demands the same action at three spots. Send the frame at \(T_{s}\), the frame at \(T_{e}\), and the frame at \(T_{c}\) through the action backbone. All three should peak for the same class \(Y\). The class scores — such as 0.67 for yellow card versus 0.70 for a close red-card rival — join into one vector. That joined vector feeds a small multilayer perceptron that predicts overlap with truth. Scoring uses intersection over union (overlap area divided by total covered area) against ground truth when truth is at hand; the perceptron learns to predict that overlap from features so it can score spans at test time when truth is absent.

Picture the three curves stacked over one time axis: \(P_{S}\) spikes at onsets, \(P_{E}\) spikes at offsets, and \(P_{A}\) stays high through the action. The takeaway is that a valid span needs a left spike, a right spike, and a raised middle all agreeing on one label.

Scope: Peak pairing assumes actions are short enough that the next end peak belongs to the current start. Assumption: The three-point agreement implies one coherent action. Overlapping actions of different classes (a run plus a kick) break this and need the anchor-free design of 10.8.

10.7.2 Worked Examples and Intuition

Label frames near an action as 1 and frames far from it as 0. Picture standing, then running, then standing again. At onset from stand to run, pixel gaps jump — that jump drives a peak in \(P_{S}\). During steady running, gaps stay high but below the onset spike, so \(P_{A}\) stays raised. At offset from run to rest, gaps jump again — that jump drives a peak in \(P_{E}\). The scoring net learns these shapes from features, not from raw gaps alone, but raw frame differencing gives the feel.

Pairing works on local peaks. Find a \(P_{S}\) peak at, say, \(t = 36\). Find the next \(P_{E}\) peak at \(t = 39\). Take their times as \(T_{s} = 36\) and \(T_{e} = 39\). Compute \(T_{c} = 37.5\). Check the action at all three times. If start says yellow card, end says yellow card, and middle says yellow card with high scores, the span looks right. If the three disagree, the proposal looks weak. Yellow versus red needs care because scores can sit close, such as 0.67 and 0.70 — the small scoring net plus overlap scoring sorts these near ties by learning which feature patterns truly overlap truth. Sense-check: a span whose middle scores background while its ends score action is almost surely a false pairing of two different events.

Highlight clipping for sports uses start and end peaks to cut goal or card moments with tight bounds — the peak positions become the cut points.

Recap: Score start, end, and action curves over time, pair peaks, and demand three-point agreement plus a strong overlap score. Bridge: Proposal pairing is fiddly at scale, so the next design drops proposals entirely and regresses bounds directly. Exam note: Draw or describe the up-down start and end curves over time, and state the three-point check of start, end, and center.

10.7.3 Student Questions and Practical Notes

Q: How do we know a paired start and end truly belong together?

A: Pair local peaks in time order, take the center, and demand the same action at start, end, and center with high probability. Then join the three feature vectors and score the span with overlap against truth. Agreement across all three points plus a strong overlap score marks a good proposal.

Finding pairs is the hard part. Peaks may split or merge — one long applause becomes three spikes. Thresholds for peak height and span length need tuning per sport or scene: football cards need short-span thresholds while cycling breaks need long ones. Tune on held-out matches, not on the test reel.

10.8 Anchor-Free Detection, Pyramids, Losses and Overlap Handling

Proposals are fiddly — peaks split, pairs mismatch, thresholds multiply. What if each instant simply names its own action and its own bounds? ActionFormer (an anchor-free transformer for detection) drops proposals: it reads dense features at many time scales, labels each spot, and regresses start and end directly. A feature pyramid (the same features viewed at strides 1, 2, 4, and more) lets short and long actions both get caught.

10.8.1 Mathematical Formulation

Let dense features be \(F_{1}, F_{2}, \dots\), where \(F_{k}\) is the code near time \(k\). Each spot goes through self-attention and then splits to two heads. The classification head (the branch that names the class) outputs class scores. The regression head (the branch that marks times) outputs start and end offsets. The session's core line is exactly this: the output of self-attention goes to a classification head and a regression head, one for class and one for start and end times.

Classification uses focal loss (a loss that down-weights easy background spots and lifts rare action spots). With \(p\) as the score of the true class, \(\alpha = 0.25\) as foreground-background balance, and \(\gamma = 2.0\) as focus strength, the form is:

\[ FL = -\alpha (1-p)^{\gamma} \log(p) \]

Here \(FL\) is focal loss, \(p\) is the true-class probability, \(\alpha\) balances foreground and background, \(\gamma\) sets how strongly easy cases get down-weighted, and \(\log\) is the natural log. This is the standard focal form: the session states minus alpha times one-minus-probability with a focusing power, and the log term completes it — high \(p\) drives \((1-p)^{\gamma}\) small so easy background spots count less, while low \(p\) keeps weight high so hard action spots count more.

Regression uses overlap on foreground spots only. Let truth span be \(G\) and predicted span be \(S\), where \(G\) means ground-truth time span and \(S\) means predicted span. Overlap scoring follows temporal intersection over union from 10.9. Total training loss adds both terms with more weight on classification:

\[ L_{total} = L_{cls} + \lambda L_{reg} \]

Here \(L_{cls}\) is focal loss, \(L_{reg}\) is overlap loss, \(L_{total}\) is the sum, and \(\lambda\) is a balance weight that keeps regression from swamping classification. A spot-check: when \(p \to 1\), \((1-p)^{\gamma} \to 0\) and \(FL \to 0\), so mastered background pays nothing; when \(p\) is small, the loss stays large and gradients keep flowing.

Scope: Focal loss assumes extreme imbalance — background instants vastly outnumber action instants. Assumption: Each instant carries one true class including background. Dense overlapping actions of different classes at the same instant need multi-label heads, which this form does not cover.

10.8.2 Worked Pyramid and Losses

Build levels by stride. Level 0 uses all spots \(F_{1}, F_{2}, F_{3}, F_{4}, F_{5}\). Stride 2 skips one each time and repeats attention plus labeling. Stride 4 skips more. One talk example reaches \(F_{9}\) to \(F_{17}\) at a coarse level, where \(9 + 8 = 17\): a stride-8 step links spots 8 apart into one long view. Each spot at each level outputs a class and a class probability.

Short actions pop at fine levels — a quick kick spans a few frames at stride 1. Long actions pop at coarse levels — if \(F_{1}\) and \(F_{17}\) share the same action, a coarse level links them as one long span. Fine levels keep tight bounds for quick kicks or jumps. That is how one net catches both scales in a single pass.

Focal numbers show the effect. With \(\alpha = 0.25\) and \(\gamma = 2.0\), an easy background spot with \(p = 0.95\) gets factor \((1-0.95)^{2} = 0.0025\), so its loss is \(FL = -0.25 \times 0.0025 \times \log(0.95) \approx 0.00003\) — it almost vanishes. A hard action spot with \(p = 0.40\) keeps factor \((1-0.40)^{2} = 0.36\), giving \(FL = -0.25 \times 0.36 \times \log(0.40) \approx 0.082\) — thousands of times larger, so it still drives learning. The right class gets higher confidence than wrong rivals because hard spots dominate every batch. Sense-check: the ratio of the two losses is about 2500 to 1, which is exactly the rebalancing the pyramid needs.

Regression runs only where action is thought to occur — background spots do not regress times. Foreground spans compare with truth spans via overlap, and the sum of focal plus overlap trains the whole net.

Overlap across windows and pyramid levels remains, so suppression closes the pipeline. Hard suppression keeps the top span and drops high-overlap rivals. Soft suppression decays rival scores by overlap instead of deleting them. The session describes the score getting decayed by overlap and decay width through an exponential, whose direct reading is the Gaussian soft-suppression form:

\[ s_{new} = s \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{TIOU^{2}}{\sigma}\right) \]

Here \(s\) is the first score, \(s_{new}\) is the decayed score, \(TIOU\) is temporal overlap from 10.9, \(\sigma\) is decay width to tune, and \(\exp\) is the exponential. A spot-check: with \(TIOU = 0\), the factor is \(\exp(0) = 1\) and the score survives; with \(TIOU = 1\) and \(\sigma = 0.5\), the factor is \(\exp(-2) \approx 0.135\) and the rival nearly dies. Keep top spans after decay — nearby frames then still signal that the action exists, but with lower scores than the best span.

Picture the decay curve: the horizontal axis is \(TIOU\) from 0 to 1 and the vertical axis is the keep factor. The curve starts at 1 and falls smoothly, steeper for small \(\sigma\). The takeaway is that soft decay punishes near-duplicates hard while sparing neighbors that may be distinct actions.

Do not regress on background: background has no true bounds, so its gradients are noise that wrecks the regression head. A second trap is setting \(\sigma\) blindly — too small deletes true neighbors (two quick passes merged into one), too large keeps duplicates that tank precision.

Multi-scale live sports and long surveillance feeds use pyramids plus soft suppression to keep both quick bursts and long plays in one pass.

Recap: A pyramid of strides feeds two heads per spot — focal loss names the class everywhere, overlap loss times the action on foreground only, and soft decay cleans duplicates. Bridge: All of this scoring rests on one overlap number, defined next with its ground-truth format. Exam note: Write the focal form with \(\alpha = 0.25\) and \(\gamma = 2.0\) and state their roles, and state that regression runs on foreground only while total loss sums both terms.

10.8.3 Student Questions and Practical Notes

Q: Where do start and end times come from if no proposals exist?

A: They get regressed. Self-attention codes go to two heads. The classification head names action versus background with scores. The regression head marks start and end times. Focal loss trains the first head. Overlap loss trains the second head on foreground spots. The sum trains the net. No pairing step is needed because each foreground spot directly predicts its own span.

10.9 Temporal Overlap Scoring and Ground Truth Format

Two spans overlap — but by how much, and is it enough to count as right? Temporal intersection over union (overlap length divided by union length over time) scores time spans with one number between 0 and 1. MOT17 (a multi-object tracking set used here as a ground-truth format example) shows how boxes plus times get stored. A threshold on temporal overlap then marks a prediction as right.

10.9.1 Mathematical Formulation

Let predicted span be \([T_{s}, T_{e}]\) and truth span be \([G_{s}, G_{e}]\), where \(T_{s}\) and \(T_{e}\) are predicted start and end, and \(G_{s}\) and \(G_{e}\) are true start and end. The session's rule is: take intersection over union of the two time frames to get temporal overlap.

The overlap length is the shared part and the union length is the total covered part. With \(\max\) and \(\min\) picking the tighter and wider bounds:

\[ |Intersection| = \max(0, \min(T_{e}, G_{e}) - \max(T_{s}, G_{s})) \]

\[ |Union| = \max(T_{e}, G_{e}) - \min(T_{s}, G_{s}) \]

\[ TIOU = \frac{|Intersection|}{|Union|} \]

Here \(TIOU\) is temporal overlap, \(|Intersection|\) is shared time length, and \(|Union|\) is total covered time length. A threshold \(\tau\), where \(\tau\) is a cutoff such as 0.5 in practice, marks success when \(TIOU \geq \tau\). The cutoff is set per task: strict leaderboards use 0.5 or 0.7, loose recall checks use 0.3.

A box example uses \((x, y, w, h)\), where \(x\) and \(y\) mark the top-left spot, \(w\) is width, and \(h\) is height. Boxes always start from the \(x, y\) spot and then use width and height, so four numbers fix the whole box and the net must regress those four. A spot-check on domains: \(TIOU\) always lies in \([0, 1]\), equals 1 only for identical spans, and equals 0 for disjoint spans — any value outside that range signals a bug.

Scope: This score judges one predicted span against one truth span. Assumption: Both spans use the same time units and the same inclusive-or-exclusive convention. Mixing frame counts with seconds, or off-by-one frame edges, shifts every score silently.

10.9.2 Worked Overlap and Ground Truth Walk

Take predicted start 10 and end 20. Take truth start 14 and end 24. In symbols \(T_{s} = 10\), \(T_{e} = 20\), \(G_{s} = 14\), \(G_{e} = 24\). Shared part is 14 to 20, length \(20 - 14 = 6\). Total covered part is 10 to 24, length \(24 - 10 = 14\). So:

\[ TIOU = \frac{6}{14} \approx 0.43 \]

Here 6 is shared length and 14 is union length. At a 0.5 cutoff this span would miss; at a lower cutoff such as 0.3 it would pass. The cutoff choice shapes reported accuracy — the same predictions look strong under 0.3 and weak under 0.7. Sense-check: the prediction covers less than half the union, so a score below one half feels right.

A second check: perfect prediction \([14, 24]\) against truth \([14, 24]\) gives intersection 10 and union 10, so \(TIOU = 1.0\). A disjoint prediction \([1, 5]\) against \([14, 24]\) gives intersection 0, so \(TIOU = 0\).

Ground-truth rows in the MOT17 style pack frame id, box, class, confidence, and visibility. The first entry is frame id. Four entries hold box values \(x, y, w, h\). Later entries hold class label, confidence score for that class, and visibility share. Time span comes from start frame plus span length. From those rows we can test whether a predicted frame span is right and compute the overlap above — for example, rows with frame ids 101 to 103 and matching boxes confirm the goal span from 10.6.

Picture the two spans on one axis: prediction 10 to 20 and truth 14 to 24 overlap in the middle third. The takeaway is that the shared middle drives the score while the two overhangs dilute it.

Tracking benchmarks, sports detection leaderboards, and video search scoring all use temporal overlap with a fixed cutoff to rank runs.

Recap: Overlap length over union length gives one score in \([0, 1]\), and a task cutoff turns it into right or wrong. Bridge: This closes the lecture arc from copying pixels to finding actions in time — the exam summary collects every load-bearing formula. Exam note: Compute temporal overlap from two time spans, and list box fields \(x, y, w, h\) plus frame id, class, confidence, and visibility.

10.9.3 Practical Notes

Truth must be at hand to train overlap scoring — without truth, proposal scoring and regression scoring lose their target. At test time without truth, the net still outputs spans plus scores, and overlap thresholds only shape scoring and ranking. Tune the cutoff \(\tau\) and the decay width \(\sigma\) together with peak thresholds for the scene at hand: fast ball sports want looser cutoffs and gentler decay than slow studio footage, since bounds are inherently fuzzier at speed.

Key Industry Applications

  • Kinetics pre-trained encoders speed action projects with few labels through fine-tuning — sports analytics teams start from Kinetics weights and adapt to club footage in days rather than months.
  • SlowFast snippet backbones feed detection pipelines for sports and street video, where the fast path catches motion and the slow path holds shape.
  • TimeSformer, ViViT, and VideoMAE encoders give options for speed versus accuracy trade-offs: divided attention for efficiency, full video transformers for accuracy, masked pre-training for label-scarce pools.
  • Two-stream flow nets fit where optical flow is rich; learned motion fits where flow is missing or too costly to compute at scale.
  • Private video owners use open nets as baselines, then ship owned light nets for privacy and cost reasons — the 89-to-78 percent story in 10.5 is the template.
  • Start-end peak cutting turns full games into goal, card, run, dribble, jump, kick, and clash highlights with tight bounds from \(P_{S}\) and \(P_{E}\) peaks.
