3.1 Why Base Models Need Retrieval Help

A large language model is a model trained on web-scale text that predicts likely next words. Systems such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini act as fluent writers, but they carry fixed limits. Their training data ends at a cutoff date, so they miss current events, weather, politics, and fresh company facts. They learn from broad public text, so they lack private company facts. Their weights hide the source of each claim, so answers are hard to trace back. And they sometimes invent fluent but wrong facts, a fault called hallucination, where the model states an answer with full confidence even when the facts are wrong.

Think of a bright student walking into a closed-book exam on company policy. The student writes well, but the facts were never in the course books. Now hand that student the policy binder and let the exam be open-book. The writing skill stays the same, yet the answers turn grounded. The binder stands for the outside authority sources the model was missing.

Retrieval augmented generation, often shortened to RAG, fixes this gap by joining the model to an outside store of trusted facts. The flow has four plain steps. First the system indexes a set of trusted documents. Then it retrieves the few pieces that match the user query. Next it augments the prompt, which means it pastes those pieces next to the question. Last it generates the answer with the model reading that added context. One compact way to state the gain: the model no longer answers from memory alone, because it now reads fresh evidence first.

A RAG system has two halves: a retriever and a generator. Write the document store as \(D\) and the user query as \(q\). The retriever returns the top relevant passages \(R(q) = d_1, \ldots, d_k\) from \(D\). The generator then writes the answer conditioned on both the passages and the query, token by token:

\[ p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(x_i \mid R(q); q; x_{\lt i}) \]

Here \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\) are the answer tokens, \(R(q)\) is the retrieved context, \(q\) is the user query, and \(x_{\lt i}\) marks the tokens written so far. In plain words: each new word is picked with both the question and the retrieved passages in view.

An index is a search-ready copy of the trusted documents, held either as word lists or as vectors. Retrieval is the act of pulling the few passages that best match the query. Augmentation is the act of pasting those passages next to the question inside the prompt. Generation is the model writing the final answer while reading that added context.

The same idea was recalled through quick questions at the start of the class. RAG improves answer accuracy by linking the model to outside authority sources. It lowers hallucination and it opens access to private or restricted data. The four working parts named were indexing, retrieval, augmentation, and generation.

A bank case made the motive concrete: about 29 models that build different reports for different customer groups, and the teams use RAG so each report pulls only the customer data it is allowed to see. A telecom case added a second shape: product teams keep long specification documents as PDFs across modules, and the testing team queries a RAG system to learn which features a new decoding release actually added. A third note tracked careers: support roles in such firms are shifting toward SRE-style AI work, with wider scope than routine daily tickets, which is one more sign that retrieval-grounded assistants are entering daily operations.

Picture a library with a strict librarian. The books are the trusted documents, the librarian is the retriever, and you are the generator. You never memorize the whole library. For each question you ask the librarian for the three most relevant pages, read them at your desk, and then write the answer with those pages open. The mapping is direct: books to index, librarian search to retrieval, pages on the desk to augmentation, your essay to generation. The picture breaks at one point: a real librarian understands the question, while a basic retriever only matches surface patterns, which is why later sections upgrade the matching step.

3.1.1 Problems That Led to RAG

Training sets the first limit. Every model, large or small, trains on a fixed high-quality set, often with instruction tuning, and that set covers only a fixed time window. Anything newer than the window is unknown to the weights. A weather question about today, a policy change from last week, or a fresh product fact all fall outside that frozen past.

A second limit is traceability. Once weights are learned, no simple map shows which training input caused a given output line. When the model states a number, the reader cannot click through to the source page. That gap hurts in banks, courts, and clinics, where each claim needs a citation.

A third limit is specialisation. A generic model gives generic facts, but a finance desk or a hardware team needs facts tied to its own books and specs, and those facts cannot be released in public. The knowledge lives in private PDFs, sheets, and tickets that public training text never saw.

Privacy forms the fourth limit, since company data must stay inside the company. Sending customer records to an outside training run is not an option, yet staff still want model-grade answers over those records.

Fine-tuning is one answer, because it reshapes model weights toward company data. But fine-tuning needs graphics processors, time, and money, since it repeats much of the cost of training, even with parameter-efficient methods that touch fewer weights. RAG skips that cost. It leaves weights frozen and changes only the input, by adding fresh, domain-tied, traceable context at query time. The trade is plain: fine-tuning suits stable house style and stable facts, while RAG suits facts that move fast or must stay private.

Scope: RAG fits when the needed facts live in readable documents and change often or must stay private. Assumption: the store holds trusted text, the retriever can find it, and the model reads context faithfully. When the task needs new reasoning skill rather than new facts, or when no trusted documents exist, retrieval adds little.

Common traps to avoid: trusting a fluent answer that cites nothing, pasting private records into a public model to "ground" it, and letting the index go stale while the world moves on. Each of these reintroduces the very fault RAG was built to remove.

3.2 The Naive Vector Pipeline End to End

Naive RAG is the basic form of the method. It is also called vector RAG, and both names point to the same pipeline. The idea is plain: cut company text into small parts, turn each part into a number list, store those lists, and at query time pull the parts whose lists sit closest to the question. The model then writes the final answer while reading those parts.

Think of a spice rack where every jar carries a scent label, and a shopper walks in holding a sample smell. The shopper does not read every jar. The shopper compares smells and picks the five closest jars. Chunks are the jars, vectors are the scent labels, and the query is the sample smell. The picture breaks at one point: smells match by nature, while vectors match only when the same embedding model wrote both sides.

A vector database is a store built to keep such number lists and to search them fast. Tools named here include Qdrant and Pinecone, which play the same role for vectors that Oracle or similar stores play for rows. A knowledge graph store such as Neo4j appears later for linked data, but the naive path needs only vectors.

The full loop runs like this. The user types a prompt through a chat box or an enterprise screen. That prompt is turned into a vector with the same embedding model used for the store. The store returns the top K chunks, where K is a small count such as five, ranked by closeness. Those chunks join the prompt as context. The model then acts like an English tutor: it reads the supplied context and writes an answer drawn from it. The result is grounded, which means each claim leans on retrieved text instead of pure memory.

Two concrete queries show why this helps. Ask who teaches a given applications course in a given batch and section and the base model cannot know, because that fact lives only in the university corpus. Ask about international travel policy for a given role and tenure and the base model can only guess, because each firm sets its own airline rules, class limits, and yearly caps. In both cases the private store holds the answer and the model only needs to read it.

Bank and travel bots show the pattern in daily use. An enterprise travel bot keeps HR policy PDFs, turns them into vectors, and answers role-wise travel questions from retrieved paragraphs instead of generic web text. The same pattern serves course facts, product facts, and policy facts wherever the truth lives in company files.

Scope: Naive vector RAG fits plain lookup questions whose answer sits in one or two prose paragraphs. Assumption: chunks are clean, embeddings share one model, and the top K window holds the answer. When facts span grids, or when links between facts matter, or when the answer needs many scattered pages, this plain pipe falls short and later sections add fixes.

Exam note: keep the retrieval quality high, because weak context can score worse than no context at all. When scores look poor, fix parsing, chunking, or embedding before blaming the model.

3.2.1 Mathematical Formulation

The method was described in plain words as turning text into vectors and comparing them with cosine closeness. This section builds that account into full math, reconciled against the standard vector-space form where the query-document cosine is the dot product of unit vectors.

An embedding is a number list that stands for a piece of text. We write the query vector as \(q \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and each chunk vector as \(c_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\), where \(d\) is the vector size fixed by the embedding model. The spoken account was "convert the query with the same embedding model used for the store, then find the closest chunks". One strict rule came with this math: the embedding model for the store and the embedding model for the query must match. Mixed models place vectors in different spaces, and then closeness scores lose meaning.

Closeness is measured with cosine similarity, which is the overlap angle between two vectors scaled to a simple score. The formula is:

\[ \text{sim}(q, c_i) = \frac{q^\top c_i}{\|q\|_2 \, \|c_i\|_2} \]

Here \(q^\top c_i\) is the dot product of query and chunk, a single number that sums element-wise products \(q^\top c_i = \sum_{j=1}^{d} q_j c_{ij}\). The terms \(\|q\|_2\) and \(\|c_i\|_2\) are the lengths of the two vectors, each computed as \(\|v\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d} v_j^2}\). Scores near 1 mark near-identical direction, a score near 0 marks unrelated direction, and a score near -1 marks opposite direction. In retrieval practice, scores below about 0.7 often mark weak matches worth replacing.

The same formula can be read as the dot product of unit vectors. Divide each vector by its own length first, then take the dot product:

\[ \text{sim}(q, c_i) = \hat{q}^\top \hat{c}_i, \quad \hat{q} = \frac{q}{\|q\|_2}, \quad \hat{c}_i = \frac{c_i}{\|c_i\|_2} \]

Here \(\hat{q}\) is the unit query vector, of length 1, and \(\hat{c}_i\) is the unit chunk vector, also of length 1. This form shows why length scaling drops out: only the angle between the two arrows matters, not how long each arrow is.

The top K set is the group of K chunks with the best scores. Written as a ranked set:

\[ S_K = \arg\max_{i}^{K} \, \text{sim}(q, c_i) \]

Here \(S_K\) is the kept set of K chunk indices, \(\text{sim}(q, c_i)\) is the cosine score of chunk \(i\), and the \(\arg\max^{K}\) operator picks the K indices with the largest scores. The spoken account was "take the top K chunks, just like a web search, and pass them as context". The final step feeds both parts to the model. With \(x\) for the user query and \(C\) for the retrieved chunks, generation is:

\[ y = G(x, C) \]

where \(G\) is the generator model and \(y\) is the grounded answer. The spoken account was "the model acts like an English tutor and writes from the supplied context". Generation quality then hinges on context quality: a thin or off-topic \(C\) drags \(y\) down, which is why the exam note warns that weak context can score worse than none.

3.2.2 Worked Examples

Travel policy lookup. Setup: HR policy PDFs hold airline, class, and cap rules per role. Steps: (1) parse PDFs to text, (2) cut text into chunks, (3) embed each chunk, (4) store vectors, (5) embed the role-wise question with the same model, (6) rank chunks by cosine score, (7) paste the top chunks next to the question, (8) let the model draft the policy answer.

A tiny numeric trace shows the ranking step. Suppose three chunk scores come back as 0.91, 0.83, and 0.45 for K = 2. The kept set is the first two chunks, since \(0.91 > 0.83 > 0.45\). Result: a role-wise answer tied to company rules, with each claim traceable to its chunk. Sense-check: the 0.45 chunk drops out, so no generic filler dilutes the grounded claims.

Course staffing query. Setup: the fact "who teaches this course in this batch" lives only in the university corpus. Steps: same eight steps as above, ending with retrieved paragraphs about staffing. Result: a grounded name and section instead of a guess. Sense-check: without the store, the model could only invent a plausible name, which is exactly the hallucination fault this pipe removes.

The two cases share one trait: public training text cannot hold the answer, so reading private chunks at query time is the only path to a correct reply.

3.2.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: What if a retrieved chunk holds only part of the answer, so the final text is worse than the base model alone?

A: That can happen. Poor parsing, poor chunking, or poor embedding all shrink answer quality, and a thin context can mislead the model. The fix sits at those early steps: parse cleanly, chunk with care, and embed well. When the context window cannot hold enough chunks, an agentic loop can retry retrieval, but each retry adds cost and time.

This exchange carries the lecture's central warning, tagged in the manifest as a thin-context alert: fix parsing, chunking, and embedding first, and only then blame the model. The exam note repeats the same rule, since retrieval quality decides generation quality.

Common traps to avoid: mixing two embedding models between store and query, setting K so high that weak chunks crowd out strong ones, and pasting chunks without checking that each claim in the draft points back to one. All three turn a grounded pipe into a fluent guessing pipe.

Naive RAG works when chunks are clean and the query meets its match: embed both sides with one model, rank by cosine, keep the top K, and draft from context. Next, chunking and retrieval choices decide how clean those chunks are and how the ranking trade-offs are set.

3.3 Chunking and Retrieval Choices

Chunking is the act of cutting a large corpus into small stored parts. A thousand-page file cannot share one vector, because one vector would blur every fact together and no single question could pull the right paragraph. So the text is split first and each part gets its own vector.

Think of cutting a long newspaper into filing cards. One card per article keeps each fact findable. Gluing the whole paper onto one card keeps nothing findable. Chunking is the cutting step, and the card box is the vector store.

Several splitters were compared. Fixed-size chunking cuts every 500 tokens or, in the demo below, every 10 sentences, which is simple and fast. Overlap keeps shared edge text between neighbours so no sentence loses its border. Semantic chunking groups related lines together, so chunk sizes vary and each chunk holds one theme. Hierarchical chunking nests levels such as paragraph then sentence. Recursive chunking splits in layers with shrinking separators. The choice rule is plain: use fixed size when speed and low cost matter, and move to semantic or layered splits when paragraphs link to each other, as in finance or medical files.

Storage has two broad forms. Dense vectors keep contextual meaning and need exact-shape matching at search time. Sparse vectors keep word-level hits, in the TF-IDF family, with BM25 as a refined sparse form that softens length and saturation effects. The spoken account was "dense vectors catch meaning, sparse vectors catch exact codes". A hybrid search runs both and joins them. Reciprocal rank fusion joins rankings from the two lists, and maximal marginal relevance trades relevance against variety so the top K chunks do not repeat each other.

Cost pressure drives much of this design. Model vendors charge by tokens, and the bill can rise from about 1,000 to 50,000 per month at enterprise scale when prompts grow. Short, varied, high-value chunks save money twice: fewer wasted tokens and fewer repeated facts.

Exact codes show the split in practice: a course identifier favours sparse search, because the full string must match, while a meaning question favours dense search, because wording varies. Teams pair the two so neither query type fails.

Scope: Hybrid ranking fits mixed query traffic where some questions need exact strings and others need paraphrase matches. Assumption: both indexes cover the same corpus, and the context window stays small enough that each added chunk must earn its tokens. When traffic is purely exact codes, sparse alone is cheaper; when traffic is purely prose meaning, dense alone is enough.

Exam note: context length caps how many chunks the model can read, so rank quality matters more than rank size. Prefer fewer strong varied chunks over many weak ones.

3.3.1 Mathematical Formulation

The spoken account for dense search was "encode words with a model such as BERT and compare with cosine similarity". That is the same cosine formula as in 3.2.1, with \(q\) for the query and \(c_i\) for each chunk. The spoken account for sparse search was "match words and keyword weights, TF-IDF style, with BM25 as the refined form". BM25 scores a document \(d\) against query \(q\) by weighting each query term \(t\) with its IDF weight, its frequency \(tf_{t,d}\) in \(d\), and the length of \(d\) against the average length, with knobs \(k\) in about \([1.2, 2]\) for term-frequency saturation and \(b = 0.75\) for length scaling as reasonable defaults. In plain words: repeat mentions help, but with fading returns, and long documents pay a length penalty.

Rank fusion joins the two orderings. With \(r_j(d)\) for the rank of chunk \(d\) in list \(j\) and \(k\) for a softening constant, reciprocal rank fusion scores each chunk as:

\[ \text{RRF}(d) = \sum_j \frac{1}{k + r_j(d)} \]

Here the sum runs over the ranking lists, \(r_j(d) = 1\) marks the top hit in list \(j\), and \(k\) (often 60) softens the gap between rank 1 and rank 2 so one list cannot dominate. The spoken account was "fuse the dense ranking and the sparse ranking to get the final chunks".

A small trace shows the effect. Suppose chunk A ranks 1st dense and 4th sparse, while chunk B ranks 2nd in both, with \(k = 60\). Then \(\text{RRF}(A) = 1/61 + 1/64 \approx 0.0320\) and \(\text{RRF}(B) = 1/62 + 1/62 \approx 0.0323\), so the steady all-rounder B edges out the split-ranked A. That is the intended behavior: chunks that both lists like rise to the top.

Variety uses a trade knob. With \(\text{Rel}(d)\) for relevance of chunk \(d\), \(\text{Sim}(d, s)\) for overlap with an already picked chunk \(s\), and \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\) for the trade weight, the next pick follows:

\[ \text{MMR}(d) = \lambda \, \text{Rel}(d) - (1 - \lambda) \max_{s} \text{Sim}(d, s) \]

Here \(\text{Rel}(d)\) is the query-match score, the \(\max\) term is the worst overlap with chunks already kept, and \(\lambda\) sets the trade. The spoken account was "combine relevance with variety so repeated chunks do not fill the costly context". High \(\lambda\) favours pure relevance, while low \(\lambda\) punishes near-duplicates.

A second trace grounds the knob. Suppose two candidate chunks score \(\text{Rel} = 0.9\) and \(0.85\), with overlaps \(0.95\) and \(0.2\) against the kept set, and \(\lambda = 0.7\). Then \(\text{MMR} = 0.7 \times 0.9 - 0.3 \times 0.95 = 0.345\) for the near-duplicate, versus \(0.7 \times 0.85 - 0.3 \times 0.2 = 0.535\) for the fresh chunk. The lower-relevance but fresher chunk wins, which is exactly the variety trade the context budget demands.

3.3.2 Worked Examples

Course code lookup. Setup: a user asks for a code like AIMLCZG519. Sparse path: the full token matches the stored string and ranks first. Dense path: the code becomes a meaning vector that may drift from the exact string. Result: sparse wins on exact codes. Sense-check: the meaning vector has no reason to keep a rare code intact, so the exact-match list must carry this query type. This single contrast motivates hybrid search, since neither path wins every query type.

A meaning-side mirror sharpens the lesson. Ask "which course covers language models this term" and the sparse list may miss paraphrases such as "NLP" or "transformers", while the dense list catches them. Result: dense wins on paraphrase. Sense-check: neither path covers both query shapes alone, so the joined ranking is the safe default for mixed traffic.

3.3.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: There are many chunking methods, so how do we pick one?

A: Fixed size is the easiest and fastest, so start there when the task needs no links between parts. Add overlap when borders matter and the budget allows longer prompts. Move to semantic, recursive, or layered splits when paragraphs relate to each other. Latency and cost decide as much as accuracy: fancier stores index slower and answer slower, so match the tool to the task.

This canonical answer sets the lecture's selection rule: cheapest splitter that keeps each theme intact, with fancier splits reserved for linked prose.

Q: Recursive chunking already groups related text, so how is that different from graph RAG, which also tracks links?

A: Recursive chunking only groups related text before embedding, so the vectors still sit alone. Graph RAG stores the links themselves as edges between entities, so generation-time steps can walk those links. Chunking shapes the input parts, while the graph shapes the stored links. When links must be read at answer time, grouping alone is not enough.

This correction guards the boundary the next sections build on: grouping text is preparation, while storing links is structure, and only the second supports multi-hop walks.

Q: What if the full answer spans two chunks, with part in chunk one and part in chunk two?

A: Then top K choice carries the load. Retrieve the most relevant yet least similar chunks, so both halves arrive without crowding each other out. That balance is exactly what the variety-aware ranking step is built to do: relevance pulls both halves up, while the overlap penalty keeps one half from evicting the other.

Common traps to avoid: slicing tables mid-row so neither half parses, skipping overlap on narrative text so border sentences lose context, and fusing lists without checking that both indexes cover the same corpus. Each turns a ranking upgrade into noise.

Chunking decides what each vector means, and fusion plus variety decide which vectors reach the model. Next, a small hand-built system shows every moving part in code before the lecture routes hard cases to vision models and graphs.

3.4 Building a Small System From Scratch

The class walked through a small build with no orchestration frame, first in plain steps and then in code. LangChain and LangGraph can automate these steps, but hand code shows each moving part. Both paths are valid: use a frame for speed or hand code for tight control of chunk size and embedding choice.

The nine steps run in order. (1) Ingest: read source files such as PDFs into text. (2) Parse: turn file bytes into clean text with a parser such as PyMuPDF, with Tesseract named as another option for scanned pages. (3) Explore: sample random pages, count tokens per page, and learn what the corpus holds before storing anything. (4) Chunk: split text into parts, here fixed at 10 sentences per chunk with a sentence splitter such as spaCy, since splitters beat naive full-stop cuts. (5) Embed: turn each chunk into a vector with an embedding model such as Hugging Face sentence transformers. (6) Store: keep vectors in a vector store for large data, or in a tensor holder such as torch tensors for small tests under about 100,000 items. (7) Query: embed the user question with the same model. (8) Rank: score chunks by cosine similarity and keep the top K, here five. (9) Generate: paste those chunks next to the question and let a local model, such as an Ollama or Qwen build, draft the answer, or send the same bundle to a larger model through an API key when the task needs it.

Two details deserve stress. First, small holders suit tests, while enterprise loads need a real vector store, because search across millions of vectors needs indexed structures and strong hardware. Second, the demo ran on graphics processors, whether local cards, Colab, or cloud machines, because embedding and search at scale need that power.

Teams prototype on tensor holders and Colab, then shift the same vectors into Pinecone or a similar store when data grows. Firms with private data keep their own graphics hardware so vectors never leave the building.

Think of setting up a small kitchen before opening a restaurant. The nine steps are the prep stations: wash, chop, portion, label, shelve, and only then cook to order. Hand code is cooking each dish from raw ingredients so every station is visible. A frame like LangChain is a food processor: faster, but it hides the blade. The picture breaks at one point: kitchen tools never change the taste of raw ingredients, while an embedding model changes what every chunk means.

3.4.1 Mathematical Formulation

The spoken account was "each chunk becomes a vector, the query becomes a vector, and cosine picks the winners". Sizes from the walkthrough: the sentence-transformer path used here caps input at about 384 tokens per pass, and the stored chunk vectors shown had width 768, so each chunk vector \(c_i \in \mathbb{R}^{768}\). The query "macro nutrient functions" becomes \(q \in \mathbb{R}^{768}\) through the same model. Ranking reuses:

\[ \text{score}_i = \frac{q^\top c_i}{\|q\|_2 \, \|c_i\|_2} \]

Here \(\text{score}_i\) is the cosine closeness of chunk \(i\), \(q^\top c_i\) is the dot product \(\sum_{j=1}^{768} q_j c_{ij}\), and the denominator holds the two vector lengths. With width 768, each dot product sums 768 products, and each length is the square root of 768 squared terms.

Two rules govern the store. The store rule: small tests can sit in tensors, while large loads need a vector store with indexed search. The model rule: the same embedding model must encode both sides, so store vectors and query vectors share one space. Token counts per sentence depend on the tokenizer in use, not on the chunker, so that part is marked where the tokenizer choice enters: the chunker fixes ten sentences, and the tokenizer decides how many tokens those sentences become.

A quick capacity check shows why the store rule matters. Width 768 in 4-byte floats means each chunk costs \(768 \times 4 = 3072\) bytes, about 3 KB. At 100,000 chunks that is about 300 MB, which fits a test tensor. At 10 million chunks it passes 30 GB, which needs an indexed store plus approximate search instead of exact scans.

3.4.2 Worked Examples

Nutrient question over a nutrition corpus. Setup: PDF pages parsed and split into 10-sentence chunks, each embedded into width-768 vectors. Given: user query "macro nutrient functions". Steps: (1) embed the query with the same sentence model, (2) score every chunk with cosine similarity, (3) sort high to low, (4) keep the top five, (5) paste those five 10-sentence chunks next to the query, (6) send the bundle to the model for drafting.

Suppose five chunk scores return as 0.88, 0.84, 0.81, 0.79, 0.77, with the next chunk at 0.61. The cut keeps the first five, since each clears the visible gap above 0.61. Result: five ranked chunks with similarity scores, highest first, each traceable to its source paragraphs. Sense-check: the gap between 0.77 and 0.61 marks the natural cut, so K = 5 keeps strong matches without inviting filler.

The same code path also returns source text for each vector, so every claim in the draft can point back to its chunk. That trace is what turns a fluent paragraph into a grounded answer.

3.4.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why not embed the whole document first and then cut the vectors into chunks?

A: That order mixes global and local context in a risky way. Pretrained word models already carry broad context from their own training, and the chunk vectors add the local theme. Embedding long files in one shot also strains the per-pass token cap and blurs themes. The safer order is split first into themed parts, then embed each part, so each vector keeps one clear meaning.

The re-explanation behind this answer is the manifest's shared-model intuition: one embedding model on both sides only works when each vector covers one theme, which the split-first order guarantees.

Q: With 10 sentences per chunk, how many tokens sit in one chunk and who decides?

A: The tokenizer decides, not the chunker. The chunker fixes the sentence count at ten, then the tokenizer turns those sentences into tokens. Choices include SentencePiece from Google and byte pair encoding from OpenAI, which splits at character level and costs more compute but splits rare words with care. Older word cuts on spaces, as in NLTK or spaCy tokenizers, are simpler but weaker on rare forms. So the same ten sentences can yield different token counts under different tokenizers.

A concrete sense of scale: ten plain sentences often land near 150 to 250 tokens, which fits the 384-token pass cap with room to spare. Dense technical sentences with rare terms can push higher under character-level splits, which is why the explore step counts tokens per page before storing anything.

Common traps to avoid: cutting on full stops so abbreviations shred sentences, skipping the explore step and discovering the token overflow only at embed time, and swapping embedding models mid-project so old and new vectors share one store. Each is cheap to prevent and costly to repair.

Hand code teaches the joints: ingest, parse, explore, chunk, embed, store, query, rank, generate. Next, tables and numeric specs break the prose assumption, so those pages get their own track.

3.5 Tables and Numbers

Dense vectors shine on prose but stumble on grids and digits. Tables carry meaning in rows, columns, and spans, and plain text parsing often drops that shape. Engine specs and finance sheets add dense numbers that current embedding tricks catch poorly, so a meaning vector may rank the wrong row first.

Think of photographing a chessboard versus listing its pieces in one long sentence. The photo keeps which piece stands on which square. The sentence loses the board. Vision models are the photo; plain parsing is the sentence.

Three fixes were discussed. First, treat pages as images and read them with vision-language models such as CLIP, which keep the grid shape that text parsing loses. A finance project with heavy tables compared plain parsing tools against vision models: text parsing won some pages, but vision models won more, and the paper from that work was accepted at a top venue. Second, keep numeric and coded facts in sparse form so exact strings match exactly instead of drifting through meaning space. Third, join both in a hybrid, since neither path covers every page type.

Finance reports with dense tables now pass through vision models that read each sheet as an image, keeping cell layout intact. Engine specs with heavy numbers pair sparse lookup with dense search so a part number lands exactly while its prose still matches by meaning.

Scope: the visual track fits pages where position carries meaning: merged cells, spanning headers, totals rows, and spec grids. Assumption: the page image renders at readable resolution and the vision model was trained on document layouts. When a table is small and regular, plain parsing plus sparse lookup stays cheaper and is enough.

Common traps to avoid: flattening a merged header into one garbled line, embedding a part number as prose so "519" drifts toward "520", and sending every page through the costly visual path when only the grid pages need it. Route by page shape, not by habit.

3.5.1 Student Questions and Answers

Q: How do we handle tables, where rows and columns link cells, and numeric specs that embeddings catch poorly?

A: Treat such pages as a separate track. Parse plain text where it works, but route grids and heavy numeric sheets through vision-language models that read the page as an image and keep its shape. Store codes and numbers in sparse form so exact strings hit exactly. Then join dense, sparse, and visual paths in a hybrid so each page type meets its best reader.

This answer carries the manifest's grid-shape warning into practice: meaning vectors alone misrank grid rows, so the pipeline needs a visual reader plus an exact-match store beside the dense index.

Prose chunks answer prose questions, but grids need eyes and codes need exact match. Next, graph RAG handles the case where the links between facts matter as much as the facts themselves.

3.6 Graph RAG for Linked Data

Naive RAG stores each chunk alone. That works for plain questions but drops links between facts. Medicine and finance break this design fast: one drug links to many genes, one illness carries many synonyms such as myocardial infarction and heart attack, and one event chains into others. Graph RAG exists for exactly this shape.

Think of a family tree pinned on a wall versus a shoebox of loose photos. The shoebox holds the same faces, but only the tree shows who married whom and who descends from whom. Naive chunks are the shoebox; the knowledge graph is the tree with labeled strings between photos.

A knowledge graph is a net of entities joined by named links. In a university page the entities include students, faculty, courses, and support staff, with links such as "student takes course" and "teacher teaches course". A family graph holds nodes such as Barack Obama, Michelle Obama, and their daughters, joined by links such as married and parent of. Each node and each link can be kept as plain strings for keyword search, as vectors for meaning search, or as both. Neo4j is the most used store for this shape, with Memgraph and NetworkX named as other options.

Build steps run in order. (1) Construct: pull entities and links from PDFs, sheets, RDF triples, JSON, or XML, by hand rules, dependency parsing, or a capable model, then clean the draft graph since auto-builds can emit thousands of nodes. (2) Store: write nodes, links, strings, and vectors into the graph store, with vector search available on top. (3) Query: turn the user question into entities and vectors, or into a graph query language such as Cypher, which reads like a simpler SQL for graphs, though model APIs can draft such queries from plain words. (4) Walk: traverse paths from matched nodes, across one hop for direct neighbours and two hops for indirect ones, and gather the nearby facts. (5) Generate: paste the walked facts next to the question and let the model draft. A hybrid build keeps both a vector store and a graph store for the same corpus and joins their contexts before generation.

Why the extra work pays off: path walks find facts that share no words with the question, multi-hop steps link distant causes, string storage keeps exact names searchable, and the visible links explain which path led to each claim. That last trait is called explainability, which means the answer can show its source trail.

Cost and fit still rule the choice. Graph builds need more steps, more cleaning, and more compute than naive stores. So the working rule is: start with naive RAG, test with an eval frame, and move to graph or hybrid only when measured scores rise on link-heavy queries. Simple question bots rarely need graphs, while research and domain reasoning tasks often do.

Medical assistants lean on graphs because drugs, genes, illnesses, and synonyms link in dense webs with many names for one thing. Finance desks use graphs where filings, firms, and events chain together. Web search itself leans on a large open knowledge graph for entity facts.

Scope: Graph RAG fits domains where links decide the answer: drug-gene webs, firm-event chains, course-prerequisite nets. Assumption: entities extract cleanly, edges stay current, and the question needs more than one hop. When questions need single paragraphs, or when the graph drifts stale, the extra build cost buys nothing.

Exam note: judge graph quality with an eval frame such as RAGAS, tracking faithfulness, answer relevance, recall, precision, and answer correctness. When edge cases fail, treat the build as a loop: fix data feeding, embedding, or graph shape, then test again on sampled edge queries with human review.

3.6.1 Mathematical Formulation

The spoken account was "store entities and links as strings plus vectors, then search both ways". Write a node vector as \(v_e \in \mathbb{R}^d\) for entity \(e\) and a link vector as \(r_l \in \mathbb{R}^d\) for relation \(l\), where \(d\) is the model width. A one-hop neighbour set \(N_1(e)\) holds directly linked nodes, while the two-hop set \(N_2(e)\) holds neighbours of neighbours. The spoken account was "one hop gives direct links, two hops give indirect links, and path walks gather the context".

Query matching reuses cosine form between the query vector \(q\) and node or link vectors:

\[ \text{match}(q, e) = \frac{q^\top v_e}{\|q\|_2 \, \|v_e\|_2} \]

Here \(v_e\) is the stored vector for entity \(e\), and the score reads exactly like chunk cosine from 3.2.1, only the target changed from chunk to node. Exact-name queries can also match stored strings directly, which is the sparse path inside the graph store. When both stores run, the hybrid context is the join of vector hits and graph-walk hits, ranked before generation.

A tiny walk shows the sets. From course node \(e\), suppose \(N_1(e) = \{\text{teacher}, \text{student A}, \text{student B}\}\). Then \(N_2(e)\) holds everyone linked to those three, such as the teacher's projects and each student's other courses. Result: one lookup fans out from 1 node to 3 neighbours to a dozen second-hop facts. The hop count is the recall knob: more hops gather more context at higher cost.

The two builds contrast on five fronts:

Aspect Naive vector RAG Graph RAG
Build Cut, embed, store; few steps Plus entity-link extraction and cleaning; more steps
Retrieval Cosine over lone chunks Path walks plus vector or string match
Reasoning Almost none Multi-hop links across entities
Explainability Low, score lists only Higher, paths show the trail
Fit Generic question bots Domains with dense links

The table's last row restates the selection rule: pick graph only when link-heavy queries lift measured scores enough to pay the build cost.

3.6.2 Worked Examples

University page graph. Setup: page text about students, faculty, and courses. Steps: pull the three entity sets, add links for takes and teaches, store nodes plus links. Suppose the extractor keeps 40 students, 8 faculty, and 12 courses with about 90 links. Result: a query about course links can walk from course to teacher to students in two hops. Sense-check: a flat chunk store would need all three facts in one chunk to match this walk, while the graph reaches them across edges.

Hybrid birthplace query. Setup: question asks where Emilia Yrton was born, with both a graph store and a vector store loaded. Steps: (1) pull entity Yrton from the question, (2) walk nearby graph paths up to the set hop count, (3) run vector search over text chunks in parallel with keyword search, (4) join both hit lists into one ranked context, (5) send the joined context with the question to the model. Result: birthplace facts from links plus supporting prose from chunks. Sense-check: the hop count and entity count are knobs the builder sets, trading recall against cost. Small counts answer fast; large counts roam wider but burn tokens and time.

3.6.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: If the top hit scores 0.92, does the missing 0.08 mean facts were lost, and do graph builds also drop small entities and weak links?

A: The 0.92 is a closeness score, not a lost share, so read it as rank strength rather than missing facts. Still, the deeper worry is real: graph builds can miss small entities and weak links even after many passes, and hybrid systems can still fail sharp edge queries. The fix is measurement plus loops. Score the system with an eval frame on faithfulness, relevance, recall, precision, and correctness, review edge samples by hand, and rebuild the weak parts, whether the fault sits in feeding, embedding, or graph shape. Rebuilds cost time and hardware, which is why this loop hurts, yet blind trust costs more.

This correction fixes two confusions at once: scores measure rank, not loss, while entity drops are a genuine build risk handled by eval-driven rebuilds.

Q: The model reads text, but we store graphs and vectors, so what exactly reaches the model?

A: Text reaches the model. Vectors serve only the search step through cosine ranking. Each stored vector keeps a link to its source text, so retrieval returns the matching text chunks. Those chunks join the prompt as plain text, and the model turns them into vectors internally in its own width before writing. Nothing about this path sends raw store vectors into the prompt.

The handoff is one-way: vectors rank, text travels, and the drafting model re-encodes the text its own way.

Q: The store uses one embedding model while the drafting model trained with another, so is that a mismatch?

A: The store side must stay self-consistent: one embedding model for both stored chunks and incoming queries. At drafting time the bundle travels as text tokens, and the drafting model encodes them its own way. Builders must still check widths and formats so the handoff stays clean, but there is no demand that the store model and the drafting model share weights.

This answer extends the shared-model rule from 3.2 and 3.4: consistency matters inside the store, while the drafting model meets the bundle as text.

Q: Can one corpus serve both vector and graph paths in a hybrid?

A: Yes. Keep the same corpus twice, once as chunks and once as a graph, then join both hit lists per question. When only some questions need links, an agent can route each question to the right path. Start simple with one path, test scores, and add the second path only when measured scores rise.

Common traps to avoid: skipping the cleaning pass so the graph floods with junk nodes, walking too many hops so the prompt fills with distant trivia, and adding a graph store before eval scores show the naive pipe failing. Each turns structure into noise.

Graphs pay when links matter and eval scores prove it: extract entities, walk hops, join with vector hits, and draft with the trail attached. Next, agentic RAG hands the choice of store and the retry loop to the model itself.

3.7 Agentic RAG

Naive and graph pipelines run fixed code written by a builder: chunk this way, pull top K that way, join, draft. Agentic RAG hands those choices to models at run time. An agent here is a model paired with memory, tools, and a plan loop that picks sources, calls them, checks the result, and retries when needed. Agentic AI is the wider term for such agents in any task, while agentic RAG is one use: agents running retrieval.

Think of a junior researcher given a hard question and a phone. A fixed pipe is a script: call these three numbers, read the answers, write up. An agent is the researcher unleashed: decide whom to call, ask follow-ups based on what comes back, check sources, and call again when the draft looks thin. The phone is the tool set; the judgment about whom to call next is the plan loop.

Each agent carries parts. The brain is a base model. Memory is an outside store, often vectors, that holds facts past the fixed context window. Tools reach further: web search APIs such as Tavily, calendars, team apps, calculators, and company stores. Planning splits the question into sub-tasks, picks which store or API serves each part, and orders the calls. Reflection then judges the draft and loops again when scores fall short.

The run loop is: analyse the question, plan the calls, run tools and stores, reason over hits, draft, score the draft, and either stop or loop. Scoring can use an eval frame such as RAGAS or plain accuracy checks, and builders cap the loop at about three to five rounds so cost stays bounded. This cap matters because agents have caused real harm when loops ran free, including runaway calls and damaged stores. The loop bound is a safety rail, not a nicety.

The price is real. Every planning, judging, and retry step burns model calls, time, and hardware. So agentic builds suit research and tangled multi-source tasks where the needed facts are unknown up front, while most bots do fine on simpler pipes. The shared rule from practice is: start with the simplest pipe, test with eval scores, and climb to graph or agentic designs only when scores demand it.

Research assistants show the payoff: they split one hard question into sub-questions, pull from web plus private stores plus calculators, and only trust hits with solid citations. Travel helpers join company policy stores with live flight search so one answer covers both rules and options.

Scope: Agentic RAG fits tangled tasks where the source list cannot be fixed up front: unknown stores, mixed years, live prices, multi-tool joins. Assumption: tools stay reachable, the judge score tracks real quality, and the round cap plus metadata keep the search bounded. When the source list is known and stable, a fixed pipe answers faster and cheaper.

Exam note: score agentic drafts with RAGAS-style checks such as faithfulness, answer relevance, recall, precision, and correctness. Output checks of this kind belong to the same family as the precision, recall, F1, and ERRANT checks used for text correction tasks, and a later review will walk them with simpler worked cases.

3.7.1 Mathematical Formulation

The spoken account was "one-shot fetch becomes plan, fetch, judge, and loop until good enough or rounds run out". Write the question as \(x\) and round \(t\) context as \(C_t\). Each round drafts:

\[ y_t = G(x, C_t) \]

Here \(y_t\) is the draft at round \(t\), \(G\) is the generator, \(x\) is the fixed question, and \(C_t\) is the context gathered up to round \(t\). Early rounds carry thin context; later rounds carry context plus prior drafts and judge notes.

A judge score \(s_t = J(y_t, C_t)\) rates the draft, where \(J\) is either an eval frame or a judge model. The loop stops when \(s_t \ge \tau\) for a set bar \(\tau\), or when round count \(t\) reaches the cap \(N \in \{3, 4, 5\}\). The spoken account for the cap was "keep three to five rounds so agent calls stay bounded". Agentic cost grows with rounds times calls per round, which is why the cap and the bar must both be set before launch.

A tiny trace shows the stop rule. Suppose \(\tau = 0.8\) and scores return \(s_1 = 0.62\), \(s_2 = 0.74\), \(s_3 = 0.86\) with \(N = 5\). Rounds 1 and 2 fall short, so the agent replans and retries; round 3 clears the bar, so the loop stops and \(y_3\) ships. If instead scores stalled at \(0.70\) through round 5, the cap would stop the loop anyway, and the builder would see a bounded bill plus a flagged weak answer rather than a runaway tab.

Metadata trims the search. Short labels on each store, such as which topics or years each holds, help the planner pick the right source on round one instead of probing all stores in loops. The manifest's label intuition is a cost saver: one good tag can replace two wasted rounds.

3.7.2 Worked Examples

Clean energy comparison. Setup: decide between nuclear and solar output for a given year worldwide, judged by return against input. Steps: (1) split into sub-tasks for nuclear output, solar output, and shared units, (2) pull each series from trusted sources with matching year and energy units, (3) call a calculator API for unit shifts, (4) drop uncited blog claims and keep sourced numbers, (5) join the series and draft the compare. Suppose nuclear returns 2700 TWh and solar returns 1300 TWh for the target year in matching units, a ratio of about 2.1 to 1. Result: a same-unit same-year table plus a judged pick. Sense-check: the ratio answers the return-against-input question only because units and years match; mismatched units would flip the pick.

Policy plus booking. Setup: user asks company travel rules and wants to book a trip. Steps: (1) fetch rules from the company store, (2) fetch flight options from a web search API, (3) join rules with options, (4) draft one plan that obeys policy and lists flights. Result: a single grounded itinerary. Sense-check: neither store alone answers both halves, which is why the agent must call two tools. A fixed pipe wired to one store would miss half the question by construction.

3.7.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: What splits agentic AI from agentic RAG?

A: Agentic AI is the wider set: agents with brain, memory, tools, and plans for any task. Agentic RAG narrows that frame to retrieval: the agent plans which store or API holds each fact, fetches it, joins it, and drafts with citations. All agentic RAG is agentic AI, but not all agentic AI does retrieval.

This boundary answer stops the two labels from blurring: agency is the engine, retrieval is one road it can drive.

Q: Does the agentic loop skip building a store and just search the web?

A: No. Stores still sit in the back end, whether vector stores, graph stores, or both. The change is who picks: the agent chooses per question whether to read the private store, the graph, the web, or a mix. Without built stores the agent has nothing private to pick, so the web-only shortcut reintroduces the very gap RAG was built to close.

Q: Must builders tell the agent what each store holds?

A: Labels help a lot. Metadata such as topics, years, and source type lets the planner aim at the right store on the first try. Builders can also sketch a light workflow that hints which query shape suits which store. That hint costs some builder effort but saves many wasted agent rounds.

The manifest marks this label answer as useful rather than essential, but in practice it is the cheapest cost control in the whole agentic stack: good tags beat extra rounds.

Common traps to avoid: launching without a round cap, judging with a weak scorer so bad drafts pass, and pointing the agent at stale stores so retries polish outdated facts. Each turns an adaptive loop into an expensive way to be wrong.

Agentic RAG trades fixed code for run-time judgment: plan, call tools, draft, judge, and loop within bounds. It wins on tangled multi-source tasks and loses on simple lookups where fixed pipes answer faster. The lecture's ladder is complete: naive vectors, then chunking and ranking, then scratch code, then grids, then graphs, then agents.

Key Industry Applications

Bank report models use RAG to serve customer facts per group while keeping private data in-house, since each of the many report builders reads only its permitted slice. Telecom testing teams query spec PDFs through RAG to track new decoding features per release, turning a long hunt through modules into one grounded question. HR and travel bots answer role-wise policy questions from company PDFs, so staff get the rule tied to their role and tenure rather than generic web text.

Medical assistants use graph stores to link drugs, genes, illnesses, and synonyms, which lets one question walk from a drug to its genes to its indications. Finance desks use graphs over filings and events, where the chain between actors matters as much as each filing alone. Research assistants use agentic loops with web, private stores, calculators, and citation filters, splitting hard questions into sub-tasks and keeping only sourced numbers.

The open stores and frames behind these builds include Hugging Face sentence models, Pinecone, Qdrant, Neo4j, LangChain, LangGraph, PyMuPDF, SentencePiece, byte pair encoding, CLIP for visual pages, Tavily for web search, and the RAGAS eval frame with BM25, TF-IDF, reciprocal rank fusion, and maximal marginal relevance as ranking aids. The selection rule travels with the list: start with the simplest pipe, measure with eval scores, and climb only when scores demand it.
