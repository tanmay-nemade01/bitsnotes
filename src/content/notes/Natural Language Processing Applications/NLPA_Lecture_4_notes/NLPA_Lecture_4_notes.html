4.1 Why RAG Exists and How the Three Main Designs Differ

A large language model on its own can only answer from what it learned during training. When a question needs fresh facts, private enterprise files, or domain records, the model needs help. Retrieval Augmented Generation, often shortened to RAG, fills this gap. RAG pulls facts from outside sources and feeds them to the model with the question. It pulls small passages from an outside data source, places them next to the user question, and asks the model to build its answer from that shared material.

Hook: Imagine a brilliant colleague with no internet access and no permission to open your company drive. They know a lot, yet they cannot answer "what did the client approve last week?" RAG is the act of handing that colleague the right page before asking the question.

Three gaps motivate the design, and each one is worth naming. First, models hallucinate: they produce fluent text that is not faithful to any source, and they state it with full confidence. Grounding the answer in fetched passages gives every claim a source to point at. Second, models cannot see proprietary data: personal mail, medical records, internal tickets, and legal files were never in the public training set, yet most enterprise questions live there. Third, models go stale: training ends on a fixed date, while prices, guidelines, and code change every week. Retrieval fixes all three at once, because the fetched passages can be fresh, private, and cited.

Every RAG design follows the same broad shape. First comes data ingestion and indexing, done offline before any user arrives. Then a user question arrives and the system works out what the question needs. Then comes retrieval of matching passages. Then the question plus the retrieved passages go to the model, which writes the final answer. Naive RAG, Graph RAG, and Agentic RAG all keep this shape. They differ in how they index, how they decide what to fetch, and who controls the search.

The shared pipeline: ingest and index offline, understand the question, fetch matching passages, then generate the answer from the question plus the fetched passages. Every design in this lecture is a variation on these four steps.

In naive RAG the archive is first split into small pieces. Each piece passes through an embedding model, a model that turns text into a numeric vector. The vectors go into a vector database, a store built for fast similarity search. When a user question arrives, it passes through the same embedding model, so the question vector and the archive vectors live in one shared space. The system ranks archive pieces by similarity to the question and keeps the top one or two.

The plain-words account given for this step is that the query is converted into a vector with the same technique used for the archive, and only then can the two be compared in a fair way. That shared-space rule matters. When the same technique embeds both sides, similarity scores stay valid. When the techniques differ, the scores lose their meaning.

Intuition: think of prices in two currencies. A price of 900 and a price of 12 cannot be compared until both sit in the same currency. An embedding (a list of numbers that stands for the meaning of a piece of text) plays the role of the currency here: the question and every archive piece must be converted with the same model before any comparison is fair. The mapping breaks where currency breaks too: conversion preserves value, not the coins themselves, so two different sentences can land near each other when they mean nearly the same thing.

4.1.1 Mathematical Formulation

The ranking step in naive RAG rests on one formula plus one construction rule. The construction rule, stated in direct terms, is that sentence vectors come from blending token vectors, for example by taking the mean or the max across the token vectors in a sentence. Token vectors with 768 numbers per word were named as the running example, matching contextual word vectors from courses on language processing. A sentence vector is then the average or max-pool of its word vectors. In symbols, for a sentence with token vectors \(t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_m\), each in \(\mathbb{R}^{768}\), the mean-pooled sentence vector is:

\[ s = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} t_i \]

Here \(m\) is the count of tokens in the sentence, \(t_i\) is the vector of token \(i\), and \(s \in \mathbb{R}^{768}\) is the resulting sentence vector. Each of the 768 slots in \(s\) is the plain average of that slot across the \(m\) token vectors. Max-pooling swaps the average for a per-slot maximum. Either way, one sentence leaves the step as one vector with the same width as the token vectors that built it.

The ranking formula is the cosine similarity. For a question vector \(q\) and an archive vector \(d\), the score is the dot product of the two vectors divided by the product of their lengths:

\[ \text{cos}(q, d) = \frac{q^\top d}{\|q\|_2 \, \|d\|_2} \]

Here \(q \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is the question vector, \(d \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is one archive vector, \(q^\top d\) is their dot product (slot-by-slot multiply, then add), and \(\|q\|_2\) and \(\|d\|_2\) are their lengths. A high score means the two vectors point in nearly the same direction. The archive piece with the highest score is fetched first. In the toy demo covered later, six short archive pieces each get one vector, one question vector is built, six cosine scores are computed, and the top piece wins.

Why the two sides must share the model: the score above only means "same direction" when \(q\) and \(d\) were built by the same embedding map. A question vector from one model and an archive vector from another live in unrelated spaces, so their dot product is noise. Notation texts often write a generic similarity as \(s(q, d)\); here we keep \(\text{cos}(q, d)\) to match the lecture.

One more check keeps the math sound. When \(q\) and \(d\) share the same dimension \(n\), the dot product \(q^\top d\) is a single number, and each norm is a single positive number, so their ratio is a single number. That number is high when the vectors align and low when they do not. As a spot check, take \(q = [1, 0]\) and \(d = [1, 0]\): the dot product is \(1\), each length is \(1\), and the score is \(1\), the maximum, exactly as "identical direction" demands. Flip \(d\) to \([0, 1]\) and the dot product is \(0\), so the score is \(0\), exactly as "unrelated direction" demands. A score of \(1\) always marks perfect alignment and a score of \(0\) marks no overlap, so rankings read the same way on every question. A common spoken form such as "w trans x" maps to \(w^\top x\), with the transpose mark written as \(\top\).

Naive RAG builds each vector on its own. It never records how two pieces relate. That is a fair price for speed, and it is also the main limit. The lecture's own picture is that naive RAG knows what each card says but never draws lines between the cards. Graph RAG draws those lines. It first pulls out entities, the named things in the archive, and relations, the links between them, and stores both as a knowledge graph. Indexing costs more here because relation finding takes added work, sometimes with model calls over each file. Search pays back that cost when questions span several hops, since the graph already holds the paths.

Agentic RAG adds a decision maker. An agent, usually a model asked to reason about the task, looks at the question and decides whether an outside store is needed at all, which store to use, and whether to split the question into parts. The plain-words account is that the agent itself breaks the query into sub-parts and picks the path. That freedom brings cost. Each reasoning step is a model call, and model calls take time and money at answer time. So the three designs form a ladder. Naive RAG is cheap and fast. Graph RAG costs more at indexing time but answers linked questions well. Agentic RAG reasons at answer time and costs the most.

Scope: the ladder assumes a plain text archive with one shared embedding space. It breaks when questions hinge on exact codes (vectors drift past letter-exact strings, so a sparse arm is needed) and when the archive is images rather than words (a text-only embedder cannot read pixels, so a joint model is needed). Both fixes appear later in this lecture.

4.1.2 Worked Examples

The full demo with numbers lives in section 4.5. The short form is this. Six one-sentence archive pieces each receive one sentence vector. The question "Who supervises Ravi Kumar?" receives one question vector from the same model. Six cosine scores are computed. The top piece names the supervisor. All three designs return the right answer here, which sets up the cost lesson of section 4.4.

Mini trace (toy 3-slot vectors, real arithmetic): let the question vector be \(q = [1, 1, 0]\) and two archive vectors be \(d_1 = [1, 1, 0.1]\) (the supervisor sentence) and \(d_2 = [0, 0.2, 1]\) (an unrelated sentence). Step 1, dot products: \(q^\top d_1 = 1 + 1 + 0 = 2\) and \(q^\top d_2 = 0 + 0.2 + 0 = 0.2\). Step 2, lengths: \(\|q\|_2 = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.4142\), \(\|d_1\|_2 = \sqrt{2.01} \approx 1.4177\), \(\|d_2\|_2 = \sqrt{1.04} \approx 1.0198\). Step 3, scores: \(\text{cos}(q, d_1) = 2 / (1.4142 \times 1.4177) \approx \textbf{0.998}\) and \(\text{cos}(q, d_2) = 0.2 / (1.4142 \times 1.0198) \approx \textbf{0.139}\). The supervisor sentence wins by a wide margin. Sense check: the near-parallel pair scores near 1 while the near-orthogonal pair scores near 0, which is exactly how a ranking should behave. Production vectors carry hundreds of slots instead of three, but the three steps never change.

Pitfalls: (1) embedding the question with a different model than the archive silently breaks every score, because the two spaces no longer line up; (2) reading a high cosine as proof the passage answers the question, when it only proves similar wording; (3) reaching for Graph or Agentic designs on single-fact lookups, where the extra machinery adds cost without changing the answer.

In naive RAG the archive is split, turned into vectors with an embedding model, and stored in a vector database. The question is embedded the same way, close vectors are fetched, and the question plus the fetched passages go to the model. Graph RAG instead builds a knowledge graph of entities and relations. Recap: RAG grounds a model in fetched passages so fresh, private, and cited facts reach the answer. 4.2 Vectorless RAG and the Exact Match Problem

Not every archive needs vectors. Vectorless RAG keeps text as text. It stores the raw words in a classic index, such as an inverted file of the kind used by Lucene index or Apache Solr setups, and matches questions with string-level scores such as TF-IDF or BM25. The plain-words account is that the words stay as they are in the index, with no shift into vectors. Vectorless RAG stores text as plain words in the index instead of turning it into vectors, much like BM25 or TF-IDF matching. Long before learned vectors, web search ran this way, and course search tools still use it.

Hook: ask a dense-only system for course code "AIMLCZG565" and it may return a page about a different course whose description sounds similar. The letters must match exactly, and meaning-similarity is the wrong tool for that job.

An inverted index (a map from each word to the list of documents holding it, plus counts) makes this fast: the system looks up each question word, intersects the lists, and scores only the documents that share words with the question. Two classic scores sit on top of that index. TF-IDF (term frequency times inverse document frequency) weights a word by how often it appears in the passage divided down by how common it is across the whole archive, so a rare code counts far more than the word "the." BM25 refines the same idea with two dampers: term frequency saturates so the tenth repeat adds less than the first, and long documents are penalized so length alone cannot win. Short notes on vectorless matching will be shared, and related courses on conversational systems also cover it. It is not part of the exam scope here, but it matters for hybrid designs.

The strong point of vectorless search is exact match. A course code, a DNA string, a drug name, a disease name, or a stock ticker must match letter for letter. Vectors are built to find near neighbors in meaning, so they can drift past the exact string. A string index does not drift. Vectors drift past exact codes, so the sparse string arm carries exact match. It returns the passage that holds the exact code or name.

When strings beat vectors: any question whose answer is a fixed token (code, clause number, ticker, DNA string) belongs to the sparse arm. Embeddings smooth over surface form, which is a strength for paraphrase and a failure mode for identifiers.

The weak point is the mirror image. A string index cannot see that two different words mean nearly the same thing. Vector search can. That is why working systems often join the two. The archive is stored twice, once as dense vectors and once as sparse word-based entries, and each question is run against both sides. The two ranked lists are then fused, often with reciprocal rank fusion, a rule that blends ranks from several lists into one order. Dense retrieval brings the semantically near passages while sparse retrieval brings the exact matches, and fusion keeps the best of both.

Toy fusion trace: a question asks for penal code "Section 302." Dense search returns passages P1 (rank 1), P2 (rank 2), P3 (rank 3); none holds the literal code. Sparse BM25 returns P3 (rank 1), P7 (rank 2), P1 (rank 3), because only P3 and P7 hold the exact string. Reciprocal rank fusion scores each passage by \(1/(k + \text{rank})\) per list with \(k = 60\) and adds the two: P1 gets \(1/61 + 1/63 \approx \textbf{0.0323}\), P3 gets \(1/63 + 1/61 \approx \textbf{0.0323}\), P2 gets \(1/62 + 0 = \textbf{0.0161}\), P7 gets \(0 + 1/62 \approx \textbf{0.0161}\). P3 rises to the top tier because both arms vote for it. Sense check: a passage ranked well by both arms always beats one ranked well by a single arm, which is exactly the behavior a hybrid wants.

Scope: vectorless search assumes the question shares words with the answer. It breaks under heavy paraphrase ("heart attack" asked, "myocardial infarction" stored) and across languages. That blind spot is exactly what the dense arm covers, which is why production systems run both.

Pitfalls: (1) treating BM25 scores and cosine scores as directly comparable numbers, when only ranks survive fusion; (2) indexing raw text with no cleaning, so spelling variants split the same term into strangers; (3) dropping the sparse arm because "vectors are modern," then failing every code lookup.

4.3 Multimodal RAG

Multimodal RAG widens the archive beyond words. Images, tables, charts, PNG pages, and clips sit next to prose. The pipeline keeps its familiar shape of ingestion, chunking, embedding, indexing, and retrieval, but the embedding step changes. A text-only embedding model cannot read pixels, so the system uses joint or vision-based models that map pictures and words into one shared space. Use a multimodal RAG design rather than a text-only one for image-input archives.

Hook: a user types "red birthday cake with candles" and the archive holds ten thousand party photos with no captions. No word index can answer, because the answer is made of pixels, not tokens.

The running example uses a photo of a dog and the sentence "this is a photo of a dog." A joint model such as CLIP turns the sentence into a vector of, say, 512 numbers and turns the dog photo into a vector in the same space. Because training pairs each picture with its caption, the caption vector lands near its own picture vector and far from a cat photo vector. A user who types "dog photo" then pulls the dog picture, not the cat picture. The plain-words account is that text vectors and image vectors are trained to sit close when they describe the same thing, and cosine similarity then does the rest. In symbols, for a text vector \(t \in \mathbb{R}^n\) and an image vector \(v \in \mathbb{R}^n\) from the same joint model, closeness is again \(\text{cos}(t, v) = \frac{t^\top v}{\|t\|_2 \, \|v\|_2}\), with the same reading as in section 4.1.

The joint-space contract: one model must produce both the text vectors and the image vectors, and its training must pair pictures with their captions. Only then does "close" mean "same content" across modalities. A 512-slot CLIP vector for a caption and a 512-slot CLIP vector for a photo are comparable; a text-only sentence vector and raw pixels are not.

Toy cross-modal trace: take 2-slot stand-ins \(t = [0.9, 0.1]\) for the caption "dog photo," \(v_{dog} = [0.85, 0.15]\) for the dog picture, and \(v_{cat} = [0.1, 0.9]\) for a cat picture. Cosine of caption to dog: dot \(0.9 \times 0.85 + 0.1 \times 0.15 = 0.78\); lengths \(\|t\|_2 \approx 0.9055\), \(\|v_{dog}\|_2 \approx 0.8631\); score \(0.78 / 0.7816 \approx \textbf{0.998}\). Cosine of caption to cat: dot \(0.9 \times 0.1 + 0.1 \times 0.9 = 0.18\); \(\|v_{cat}\|_2 \approx 0.9055\); score \(0.18 / 0.82 \approx \textbf{0.22}\). The dog picture wins by a wide margin. Sense check: paired training pulls matched pairs together and pushes the rest apart, so the caption lands on its own picture.

Two practical twists follow. First, input form and archive form can differ. A user often types words to find pictures, for example a birthday photo set or all photos tied to one person name. The back end then labels one face, embeds it, and pulls other images whose feature vectors sit near it. Image-to-image search is also valid, where an uploaded photo finds near photos, but text-to-image is the common path. Second, tables inside PDF files now often stay as images. Flattened tables lose links between cells, so teams keep tables as pictures for vision models. When a table is flattened to plain rows, relation links between cells can break. Teams keep the table as a picture and run a vision language model over it, which keeps cell relations intact. Gemini was named as a popular choice for image-heavy content, and open source vision models fill the same slot where cost or control matters.

Scope: joint search works when the query and archive describe the same visible content. It breaks on questions that need reading fine print inside a low-resolution scan, counting exact cell values across merged spreadsheet cells, or matching a face the model never saw in training. For those, keep the raw file alongside the vector so the generator can look at the source pixels.

Size is the tax. Image vectors and image archives are large. Vector stores such as Pinecone and Milvus accept multimodal vectors, and cloud platforms hold the raw files when local disks fall short. A demo link set for the RAG webinar was promised, with Graph RAG and multimodal coverage, because local setups often lack the needed resources.

Pitfalls: (1) feeding images through a text-only embedder, which silently returns vectors that mean nothing; (2) flattening merged-cell tables into rows, which breaks the very relations the question needs; (3) storing vectors but deleting the source images, leaving the generator nothing to cite or show.

4.4 Choosing the Right RAG Design

An AI specialist should not reach for the heaviest tool by habit. Simple single-hop questions with one fact each are served well by naive RAG. Setup is plain, answer time is fast, and cost stays low. A standard FAQ list is the textbook case.

Hook: nobody hires a detective to find a phone book entry. Match the machinery to the mystery: one fact needs one fetch, linked facts need a graph, and an unclear path needs a planner.

Graph RAG fits when relations carry the answer. Multi-hop questions, biomedical links, legal research chains, and any archive where entities join through named relations all point to a graph. The price is indexing time and graph size. Relation finding costs work up front, hop search costs time per question, and a large graph can grow hard to scale.

Agentic RAG fits when the path itself is unclear. The agent plans, picks tools, reads outside sources, reflects, and joins partial answers from memory. That loop can answer hard research questions, but each loop step is a model call, so latency shifts with the question and cost runs highest of the three.

Question shape Reach for Why Watch out for
One fact, one passage (FAQ, refund timing, store hours) Naive RAG Cheapest and fastest; one fetch answers it Extra machinery adds cost without changing the answer
Linked facts, hop chains (drug-disease-age, research trails) Graph RAG Relations are stored, so hops are walks, not guesses Indexing cost and graph size at scale
Unclear path, mixed sources (finance desk, deep research) Agentic RAG Plans, picks tools, and joins partial answers Highest answer-time cost; latency shifts per question

The working rule: start with naive RAG. Move to Graph RAG only when relations are needed. Move to Agentic RAG only when neither gives good results. Simple factual lookups never need a reasoning agent, and paying for one there is waste.

The cost ladder of naive, graph, and agentic designs is the kind of comparison that suits short written answers. Expect to justify a pick in one paragraph: name the question shape, name the design, and name the price paid.

Scope: the ladder assumes the answer lives in the archive. It breaks when the archive holds no relevant passage (then the fix is coverage, not a heavier design) and when the question needs an exact code (then a sparse arm matters more than graph or agent machinery).

Pitfalls: (1) defaulting to an agent loop for prestige, then paying reasoning calls on questions one fetch could answer; (2) building a graph over an archive with no real relations, then paying relation-finding cost for zero gain; (3) judging only accuracy while ignoring indexing time, answer latency, and scale pain.

Recap: one fact points to naive search, linked facts point to a graph, and an unclear path points to an agent, with cost rising at each step. Bridge: the next section proves the ladder on a shared six-document toy archive where every design faces the same three questions. Exam note: no direct exam item was tied to this section, but the cost ladder of naive, graph, and agentic designs is the kind of comparison that suits short written answers.

A customer help desk with fixed refund and ordering questions runs well on naive RAG. A finance research desk that weighs risk, horizon, and budget across many stocks needs planning and tools, which points to Agentic RAG.

4.5 Comparative Demo Over a Six-Document Toy Archive

A shared demo builds one tiny archive of six short pieces and asks three kinds of questions against it. The archive is small on purpose so each design choice stays clear. Real archives are far larger, with far longer pieces, but the mechanics match.

Hook: three runners, one track, three very different races: a sprint to one fact, a survey of the whole field, and a relay across linked posts. The same six documents host all three, so only the question shape changes.

4.5.1 Setup and Indexing

Indexing runs offline, before any question arrives. Each design indexes the same six pieces in its own way.

Naive RAG turns each one-sentence piece into one sentence vector with a model such as a sentence transformer, Cohere embeddings, or any open embedding model, and stores the six vectors in a vector store such as Qdrant, Milvus, or even a local tensor structure in code. Graph RAG reads the same six pieces, pulls out entities and relations, at times with model calls, and stores the result as a graph. Agentic RAG can index either way, as plain vectors or as a graph, because its added value sits at answer time, not at indexing time.

Purpose, inputs, outputs: the demo tests whether question shape alone predicts the winner. Input: six one-sentence documents plus three questions of rising link-depth. Output: a correct answer plus a cost receipt showing what each design paid.

4.5.2 Question One: A Simple Factual Lookup

The first question is "Who supervises Ravi Kumar?" One entity sits at the center, one named supervisor answers it, and no chain of links is needed.

Graph RAG treats the question as an entity task. It pulls out the entity Ravi Kumar with a named entity extractor and walks the supervision link. Naive RAG treats the question as a vector task. It embeds the full question with the same model used for the archive and ranks the six pieces by cosine similarity:

\[ \text{score}_i = \frac{q^\top d_i}{\|q\|_2 \, \|d_i\|_2} \]

Here \(q \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is the question vector and \(d_i \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is the vector of archive piece \(i\). The top piece states the supervisor name and answers the question on its own. Agentic RAG asks its reasoning model first, which judges the question a plain factual type, skips outside tools, and runs a plain fetch that returns the same top piece.

Worked lookup: six one-sentence pieces each carry one sentence vector from the shared model, and the question "Who supervises Ravi Kumar?" carries one question vector from that same model. Step 1, score every piece with the per-document cosine above. Step 2, sort the six scores from high to low. Step 3, read the top piece: it names the supervisor, so the answer is that name. All three designs return the right answer here. Sense check: a one-entity question needs no hop and no plan, so the cheapest reader wins the cost comparison by default.

All three designs answer well, and that is the point. For a single lookup, plain naive RAG is the right pick. It is cheapest and fastest. For a single lookup, plain naive RAG is the right pick because it is cheapest and fastest. Graph RAG pays for entity finding and graph building it did not need. Agentic RAG pays for reasoning calls it did not need. Reaching for an agentic loop on such simple facts is overkill: the agent adds reasoning calls without changing the answer, so plain naive RAG is the cheapest and fastest pick here.

4.5.3 Question Two: A Summary Across the Archive

The second question is "What are the main themes across these documents?" This is a summary task over all six pieces, not a lookup of one.

Graph RAG groups pieces that share meaning into communities. In the demo it returns two theme groups, academic research and clinical use, by following entity and relation overlap. Naive RAG has no group step. Its six vectors were built apart from each other, so it cannot see shared themes. It may return one theme or a partial answer. Agentic RAG can still answer through planning, reasoning, and joining steps, but that loop needs several model calls where the graph needs fewer.

Worked summary: the six vectors are scored on their own in naive search, so each fetch returns one neighborhood, and the generator sees one theme. The graph instead walks entity and relation overlap across all six pieces and finds two dense clusters: three pieces sharing research terms and three sharing clinical terms. The graph returns two theme groups, academic research and clinical use. Sense check: themes are relations between documents, so only the design that stores relations can report them directly.

4.5.4 Question Three: A Multi-Hop Chain

The third question is "In which city is the hospital that uses the model built by doctors?" The answer needs a chain: doctors build a model, a hospital uses that model, and that hospital sits in a city. No single piece holds the full chain. The chain runs doctors build a model, a hospital uses that model, and that hospital sits in a city.

Graph RAG follows its stored path from doctors to model to hospital to city and reads off the end. Agentic RAG keeps running notes in memory, plans the hops, fetches each link, reflects, and joins them into the final city. Both reach the answer. Naive RAG, with no links stored, cannot cross the hops and falls short. Between the two winners, the graph path is usually the more direct one, while the agent loop costs more.

Worked chain: hop 1 finds the piece where doctors build the model and names the model. Hop 2 searches for that model name and finds the piece where a hospital uses it. Hop 3 searches for that hospital and finds the piece naming its city. The final answer is the city at the end of the stored path. Sense check: each hop names the search key for the next hop, so a missing link breaks the chain at a visible point.

Pitfalls: (1) grading designs on the lookup alone, where all three tie, instead of on the linked questions that separate them; (2) forgetting the cost column: the agent matches the graph on hops but pays model calls per hop; (3) assuming a toy-archive win transfers without retesting, when real archives add noise, duplicates, and scale pain.

4.6 Hybrid Retrieval and the Zero-Miss Debate

Dense vectors miss exact strings, sparse word indexes miss near meanings, and hard questions need both. The hybrid answer stores each passage twice, once as a dense vector and once as a sparse word entry, runs the question against both arms, and fuses the two ranked lists. Dense vectors bring near-meaning passages and the string arm brings exact hits. Fusion rules such as reciprocal rank fusion blend the lists so passages that score well on either side rise. BM25 often serves as the sparse arm.

Hook: a petabyte-scale team tried plain search, then graph search, and still lost corner cases. Only when they ran meaning search and keyword search side by side did the misses close. Neither arm alone covers both failure modes.

A practitioner story made the point concrete. On a very large archive, plain RAG and even Graph RAG left corner cases unfound. A mix of dense retrieval with a keyword arm built on an inverted index closed the gap and reached clean results on the team's data. The price was a hybrid pipeline with added latency, since two arms plus fusion take more time than one arm.

The reply agreed with the core claim and set its edge. Zero misses are hard to promise. Dense arms drift on exact codes, sparse arms miss near meanings, and only the pair covers both. When exact codes lead, the sparse arm carries the load. When meaning leads, the dense arm does. Caching of repeat questions, precompute of common answers, parallel arms, and smaller quantized vectors all trim the latency tax.

The reciprocal-rank rule: for each list, a passage at rank \(r\) earns \(1/(k + r)\) with \(k = 60\) by convention, and its fused score is the sum across arms. A passage ranked first by one arm and twentieth by the other still beats a passage ranked fifth by one arm alone, because two votes outweigh one.

Worked fusion on the corner case: the keyword arm ranks the exact-code passage P3 first and a paraphrase passage P1 tenth; the dense arm ranks P1 second and P3 eighth. With \(k = 60\): P3 earns \(1/61 + 1/68 \approx \textbf{0.0311}\), P1 earns \(1/70 + 1/62 \approx \textbf{0.0304}\), and a dense-only leader P2 at rank 1 with no sparse vote earns \(1/61 + 0 \approx \textbf{0.0164}\). P3 wins because both arms vote for it. Sense check: the exact-code passage survives even though the dense arm buried it, which is exactly the corner case the hybrid exists to catch.

Scope: hybrid fusion assumes both arms index the same passages. It breaks when the sparse index and the vector store drift apart (different chunking, different cleaning), because ranks then refer to different units. Keep chunking and cleaning identical on both arms.

Pitfalls: (1) promising zero misses, when each arm keeps a blind spot and the pair only shrinks the gap; (2) fusing raw scores from incompatible scales instead of ranks; (3) running the arms in sequence and paying double latency, when parallel arms plus caching cost far less.

4.8 RAGAS Evaluation Framework

Evaluation asks whether the system fetched the right passages and whether the final answer used them well. RAGAS is the popular open framework for this job. Its measures split across two stages. Some sit at the retrieval stage and judge the fetched chunks. Some sit at the generation stage and judge the final answer. Relevancy and faithfulness judge the final answer; recall and precision judge the fetched chunks. That split is the first thing to fix in mind, because each score answers a different question.

Hook: a system can fail in two places: it fetches the wrong pages, or it ignores the right ones. One score cannot tell those apart, so RAGAS scores each stage separately.

Score Stage Asks Needs
Faithfulness (groundedness) Generation Did the answer stay inside the fetched chunks? Fetched chunks, no outside truth needed
Answer relevancy Generation Did the answer fit the user question? Question plus answer claims
Context recall Retrieval What share of needed facts arrived? List of facts the question truly needs
Context precision Retrieval What share of fetched material was useful? Relevance label per fetched chunk
Answer correctness Both Did the answer fit and stay grounded? Both sides; broadest single score

The stage split: retrieval scores judge the fetched set against the question's needs; generation scores judge the final answer against the fetched set and the question. Read them together: low generation scores with high retrieval scores blame the writer, while low retrieval scores blame the fetcher.

4.8.1 Faithfulness at the Generation Stage

Faithfulness, also called groundedness or generated precision, asks whether the final answer stays inside the fetched passages. The plain-words account is that the fetched chunks are the allowed source, and any claim outside them counts as unsupported. In symbols, for a generated answer split into small claims:

\[ \text{Faithfulness} = \frac{\text{supported claims}}{\text{total claims}} \]

Here a claim is one checkable statement in the answer, a supported claim is one the fetched passages back, and total claims is the count of all such statements. High faithfulness means low hallucination against the fetched passages: the output came from the given material. Low faithfulness means weak grounding in the fetched chunks: the model wrote from its own memory instead.

Worked faithfulness (one of two claims grounded): the fetched chunks support one statement but the answer gives two, with the second drawn from training memory. Step 1, split the answer into claims: claim 1 (in the chunks), claim 2 (not in the chunks). Step 2, count: supported claims \(= 1\), total claims \(= 2\). Step 3, divide: \(\text{Faithfulness} = 1/2 = \textbf{0.5}\). The answer is half grounded. Sense check: one backed claim out of two must score one half, and adding more unbacked claims can only pull the score down.

A worked case makes the edge sharp. Suppose the fetched chunks support one statement but the answer gives two, with the second drawn from training memory. Then one of two claims is supported, so faithfulness is one half. The unsupported second claim is counted as hallucinated against the fetched material, even when it happens to be true in the world. The Python-inventor case shows why. A correct real-world fact with no backing in the fetched chunks scores zero faithfulness. That zero does not mean the fact is false. It means the system ignored its fetched passages, which points to a broken fetch, wrong cleaning, or wrong embedding rather than a wrong fact. Hallucination here is always read against the fetched material, not against world truth.

The misconception and its fix: a correct real-world fact with no backing in the fetched chunks scores zero faithfulness, which looks like the metric calling truth false. It is not. The score asks "did the answer come from the fetched material," not "is this true in the world." A zero here flags the fetch or the embedding, not the fact. Fix the chunking, cleaning, or embedding so the right passages arrive.

4.8.2 Answer Relevancy at the Generation Stage

Answer relevancy asks whether the final answer fits the user question. It never looks at the fetched chunks. The plain-words account is that the final claims are set next to the question and each claim is judged for fit. In symbols:

\[ \text{Answer Relevancy} = \frac{\text{relevant claims}}{\text{total claims}} \]

Here total claims is again the count of checkable statements in the answer, and relevant claims are those that speak to the question asked. A worked case used an earbud question with four answer claims about phone fit, spatial audio, laptop use, and battery life. Two of the four spoke to the asked question, so the score was two out of four. Labels for which claims count as relevant may come from people or from model-based claim splitting, but some outside judgment is needed.

Worked relevancy (two of four claims on point): the earbud question asks about phone fit, and the answer gives four claims: phone fit (relevant), spatial audio (off point), laptop use (off point), battery life (relevant). Step 1, label each claim against the question: relevant \(= 2\). Step 2, count total claims \(= 4\). Step 3, divide: \(\text{Answer Relevancy} = 2/4 = \textbf{0.5}\). Half the answer speaks to the question asked. Sense check: padding an answer with true but off-point facts leaves faithfulness high while relevancy falls, which is exactly why the two scores stay separate.

4.8.3 Context Recall and Context Precision at the Retrieval Stage

Context recall and context precision judge the fetched chunks, not the final answer. The plain-words account is that the fetched set is set next to the question needs and each need is checked. In symbols:

\[ \text{Context Recall} = \frac{\text{retrieved relevant claims}}{\text{total relevant claims needed}} \]

\[ \text{Context Precision} = \frac{\text{relevant retrieved claims}}{\text{total retrieved claims}} \]

Here total relevant claims needed is what the question truly requires, retrieved relevant claims are those needs the fetched chunks meet, and total retrieved claims is what the fetch returned. A worked earbud case asked three things: phone fit, features, and battery life for a specific phone model. The fetched chunks met two of the three needs, so recall was two out of three. The chunks also held extra lines about other devices that did not fit the question, which drags precision down. Hard counts or soft model scores can be used. One soft path embeds each claim and the question, takes cosine similarity, and calls a chunk relevant when its score tops a cutoff such as 0.5. Either way the meaning holds: recall asks what share of needs were met, precision asks what share of fetched material was useful.

Worked retrieval pair (earbud case): the question needs three facts: phone fit, features, and battery life for one phone model. The fetch returns four chunks: two meet the needs (phone fit, battery life), one misses (features absent), and two extra chunks describe other devices. Step 1, recall: retrieved relevant \(= 2\), needed \(= 3\), so \(\text{Context Recall} = 2/3 \approx \textbf{0.67}\). Step 2, precision: relevant retrieved \(= 2\), total retrieved \(= 4\), so \(\text{Context Precision} = 2/4 = \textbf{0.5}\). Two thirds of the needs arrived, but only half the fetch was useful. Sense check: adding more off-point chunks leaves recall flat while precision falls, which mirrors the classic precision-recall trade.

4.8.4 Answer Correctness

Answer correctness looks at both sides. It asks whether the answer fits the question and whether it stays true to the fetched material. The plain-words account is that it joins the relevancy check with the grounding check. It is the broadest single score and suits final acceptance best. In practice teams read it last: a high correctness score means the answer was both on point and backed, while a low one sends the reader back to the four stage scores to find which half failed.

Scope: answer correctness still reads grounding against the fetched chunks, not against world truth. A correct-in-the-world answer built on the wrong chunks can score poorly here, which again points at the fetcher rather than the fact.

Pitfalls: (1) reporting faithfulness alone and missing off-point padding that only relevancy catches; (2) reporting recall alone and missing a fetch full of junk that only precision catches; (3) hand-scoring a handful of questions and treating the average as stable, when small samples swing wildly.

A claim outside them is unsupported, which counts as hallucination for this score. No outside ground truth is needed because the fetched chunks serve as the reference. 4.9 Production Readiness for RAG

A demo can run on rough files. A live system cannot. Data quality sets the ceiling, so files need cleaning before indexing: noise removal, duplicate removal, spelling fixes, and grammar fixes. Duplicate passages waste fetch slots when the same chunk returns twice. Stale files mislead the model, so freshness checks belong in the pipeline.

Hook: a demo forgives a duplicate paragraph; a live help desk does not, because the same chunk twice means one useful passage never made the shortlist.

Latency needs active control because every fetch, rank, and model call takes time. Repeat questions suit cached answers that skip the pipeline. Common questions suit precompute. Parallel arms shorten hybrid fetching. Smaller quantized vectors move faster. None of these remove latency fully, but together they keep answers timely. Think of the cache as the desk drawer: frequently-used tools kept on the desk beat a trip to the store every time.

Access control is a must. Not every user may see every store. Finance archives in particular need per-group stores so each user set reaches only its own vectors. Medical archives need PII scrubbing, the removal of names, addresses, mail IDs, photos, and other personal markers before vectors are built. Logs and monitors need the same care. Freshness, access rules, and scrubbing belong in design from day one, not as late patches.

The readiness checklist: clean and dedupe before indexing, stamp freshness and re-crawl on a schedule, gate every store by user group, scrub personal markers before embedding, and budget latency with caching, precompute, parallel arms, and quantized vectors.

Scope: these controls assume one team owns the pipeline end to end. They break when a third party hosts the vectors but never sees the access rules; then access must be enforced at fetch time by filtering chunks after scoring, not by trusting the store.

Pitfalls: (1) indexing first and cleaning later, which bakes duplicates and stale pages into every future answer; (2) logging raw prompts with personal markers, which leaks exactly what scrubbing removed; (3) one shared store for all teams, where a salary file becomes retrievable by anyone who phrases the question well.

Recap: ship clean, fresh, gated, and scrubbed data, then buy speed with caching and parallel arms. Bridge: even a correct answer fails when nobody can read it fast, which is the next section's open problem.

An enterprise help system must bar most staff from salary and review files while letting HR staff through. A hospital RAG must drop patient names and contact lines before any embedding step.

4.10 Readability of Generated Answers

A long open exchange asked how to score whether model answers read fast. The setting was concrete: busy merchants read bot notes that mix words with counts such as order volumes and regional splits, then act at once, for example by starting a promotion. The team wanted an eleven-second style read and asked for a science-backed measure of that speed.

Hook: a merchant has eleven seconds between customers. The bot note is correct, yet the sale is lost, because correct and quickly usable are different properties.

The reply worked through what such a measure would need. Any speed claim needs a reference point: who reads, in how long, with what background. The team fixed its reader as a college graduate with some science background, not a school student and not a non-technical reader. Even with the reader fixed, scoring needs ground truth: timed reads, rated ease, or action logs tied to reads. Without timed data or rated samples, no score can be checked. Readability also shifts with reader knowledge, so one number cannot fit all groups.

Rule-based school-grade formulas were raised and set aside. They score surface traits and often miss the mark on dense technical prose. Semantic closeness scores were raised next and set aside for a different reason. A paper can be well linked yet still read slow, because links do not imply speed. The coherence idea, proposed as a readability test, failed for the same reason. Model-as-judge scores drew the sharpest reply from the floor: a single model-given number with no readable cause, where a nine becomes an eight for no stated reason, is a black box that working teams cannot act on.

What a real readability score would need: a fixed reader profile, timed reads or ease ratings as ground truth, and action delay tied to reads. Candidate answers are scored against that truth, not against surface length or a bare judge-model number.

No settled measure came out of the exchange. The live threads to carry forward are timed reads against a fixed reader profile, rated ease on a Likert scale, and action delay after reading, each tied to ground truth samples.

Scope: these threads assume one reader group and one task shape (scan numbers, act at once). They break when the audience mixes experts and newcomers, because one number cannot fit all groups, and when the task is study rather than action, where slow reading is the point.

Pitfalls: (1) optimizing a school-grade formula and declaring dense prose "readable" while merchants still stall; (2) mistaking linked prose for fast prose, when coherence and speed diverge; (3) shipping a judge-model number with no cause attached, so no edit can move it on purpose.

Then gather ground truth: timed reads, ease ratings, or action delays after reading. Score candidate answers against that truth. Surface formulas and closeness scores alone do not capture read speed on dense prose, and a bare model-given number gives no cause to act on.

4.11 Question Answering Versus Conversational Systems

A question answering system takes one question and returns one grounded answer. Many single needs, such as fixed help-desk questions, need no multi-turn memory. A conversational system holds a multi-turn exchange: it recalls past turns, asks follow-up questions, and tracks changing inputs. The two overlap, since an answer step sits inside both, but their aims differ. Question answering targets a single need. Conversation targets a task that unfolds over turns. Question answering gives one grounded answer to one question, while conversational AI holds turns, keeps memory of the exchange, and may ask the user for more inputs.

Hook: "store hours?" needs one fact; booking a flight needs ten turns of shifting dates, seats, and prices. One system answers, the other negotiates.

Cost and delay decide between them. A plain question answering path skips dialogue memory, planning loops, and tool chains. A conversational path pays for all three. So fixed, repeat questions belong to question answering: refund timing, refund steps, late-order help, store hours, travel rules, leave rules, medical safety lines, account-opening file lists, quarterly sales by region, or voice orders such as playing a song or setting an alarm. When answers are fixed and controlled, question answering is enough. A small model at the door can even sort inputs: greetings and cached repeats get instant replies with no RAG call at all, while fresh questions flow onward.

Flight booking shows the other side. Stops, dates, seats, and prices shift turn by turn with availability, so each answer depends on the last. A single-turn answer cannot hold that state. A single-turn answer cannot hold shifting availability across turns. Conversational handling with memory and tools fits there.

The decision rule: fixed, repeat, single-need questions belong to question answering with caching and no dialogue state. Shifting, multi-turn tasks with dependencies between turns belong to conversational handling with memory and tools.

Scope: the split assumes the task shape is known up front. It breaks when a "simple" question turns out to need clarification mid-stream; then the door model must escalate to a conversational path instead of forcing a single-turn answer.

Pitfalls: (1) wiring memory, planning, and tools around fixed FAQs, where a cached single answer would do; (2) forcing a single-turn answer onto a booking flow, where each answer depends on the last; (3) keeping full dialogue memory forever, when most turns expire after the task closes.

4.11.1 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Is conversational AI the same as question answering? A: They overlap but differ in shape. Question answering gives one grounded answer to one question. Conversational AI holds turns, keeps memory of the exchange, and may ask the user for more inputs. Many single needs, such as fixed help-desk questions, need no multi-turn memory, so a conversational stack there is overkill.

Recap: answer once for fixed needs, converse with memory for unfolding tasks. Bridge: the final section narrows question answering to its most common industrial form: short fixed factoid answers.

4.12 Factoid and Retrieval-Based Question Answering

Factoid question answering handles fact questions with short fixed answers: names, dates, codes, and counts. Most industry help loads fall in this band. The plain path is information retrieval: match the question string against stored passages and return the best hit.

Hook: most enterprise questions are not essays waiting to happen. They are "what is the refund window," "which file opens an account," "what were Q3 sales by region" — one fact, one passage, one answer.

One team described exactly this build. Staff files sit in a back-end store. Each user question is matched by keywords against the files, and the best match is returned. That is a sparse string design in the BM25 and TF-IDF family. It is cheap, fast, and strong on exact facts. Its limit matches section 4.2: wording shifts can slip past it, which is where dense vectors or a hybrid pair help.

Team build trace: a staffer asks "what is the refund window for late orders." Step 1, keyword-match the question against staff files with BM25: the refund-policy file shares "refund," "late," and "orders," so it outscores the travel-rules file. Step 2, return its best passage: "late orders qualify for a full refund within 30 days." The answer is one grounded sentence with no generation beyond it. Sense check: on fixed wording the sparse match is both cheapest and most exact; paraphrase is the only input that would push this toward dense or hybrid search.

Scope: pure keyword matching assumes the question shares words with the stored file. It breaks on paraphrase ("return" asked, "refund" stored) and on multi-hop questions whose answer spans files. Both cases point back to the dense arm or the graph from earlier sections.

Pitfalls: (1) generating a long free-form answer where one cited sentence would do; (2) skipping the cache on questions asked hundreds of times a day; (3) grading factoid answers with style scores instead of exact or near-exact match against the stored fact.

Recap: factoid loads want string matching first, dense or hybrid help only where wording shifts.
