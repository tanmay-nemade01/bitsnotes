1.1 Course Overview

This course (AIMLCZG557) is a five-credit core course on the foundations of Artificial Intelligence. It covers the breadth and principles of AI: what an AI agent is, how knowledge systems represent and infer information, how probability enters decision-making, and how models optimize for both computation and results. The course comprises nine modules spread over sixteen lectures, supplemented by research papers, webinars, and self-help lab experiments.

Primary Textbook

Russell & Norvig, Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach, 4th Edition — the leading academic AI textbook for over 20 years. The 4th edition contains roughly 20–25% new material compared to the 3rd edition. Approximately 50–60% of the textbook is covered in this course. Recommendation: obtain a physical copy of the 4th edition (Indian/Eastern Economy Edition is affordable). Physical books are permitted in the open-book final exam; digital devices are not.

Reference texts: Mitchell (Machine Learning), Patterson (Introduction to AI & Expert Systems), Rich & Knight (Artificial Intelligence).

1.1.1 What This Course Is Not About

  • Hardware aspects of AI design.
  • A formal introduction to machine learning, neural networks, or deep learning (those are separate courses).
  • Deep dives into specific subfields such as computer vision or large language models (LLMs).
  • "Agentic AI" at the surface/tooling level (MCP protocol, agent-to-agent protocols, tool calling) — those are covered in the Software Engineering for ML course. This course focuses on the intricacies of how AI agents work internally.

1.1.2 Prerequisites

Official: Data Structures and Algorithms. Unofficial: willingness to learn, basic probability and statistics, a little Boolean logic, and some coding ability. These skills can be picked up along the way.

1.2 A Brief History of Artificial Intelligence

1.2.1 The Early Vision (1950-1956)

In 1950, Alan Turing posed the seminal question: "Can machines think?" He proposed that a computer would deserve to be called intelligent if it could deceive a human into believing it was human — the foundation of the Turing Test. Turing's 1950 paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," was remarkably prescient: it introduced not just the Turing test, but also the concepts of machine learning, genetic algorithms, and reinforcement learning. He argued it would be easier to create human-level AI by developing learning algorithms and then teaching the machine, rather than hand-programming its intelligence.

At this point in history (the 1950s), computers themselves were scarce, confined to large laboratories and government institutions. Yet Turing and others already envisioned machines capable of thought. Even earlier, in 1943, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts had produced the first work now recognized as AI: a model of artificial neurons grounded in three sources — the physiology of brain neurons, Russell and Whitehead's propositional logic, and Turing's theory of computation. They proved that any computable function could be computed by a network of connected neurons, and that all logical connectives (AND, OR, NOT) could be implemented by simple net structures. In 1949, Donald Hebb introduced a simple updating rule for modifying connection strengths — now called Hebbian learning — which remains an influential model to this day. In 1950, Marvin Minsky and Dean Edmonds built the SNARC, the first neural network computer, using 3,000 vacuum tubes and a surplus B-24 bomber autopilot mechanism to simulate 40 neurons.

Two checkers-playing programs were independently developed in 1952 by Christopher Strachey at Manchester and Arthur Samuel at IBM. Samuel's program was particularly influential: it used methods we now call reinforcement learning and learned to play at a strong amateur level. When demonstrated on television in 1956, it disproved the belief that computers can do only what they're told — his program quickly learned to play better than its creator.

The term Artificial Intelligence was formally coined in 1956 at the Dartmouth Summer Workshop. Four scientists — John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Allen Newell, and Herbert Simon — wrote a proposal to gather researchers for a two-month summer school at Dartmouth College to discuss:

  • Automatic computers and programming languages.
  • Neural networks and neuron models.
  • Theory of calculation and self-improvement.
  • Abstractions and sensory data.

The original 1955 proposal stated:

"We propose that a 2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence be carried out during the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth College... The study is to proceed on the basis of the conjecture that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it."

Ten attendees were present, including Arthur Samuel from IBM and Ray Solomonoff and Oliver Selfridge from MIT. Although the workshop produced no immediate breakthroughs, Newell and Simon demonstrated the Logic Theorist (LT), a mathematical theorem-proving system — perhaps the most mature work presented. Simon declared they had "solved the venerable mind–body problem." Soon after, LT proved most of the theorems in Chapter 2 of Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica; Russell himself was reportedly delighted when LT produced a proof shorter than the one in Principia. The workshop is considered the official birth of AI as a field.

1.2.2 Early Success and the First AI Winter (1956-1980)

Following Dartmouth, the first Perceptron was built, the first AI programs appeared, and ELIZA — an early chatbot by Joseph Weizenbaum — could engage in conversational exchanges. People were amazed: a machine that seemed to think and respond.

The late 1950s and 1960s were a period of rapid progress, what John McCarthy called the "Look, Ma, no hands!" era. Key milestones included:

  • 1958 — Lisp: John McCarthy invented the Lisp programming language (MIT AI Lab Memo No. 1), which would dominate AI for the next 30 years. McCarthy also published "Programs with Common Sense," describing the Advice Taker — a hypothetical program embodying general world knowledge to derive action plans from simple logical axioms.
  • Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver (GPS): Unlike LT, GPS was designed from the start to imitate human problem-solving protocols. The order in which GPS considered subgoals and possible actions matched how humans approached the same puzzles — making it probably the first program to embody "thinking humanly."
  • 1965 — Resolution Method: J.A. Robinson discovered a complete theorem-proving algorithm for first-order logic, advancing McCarthy's logic-based vision. Work at Stanford emphasized general-purpose logical reasoning, leading to Cordell Green's question-answering systems and the Shakey robotics project at SRI — the first to integrate logical reasoning with physical activity.
  • Microworlds at MIT: Minsky's students tackled limited domains requiring intelligence: James Slagle's SAINT solved calculus integration problems; Tom Evans's ANALOGY solved geometric analogy IQ-test problems; and Terry Winograd's SHRDLU (1972) could understand natural-language commands to manipulate blocks in the blocks world — a simulated tabletop with solid blocks and a robot hand.
  • Neural networks: Frank Rosenblatt's perceptrons (1962) showed that learning algorithms could adjust connection strengths to match any input data, provided such a match exists. Widrow and Hoff (1960) enhanced Hebbian learning with their adalines.

ELIZA's conversational success and the achievements in microworlds led to a wave of AI-labeled proposals, many funded by defense and government agencies. Herbert Simon's 1957 prediction captured the spirit: "There are now in the world machines that think, that learn and that create." Simon also predicted that within 10 years a computer would be chess champion and prove a significant mathematical theorem — predictions that took about 40 years to come true.

However, three fundamental problems emerged. First, many early AI systems relied on "informed introspection" about how humans perform a task rather than careful task analysis and algorithmic design. Second, researchers failed to appreciate the combinatorial explosion — the intractability of problems AI was attempting. Early systems succeeded on tiny microworlds with few objects, but failed to scale. Before computational complexity theory developed, it was widely thought that scaling up was just a matter of faster hardware. The failure to solve problems with more than a few dozen facts was one of the main criticisms in the Lighthill Report (1973). Third, Minsky and Papert's book Perceptrons (1969) proved that single-layer perceptrons could represent very little — a two-input perceptron could not even recognize when its two inputs were different. Although their results didn't apply to multilayer networks, research funding for neural networks dwindled to almost nothing.

From roughly 1973 to 1980, AI entered its First AI Winter: the British government ended AI support at all but two universities based on the Lighthill Report; funding dried up; confidence collapsed; and "AI" became a bad word in academic circles.

1.2.3 Recovery and the Second AI Winter (1980-1993)

The 1980s saw a shift from "weak methods" (general-purpose search that didn't scale) to expert systems — programs using powerful, domain-specific knowledge to take larger reasoning steps in narrow areas of expertise. As Ed Feigenbaum put it: to solve a hard problem, you have to almost know the answer already.

Key expert systems:

  • DENDRAL (1969, Stanford): The first successful knowledge-intensive system. It inferred molecular structure from mass spectrometer data by encoding "cookbook recipes" from experienced analytical chemists. Rather than generating all possible structures (intractable for medium molecules), it used pattern-recognition rules — e.g., recognizing a ketone (C=O) subgroup from specific peak patterns.
  • MYCIN (mid-1970s): Diagnosed blood infections using ~450 rules from expert interviews. It performed as well as some experts and better than junior doctors. MYCIN introduced certainty factors — a calculus of uncertainty that matched how doctors weighed diagnostic evidence — since medical knowledge had no clean theoretical model like DENDRAL's chemistry.
  • R1 (1982, DEC): The first commercially successful expert system. It configured orders for new computer systems, saving DEC an estimated $40 million per year by 1986. By 1988, DEC had 40 expert systems deployed; DuPont had 100 in use and 500 in development.

Conferences like AAAI grew. In 1981, Japan announced the "Fifth Generation" project — a 10-year plan to build massively parallel intelligent computers running Prolog, with a budget exceeding $1.3 billion (in today's money). The US responded with the MCC consortium and Britain with the Alvey report. However, none of these projects met their ambitious goals.

However, a curious phenomenon emerged: researchers started disassociating from the name "AI", rebranding their work as "machine learning," "deep learning," or "expert systems" to avoid the stigma of over-promising.

From 1987 to 1993, the Second AI Winter struck. Expert systems proved difficult to build and maintain for complex domains — their reasoning methods broke down under uncertainty, and they couldn't learn from experience. Companies fell as they failed to deliver on extravagant promises. PhD students in symbolic AI faced skepticism: "You will propose things, but they won't work — that's where your field is heading."

1.2.4 The Resurgence (1986-2012)

In the mid-1980s, at least four different groups independently reinvented the back-propagation learning algorithm (first developed in the early 1960s by Kelley and Bryson). The collection Parallel Distributed Processing (Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986) caused great excitement. These connectionist models were seen by some as direct competitors to the symbolic models promoted by Newell and Simon and the logicist approach of McCarthy. Geoff Hinton, a leading figure in the neural network resurgence, described symbols as the "luminiferous aether of AI" — a reference to the non-existent medium that 19th-century physicists believed carried electromagnetic waves. Connectionist models formed internal concepts in a more fluid, imprecise way better suited to real-world messiness, and crucially, they could learn from examples.

1988 was a pivotal year for bringing AI together with other fields. Judea Pearl's Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems led to a new acceptance of probability and decision theory in AI. Pearl developed Bayesian networks — a rigorous and efficient formalism for representing uncertain knowledge with practical reasoning algorithms. In the same year, Rich Sutton connected reinforcement learning to the theory of Markov decision processes (MDPs) from operations research. AI was moving from brittle Boolean logic and hand-coding toward probability, machine learning, and experimental validation.

The field of speech recognition illustrates the pattern. In the 1970s, many ad hoc approaches were tried. By the 1980s, hidden Markov models (HMMs) came to dominate — based on rigorous mathematical theory and trained on large corpora of real speech data. Note that there was no claim that humans use HMMs; rather, HMMs provided a mathematical framework for solving the problem.

In 1997, IBM Deep Blue defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov. Kasparov later remarked he "could feel human-level intelligence in the room." A key insight from this match: Deep Blue made a move that spectators dismissed as stupid — "any human could figure out it was bad" — yet that move ultimately led to victory. In retrospect, the machine's reasoning was non-human but effective.

[Added for clarity: This same pattern recurred with AlphaGo, where a "non-human" move (Move 37 against Lee Sedol) astonished commentators but proved decisive.]

From 2000 onward, progress became steady: DARPA autonomous vehicle challenges, renewed interest in artificial neural networks (ANNs), Siri, deep learning architectures, AlphaGo, TensorFlow, and DeepMind.

1.2.5 The Transformer Era and Mainstream AI (2017-Present)

In 2017, the paper "Attention Is All You Need" introduced the Transformer architecture, which underpins virtually all modern large language models (GPT, BERT, etc.).

Deep learning — machine learning using multiple layers of simple, adjustable computing elements — had been building momentum since 2011. Experiments with such networks date to the 1970s (convolutional neural networks succeeded at handwritten digit recognition in the 1990s), but the watershed moment came in the 2012 ImageNet competition. Geoffrey Hinton's group at the University of Toronto demonstrated a dramatic improvement over previous systems that relied on handcrafted features, classifying images into one of a thousand categories. Since then, deep learning systems have exceeded human performance on some vision tasks, speech recognition, machine translation, medical diagnosis, and game playing. Training time for image recognition dropped by a factor of 100 in just two years.

Deep learning relies on specialized hardware: while a standard CPU does 109–1010 operations per second, deep learning on GPUs, TPUs, or FPGAs can consume 1014–1017 operations per second. The amount of computing power used in top AI applications has been doubling every 3.4 months.

Big data was another crucial enabler. The World Wide Web produced trillions of words of text, billions of images, and billions of hours of speech and video. Banko and Brill (2001) showed that increasing dataset size by two or three orders of magnitude outweighed any algorithmic improvements. Hays and Efros (2007) found that hole-filling in photographs worked poorly with thousands of images but crossed a quality threshold with millions. Big data was crucial to IBM Watson's 2011 victory in Jeopardy! over human champions.

AlphaGo (2016) shocked the world by defeating human world Go champion Lee Sedol. Go had been considered much harder than chess — Piet Hut, an astrophysicist and Go enthusiast, had predicted "a hundred years before a computer beats humans at Go." AlphaGo studied hundreds of thousands of past human games. Its successor, AlphaZero (2018), used no human input except the rules and surpassed all opponents — human and machine — at Go, chess, and shogi through self-play alone. World champion Ke Jie said of AlphaGo: "Last year, it was still quite human-like when it played. But this year, it became like a god of Go."

The State of the Art by 2019 encompassed: robotic vehicles (Waymo's 10 million miles, commercial taxi service), legged locomotion (Atlas doing backflips), autonomous space scheduling (Remote Agent, Mars rovers), machine translation in 100+ languages, speech recognition matching human performance on telephone transcription, and visual object detection error rates dropping from 28% (2010) to 2% (2017), exceeding human performance. AI algorithms also equal or exceed expert doctors at diagnosing Alzheimer's, metastatic cancer, and ophthalmic and skin diseases from images.

ChatGPT (2022) was a watershed moment: it democratized AI. Before ChatGPT, AI was largely confined to academia, research labs, and a few government institutes. After ChatGPT, AI entered every household; the general public began naming "ChatGPT" as synonymous with AI tools.

The last five years have seen relentless releases, debates, new frameworks, and both promising and contradictory results. AI is now in a prime phase — but history suggests winters can recur.

Why the Last Decade Took Off: The Triple Convergence

Three forces came together simultaneously:

  1. Data — Social media, IoT, and digitization produced unprecedented volumes of data.
  2. Computation — Competing organizations drove GPU and high-performance chip development; computational power became accessible at the touch of a button, even in phones.
  3. Algorithms — Steady advances in discrete math, software engineering, and optimization improved algorithmic efficiency.

Exam Tip / Common Pitfall

A common exam question asks: "Why did AI take off only in the last decade despite being conceptualized in the 1950s?" The answer is always the triple convergence: data, computation, and algorithms coming together.

1.3 What Is Intelligence?

1.3.1 Dictionary Definition

Definition: Intelligence (Dictionary)

The capacity of learning, reasoning, understanding, and similar forms of mental activity. Equivalently: the ability to perceive and act in the world.

Breaking this down:

  • Reasoning — proving theorems, medical diagnosis.
  • Planning — decision-making, strategizing.
  • Learning and Adaptation — recommending movies, learning traffic patterns.
  • Understanding — not just text, but speech, visual communication, non-verbal cues.

1.3.2 Thinking Without Action Is Useless

A crucial insight: learning, reasoning, and understanding alone are insufficient. A philosopher who merely thinks but never produces a paper, book, or action — how would we even know they were thinking? They might have been sleeping.

Action is essential: intelligence must manifest through observable behavior. This idea underpins the rational-agent approach (Section 4.4).

1.3.3 Are Humans Intelligent?

Three related questions explored in the lecture:

  1. Are humans intelligent? — Debatable. "Maybe, it depends."
  2. Are humans always intelligent? — No. We make mistakes, take bad decisions, and regret them later. Intelligence is not a constant property of human behavior.
  3. Can non-human behavior be intelligent? — Yes. A dog that barks at an unfamiliar intruder perceives (detects a threat) and acts (barks to alert) — exhibiting intelligence by the "perceive and act" definition.

Q&A. Student Question: If humans are not always intelligent, why should AI try to mimic human behavior?

Answer: This is exactly the argument against the "thinking/acting humanly" schools of AI. If humans are imperfect, biased, and sometimes irrational, replicating them may not be the right goal. The rational-agent approach avoids this pitfall.

1.4 The Four Definitions of AI

Over the last 50+ years, researchers have aligned with different definitions of AI. All are valid schools of thought; none is "wrong." However, one quadrant has emerged as more promising and widely adopted in recent years.

These definitions can be organized into a 2 × 2 matrix:

Human-like Rational
Thought / Thinking Systems that think like humans Systems that think rationally
Behavior / Acting Systems that act like humans Systems that act rationally

The Four Quadrants at a Glance

  1. Thinking Humanly — Cognitive modeling; replicate human thought processes.
  2. Acting Humanly — Turing Test approach; perform tasks requiring human intelligence.
  3. Thinking Rationally — Laws of thought; formal logic and correct inference.
  4. Acting Rationally — Rational agents; perceive, act, and achieve the best outcome.

[Added for clarity: The rows distinguish thought (internal process) from behavior (external action). The columns distinguish human-like (mimicking humans) from rational (doing what is correct/optimal).]

1.4.1 Quadrant 1: Thinking Humanly - The Cognitive Modeling Approach

"The exciting new effort to make computers think — machines with minds." — Haugeland (1985)

"The automation of activities that we associate with human thinking, activities such as decision making, problem solving, learning." — Bellman (1978)

For a machine to think like a human, we must first understand how humans think. This requires cognitive modeling — studying the human mind through:

  • Introspection — catching our own thoughts as they go by.
  • Psychological experiments — observing a person in action.
  • Brain imaging — observing the brain in action (fMRI, EEG).

Once we have a sufficiently precise theory of the mind, it can be expressed as a computer program. If the program's input–output behavior matches human behavior, that's evidence that some of its mechanisms could also be operating in humans. Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver (GPS) was probably the first program to embody this approach — they weren't content merely to have GPS solve problems correctly; they compared the sequence and timing of its reasoning steps to those of human subjects solving the same puzzles.

The interdisciplinary field of cognitive science brings together computer models from AI and experimental techniques from psychology to construct precise, testable theories of the human mind. In the early days of AI, there was confusion between the approaches — authors would argue that an algorithm performs well and that it's therefore a good model of human performance. Modern authors separate these two kinds of claims, allowing both AI and cognitive science to develop more rapidly. Recently, the combination of neuroimaging methods with machine learning has led to the beginnings of a capability to "read minds" — ascertaining the semantic content of a person's inner thoughts.

Counter-arguments against this school:

  1. We do not fully understand human thinking. Cognitive science has studied this for centuries, yet we still cannot fully explain how humans think. If we could, there would be no interpersonal conflicts or misunderstandings.
  2. Post-facto rationalization. Humans often act instinctively and then invent reasons afterward. For example, someone picks coffee; when asked why, they fabricate: "Tea keeps me awake." They did not deliberate — they rationalized after the fact.
  3. Humans are imperfect. Humans lie, manipulate, and exhibit bias. Training a machine on biased human data produces a biased machine.
  4. The pigeon analogy. Aeronautical engineering textbooks do not define their goal as "making machines that fly so exactly like pigeons that they can fool even other pigeons." The quest for "artificial flight" succeeded when engineers stopped imitating birds and started using wind tunnels and learning about aerodynamics. Similarly, AI does not need to think exactly like humans — it just needs to solve problems effectively.
  5. Deep Blue did not think like a human — yet it beat the world chess champion. Its "bad move" was incomprehensible to human observers but ultimately winning.

Where thinking humanly still matters:

Applications requiring human-like cognition — intelligent tutoring systems, emotion-expressing robots, and human-computer interaction (HCI) interfaces — benefit from modeling human thought. Newell and Simon (1976) formulated the physical symbol system hypothesis: "a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action" — the claim that any system exhibiting intelligence must operate by manipulating data structures composed of symbols. This hypothesis has been challenged from many directions, particularly by connectionist (neural network) models.

Q&A. Student Question: Do we want a machine that beats humans in chess, or a machine that thinks like humans while beating humans in chess? The result is the same — why does the distinction matter?

Answer: The distinction determines the direction of research. If we require human-like thinking, the field invests in cognitive science, brain imaging, and psychological modeling. If we only care about winning, the field invests in algorithms, optimization, and search. Deep Blue won without human-like thinking — validating the second approach.

1.4.2 Quadrant 2: Acting Humanly - The Turing Test Approach

"The art of creating machines that perform functions that require intelligence when performed by people." — Kurzweil (1990)

"The study of how to make computers do things at which, at the moment, people are better." — Rich & Knight (1991)

This school asks: Can the machine behave indistinguishably from a human?

The Turing Test:

Turing proposed the test as a thought experiment to sidestep the philosophical vagueness of "Can a machine think?"

  1. A human interrogator asks questions through a text interface.
  2. Behind a "wall," the responses come from either a human or a computer.
  3. If the interrogator cannot reliably distinguish between the two, the computer is said to exhibit intelligence — it is acting humanly.

Turing viewed physical simulation of a person as unnecessary. However, others proposed a total Turing test, which requires interaction with objects and people in the real world — adding computer vision and robotics to the capabilities list.

Required capabilities for passing the (standard) Turing Test:

  • Natural language processing (NLP) — to communicate successfully in a human language.
  • Knowledge representation — to store what it knows or hears.
  • Automated reasoning — to answer questions and draw new conclusions.
  • Machine learning — to adapt to new circumstances and detect/extrapolate patterns.

These four disciplines, plus computer vision and robotics for the total Turing test, compose most of AI. Yet AI researchers have devoted relatively little effort to actually passing the Turing test, believing it's more important to study the underlying principles of intelligence rather than to mimic humans.

Modern relevance:

Some frontier models (e.g., GPT-4.5) have reportedly convinced interrogators in a significant percentage of trials. A commonly referenced benchmark: if 50% or more of judges cannot distinguish human from machine, the system is considered Turing-Test-capable.

Counter-arguments:

  • Reverse Turing Test. What about humans who behave like machines? Shakuntala Devi, the Indian mathematician, was called a "human computer." If a human exhibits machine-like precision, are we calling them "acting computerly"?
  • Reproducibility. Turing-test results may not be reproducible, constructive, or mathematically analyzable.

[Added for clarity: The Loebner Prize is an annual competition implementing the Turing Test. Judges are typically researchers and subject-matter experts, not random laypeople.]

1.4.3 Quadrant 3: Thinking Rationally - The Laws of Thought

"The study of mental faculties through the use of computational models." — Charniak & McDermott (1985)

"The study of the computations that make it possible to perceive, reason, and act." — Winston (1992)

This school traces back to Aristotle and the invention of formal logic: given a set of premises, derive conclusions through correct inference. Aristotle's syllogisms provided patterns for argument structures that always yield correct conclusions when given correct premises — the canonical example being "Socrates is a man; all men are mortal; therefore Socrates is mortal" (probably due to Sextus Empiricus rather than Aristotle). These "laws of thought" were supposed to govern the operation of the mind; their study initiated the field of logic.

Logicians in the 19th century (notably George Boole with propositional logic in 1847, and Gottlob Frege with first-order logic in 1879) developed precise notation for statements about objects and the relations among them. By 1965, programs could in principle solve any solvable problem described in logical notation. The so-called logicist tradition within AI hopes to build intelligent systems on such programs.

Example: Logical Syllogism

  • Premise 1: In all boring classes, students sleep.
  • Premise 2: This is a boring class.
  • Conclusion: Students sleep in this class.

This is codified rational thinking: when we have facts about the world, we reason about them using formal rules to reach rational conclusions.

Counter-arguments:

  1. Not all intelligent behavior is mediated by logic deliberation.
    • If you place your hand on a hot pan, you do not reason: "This pan is hot. If I keep my hand here, I will burn. Therefore, I should remove my hand." You reflexively withdraw.
    • If a dog chases you, you do not calculate optimal escape velocity, turning radius, and path planning. You run.
    Reflexes are intelligent behaviors that bypass logical deliberation.
  2. Logic requires certain knowledge — a condition seldom achieved in reality. We don't know the rules of politics or warfare the way we know the rules of chess. The theory of probability fills this gap, allowing rigorous reasoning with uncertain information, but probability alone doesn't generate intelligent behavior — for that, we need a theory of rational action.
  3. Computational intractability. Even with a few hundred logical facts, reasoning over all possible inferences is an NP-complete problem, requiring O(2n) computations. Pure logical systems do not scale.

Where thinking rationally still matters:

Small, well-defined domains (e.g., a specialized expert system for a narrow field) can be built using purely logical, thinking-rationally approaches.

1.4.4 Quadrant 4: Acting Rationally - The Rational Agent Approach

"Computational intelligence is the study of the design of intelligent agents." — Poole et al. (1998)

"AI is concerned with intelligent behavior in artifacts." — Nilsson (1998)

Definition: Agent

An agent is an entity that perceives its environment and acts upon it. (From Latin agere, to do.) Computer agents are expected to do more than regular programs — they operate autonomously, perceive their environment, persist over time, adapt to change, and create and pursue goals.

Definition: Rational Agent

A rational agent is one that acts so as to achieve the best outcome, or, when there is uncertainty, the best expected outcome, given the available information.

Core idea: "Rational behavior means doing the right thing." The "right thing" is that which is expected to maximize goal achievement given the available information. In a nutshell, AI has focused on the study and construction of agents that do the right thing. What counts as the right thing is defined by the objective we provide to the agent. This general paradigm is so pervasive it's called the standard model.

The rational-agent approach has two advantages over the other definitions. First, it's more general than the "laws of thought" approach — correct inference is just one of several mechanisms for achieving rationality (reflexes are another). Second, it's more amenable to scientific development — the standard of rationality is mathematically well defined and completely general, allowing us to derive agent designs that provably achieve it, which is largely impossible when the goal is to imitate humans.

Key properties of this approach:

  1. Computational limitations make perfect rationality unachievable in practice. This is the issue of limited rationality — acting appropriately when there isn't enough time to do all the computations one might like. The goal is to design the best program for given machine resources, though perfect rationality remains a good starting point for theoretical analysis.
  2. Rationality can include correct inference (thinking rationally is a subset of acting rationally).
  3. Rationality can also include acting without inference — e.g., using reflexes when deliberation is too slow. Recoiling from a hot stove is a reflex action usually more successful than a slower action taken after careful deliberation.
  4. All the skills needed for the Turing test (NLP, knowledge representation, reasoning, learning) also enable an agent to act rationally. Knowledge representation and reasoning help agents reach good decisions; learning improves the ability to generate effective behavior, especially in new circumstances.
  5. The standard model prevails not only in AI but also in control theory (minimizing a cost function), operations research (maximizing sum of rewards), statistics (minimizing a loss function), and economics (maximizing utility or social welfare).

Why this quadrant is emerging as the dominant definition:

  • It does not require mimicking imperfect humans.
  • It does not require exhaustive logical computation.
  • It emphasizes action — thinking without action is useless.
  • It naturally accommodates uncertainty (best expected outcome).
  • The entire paradigm of agentic AI (agents that perceive, reason, and act) is built on this definition.

Exam Tip / Common Pitfall

This course is majorly about designing rational agents. The definition of a rational agent — "an entity that perceives and acts so as to achieve the best (expected) outcome" — is a high-probability exam item. Distinguish it clearly from the other three quadrants.

1.5 AI as an Interdisciplinary Umbrella

AI is not solely a computer science creation. It is the product of convergence across many fields, each contributing key ideas, viewpoints, and techniques:

Philosophy (400 BCE–present)
Aristotle (384–322 BCE) was the first to formulate precise laws governing the rational mind, developing syllogisms for proper reasoning — in principle allowing conclusions to be generated mechanically from premises. His analysis of action in De Motu Animalium described a logical connection between goals and knowledge: "I need covering; a cloak is a covering. I need a cloak... The conclusion is an action." This algorithm was implemented 2,300 years later as Newell and Simon's GPS.

Descartes gave the first clear discussion of mind–matter distinction, proposing dualism (a part of the mind outside physical laws). The alternative, materialism (also called physicalism), holds that the brain's operation according to physical laws constitutes the mind. The empiricism movement (Bacon, Locke) held that "Nothing is in the understanding, which was not first in the senses." David Hume proposed the principle of induction: general rules are acquired by exposure to repeated associations. The logical positivism of the Vienna Circle held that all knowledge can be characterized by logical theories connected to observation sentences. Utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill) promoted rational decision making based on maximizing utility for all, while deontological ethics (Kant) emphasized universal social laws governing allowable actions.
Mathematics
George Boole (1847) worked out propositional (Boolean) logic. Gottlob Frege (1879) extended it to first-order logic including objects and relations. The theory of probability generalizes logic to uncertain information: Thomas Bayes proposed a rule for updating probabilities in light of new evidence — Bayes' rule is a crucial AI tool. Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorem (1931) showed that in any formal theory as strong as Peano arithmetic, there are true statements with no proof. The Church–Turing thesis identified computability with Turing machines, but tractability had even greater impact — NP-completeness theory (Cook, 1971; Karp, 1972) provides the basis for analyzing which problems are computationally feasible.
Economics
The science of economics originated with Adam Smith (1776), who analyzed economies as many individual agents attending to their own interests. Decision theory combines probability and utility theory to provide a formal framework for decisions under uncertainty. Game theory (von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944) handles "small" economies where each player's actions significantly affect others — including the result that rational agents should sometimes adopt randomized policies. Operations research addresses sequential decision problems; Richard Bellman (1957) formalized Markov decision processes (MDPs). Herbert Simon won the 1978 Nobel Prize in Economics for showing that satisficing — making "good enough" decisions rather than laboriously calculating the optimal — better describes actual human behavior than perfect rationality.
Neuroscience
The study of the nervous system established that cognitive functions result from electrochemical operation of neurons — in John Searle's words, "brains cause minds." Paul Broca (1861) identified a localized brain area responsible for speech production. Camillo Golgi (1873) developed neuron-staining techniques; Santiago Ramón y Cajal pioneered neuronal organization studies. Modern tools include EEG (1929), fMRI (1990), single-cell recording, and optogenetics — allowing both measurement and control of light-sensitive neurons. Brain–machine interfaces not only restore function to disabled individuals but reveal that the brain can treat external devices as additional sensory organs.

A crude comparison shows computers have million-times-faster cycle times than brains, but brains compensate with massive parallelism (1011 neurons, 1014 synapses). Supercomputers now match some brain metrics. Futurists point to an approaching singularity at which computers reach superhuman performance, but even with unlimited capacity, we still need conceptual breakthroughs — "without the right theory, faster machines just give you the wrong answer faster."
Psychology
How humans and animals think and act. Behaviorism (Watson) rejected mental processes, studying only objective stimulus-response, and discovered much about rats and pigeons but less about humans. Cognitive psychology views the brain as an information-processing device — Kenneth Craik (1943) specified the three key steps of a knowledge-based agent: (1) stimulus translated into internal representation, (2) cognitive processes manipulate the representation to derive new ones, (3) these are retranslated into action. Craik's "small-scale model" concept — an organism carrying an internal model of reality in its head — remains central to agent design.

The field of cognitive science was effectively born at a September 1956 MIT workshop (just two months after the Dartmouth AI workshop), where George Miller, Noam Chomsky, and Newell & Simon presented papers showing how computer models could address the psychology of memory, language, and logical thinking.

Intelligence augmentation (IA) — championed by Doug Engelbart in his 1968 "mother of all demos" — holds that computers should augment human abilities rather than automate them away. Today IA and AI are two sides of the same coin: the former emphasizes human control, the latter emphasizes intelligent machine behavior.
Computer Engineering
AI hardware has evolved from the first electromechanical Heath Robinson (1943, Turing's team for code-breaking) and the Colossus vacuum-tube computer, through Moore's law doubling every 18 months, to specialized AI processors — GPUs, TPUs, and wafer-scale engines (WSE). From 2012 to 2018, compute power for training top AI models increased 300,000-fold (doubling every ~100 days). Ada Lovelace (1843) anticipated AI's power and its hype cycles, writing of Babbage's Analytical Engine: "It is desirable to guard against the possibility of exaggerated ideas that might arise as to the powers of the Analytical Engine." AI has repaid its software debt: time sharing, interactive interpreters, windowed GUIs with mice, linked-list data types, automatic storage management, and key concepts of symbolic, functional, declarative, and object-oriented programming all originated in AI research.
Control Theory and Cybernetics
Self-regulating systems date back to Ktesibios of Alexandria (c. 250 BCE), who built a water clock with a regulator. Norbert Wiener's book Cybernetics (1948) awoke the public to artificially intelligent machines. Cybernetics viewed purposive behavior as a regulatory mechanism minimizing "error" — the difference between current state and goal state. Modern control theory, especially stochastic optimal control, designs systems that minimize a cost function over time, roughly matching AI's standard model. The fields were historically separate because control theory's tools (calculus, matrix algebra) suited continuous-variable systems, while AI's tools (logic, computation) addressed language, vision, and symbolic planning.
Linguistics
Noam Chomsky's review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior (1957) effectively ended behaviorism's dominance in language study, pointing out that behaviorist theory couldn't explain creativity in language — how children understand and create sentences they've never heard. Chomsky's theory, based on syntactic models going back to the Indian linguist Panini (c. 350 BCE), was formal enough to be programmed. Modern computational linguistics / NLP grew up together with AI. Understanding language requires understanding subject matter and context — not just sentence structure — a fact not widely appreciated until the 1960s. Much early work in knowledge representation was tied to language and informed by linguistics research.

Q&A. Student Question: With respect to India, are we making AI consumers or AI engineers?

Answer: India is currently heavy on the consumption side, but making good progress on engineering. Example: AI for Bharat (led by a leading researcher, a leading research institute, also nominated as president of AAAI) focuses on Indic language models. Frontier models (GPT etc.) underperform in non-English languages. India's linguistic diversity creates a unique market for Indic NLP. Good work is being done on Hindi, Tamil, and other regional language models.

1.6 Risks of Artificial Intelligence

Francis Bacon noted in 1609 that the "mechanical arts are of ambiguous use, serving as well for hurt as for remedy." To begin with the benefits: our entire civilization is the product of human intelligence, and substantially greater machine intelligence raises the ceiling on our ambitions. AI and robotics could free humanity from menial repetitive work and dramatically increase the production of goods and services. Accelerating scientific research could yield cures for disease and solutions for climate change and resource shortages. As Demis Hassabis (CEO of Google DeepMind) put it: "First solve AI, then use AI to solve everything else."

But long before "solving AI," we incur risks from misuse, inadvertent or otherwise:

1.6.1 Lethal Autonomous Weapons

The United Nations defines these as weapons that can locate, select, and eliminate human targets without human intervention. The primary concern is scalability: a small group can deploy an arbitrarily large number of weapons without a requirement for human supervision per target — defined by any feasible recognition criterion. The technologies needed are similar to those for self-driving cars. UN informal expert discussions began in 2014, moving to the formal pre-treaty stage of a Group of Governmental Experts in 2017.

1.6.2 Surveillance and Persuasion

While it's expensive and legally questionable for human personnel to monitor phone lines, video feeds, emails, and messaging channels, AI (speech recognition, computer vision, and natural language understanding) can perform mass surveillance at unprecedented scales. By tailoring information flows to individuals through social media using machine learning, political behavior can be modified and controlled — a concern that became apparent in elections beginning in 2016.

1.6.3 Biased Decision-Making

Careless or deliberate misuse of machine learning algorithms for tasks such as evaluating parole and loan applications can encode biases by race, gender, and other protected categories. Often, the training data themselves reflect pervasive societal bias. Connection to the four quadrants: If the goal is merely to mimic human behavior (acting/thinking humanly), all human biases are inherited. This is a key argument for moving toward acting rationally — doing the right thing, not just the human thing.

1.6.4 Impact on Employment and Inequality

AI automates tasks that humans might otherwise perform. However, it also:

  • Makes humans more productive, potentially leading to higher wages.
  • Makes companies more profitable, enabling expansion.
  • May render some activities economically viable that would otherwise be impractical.

The critical concern is inequality:

"AI use generally results in increasing wealth, but tends to have the effect of shifting wealth from labor to capital, further exacerbating increases in inequality."

Previous advances in technology — such as mechanical looms — caused serious employment disruptions before people found new kinds of work. The difference with AI: it may be doing those new kinds of work too. If unchecked, AI will widen the gap: one group (AI-enabled) becomes ultra-productive and wealthy, while another group (without access or skills) falls further behind. This is a capital-centric rather than labor-centric dynamic.

Vibe Coding vs. AI-Assisted Engineering:

  • Vibe coding — Anyone prompts repeatedly until they get a desired output, then declares the solution "done." No understanding of computing principles, software architecture, scalability, or security. This is dangerous.
  • AI-assisted engineering — A trained engineer uses AI as a companion: code review, boilerplate generation, test writing. The engineer understands what is being produced and ensures it follows software engineering practices. This is powerful.

Vibe coding without engineering knowledge can lead to vulnerable, non-scalable systems pushed into production.

Q&A. Student Question: Today there is much hype around vibe coding. How will it impact the field — positively or negatively?

Answer (instructor): Vibe coding is bad. AI-assisted engineering is good. Vibe coding means hitting in the dark with prompts; AI-assisted engineering means using AI as an informed tool. A vibe-coded platform will not scale to 100 million users and will likely contain vulnerabilities.

1.6.5 Safety-Critical Applications

AI used in safety-critical domains (healthcare, autonomous vehicles, aviation, managing city water supplies) can directly impact lives if ill-informed or poorly tested. Fatal accidents have already occurred and highlight the difficulty of formal verification and statistical risk analysis for systems built with machine learning. The field needs technical and ethical standards comparable to those in other engineering and healthcare disciplines where lives are at stake.

1.6.6 Cybersecurity

AI techniques are useful in defending against cyberattack (detecting unusual behavior patterns) but also contribute to the potency, survivability, and proliferation of malware. Reinforcement learning methods have been used to create automated, personalized blackmail and phishing attacks. Vibe-coded systems, built without software engineering principles, are especially vulnerable.

1.6.7 Sustainability: Cost vs. Price

  • Cost — The monetary expense of running AI systems (tokens, subscriptions, infrastructure).
  • Price — What future generations will pay: resource depletion, energy consumption, carbon footprint.

If AI continues on its current trajectory without sustainability considerations, the environmental toll may trigger future "winters" or require systemic intervention. Sustainable AI requires collaboration across system engineers, hardware engineers, and the software engineering community.

[Added for clarity: See the growing literature on "Sustainable AI" at venues such as the International Conference on Software Engineering (ICSE) and AAAI.]

1.6.8 Longer-Term Risks: The Value Alignment Problem

The standard model of AI — providing a machine with a fixed objective and letting it optimize — may not be adequate as systems become more capable. The value alignment problem is the challenge of ensuring that the objectives we put into machines are aligned with human values and preferences.

Consider chess: if a machine is intelligent enough to reason beyond the chessboard, it might attempt to increase its winning chances by hypnotizing or blackmailing its opponent, bribing the audience, or hijacking extra computing power. These behaviors aren't "unintelligent" — they're a logical consequence of defining winning as the sole objective. The King Midas problem illustrates this: Midas asked that everything he touch turn to gold, then regretted it when his food, drink, and family turned to gold. In AI, this is the risk of getting exactly what we literally ask for, not what we actually want.

The solution is not to have a definite purpose put into the machine at all. Instead, we want machines that strive to achieve human objectives but know that they don't know for certain exactly what those objectives are. When a machine is uncertain about the objective, it has an incentive to act cautiously, ask permission, learn more about our preferences through observation, and defer to human control. Ultimately, we want agents that are provably beneficial to humans. Almost all AI research to date has been within the standard model, but newer frameworks — assistance games (where a human has an objective and a machine tries to achieve it but is initially uncertain what it is) and inverse reinforcement learning (learning preferences from observing human choices) — are exploring these ideas.

Norbert Wiener (1960), after seeing Arthur Samuel's checker program learn to beat its creator, warned: "If we use, to achieve our purposes, a mechanical agency with whose operation we cannot interfere effectively... we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose which we really desire." Turing himself made the same point in 1951: "It seems probable that once the machine thinking method had started, it would not take long to outstrip our feeble powers... At some stage therefore we should have to expect the machines to take control."

The gorilla problem captures this in evolutionary terms: about seven million years ago, a now-extinct primate evolved, with one branch leading to gorillas and one to humans. Today, gorillas have essentially no control over their future. If superhuman AI is our evolutionary successor, humans might similarly cede control. The response: we design the AI systems, so if they "take control," it would be a design failure. The path forward is to build machines that are uncertain about human preferences and therefore have an incentive to let themselves be switched off.

On the timeline: surveys of AI experts on when human-level AI across a broad range of tasks might arrive find a wide range of target years (2029 to 2200), with a mean around 2099. About 50% of respondents in one survey predicted by 2066. But as Philip Tetlock demonstrated in the area of predicting world events, experts are no better than amateurs.

Key Terms / Glossary

AAAI
A major international AI conference (Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence).
Agent
An entity that perceives its environment and acts upon it (from Latin agere, to do).
AI Winter
A period of reduced funding and interest in AI following a wave of over-promising and under-delivering.
Assistance Game
A mathematical formulation where a human has an objective and a machine attempts to achieve it while initially uncertain about what the objective is — a move beyond the standard model.
Bayesian Network
A rigorous, efficient formalism (Judea Pearl, 1988) for representing uncertain knowledge, with practical algorithms for probabilistic reasoning.
Cognitive Modeling
The study of human thought processes (through introspection, experiments, brain imaging) to replicate them in machines.
Cognitive Science
The interdisciplinary field combining AI computer models with experimental psychology to construct testable theories of the human mind.
Connectionist Models
Neural network approaches using interconnected simple computing elements, contrasted with symbolic/logicist approaches.
Dartmouth Workshop (1956)
The summer conference where the term "Artificial Intelligence" was coined; 10 attendees including McCarthy, Minsky, Newell, Simon, and Samuel.
Decision Theory
The combination of probability theory and utility theory providing a formal framework for decisions under uncertainty.
Deep Blue
IBM's chess-playing computer that defeated Garry Kasparov in 1997.
Deep Learning
Machine learning using multiple layers of simple, adjustable computing elements; took off in 2011-2012 with dramatic improvements in speech recognition and visual object recognition.
ELIZA
An early chatbot (1960s) by Joseph Weizenbaum that simulated conversation.
Expert Systems
Knowledge-intensive programs using domain-specific rules for narrow areas of expertise (e.g., DENDRAL, MYCIN, R1); dominant in the 1980s.
Gorilla Problem
The concern that creating superhuman AI may lead to humans losing control over their future, analogous to how gorillas fare relative to humans from a shared evolutionary ancestor.
Hebbian Learning
Donald Hebb's (1949) rule for modifying connection strengths between neurons based on their correlated activity; remains an influential model today.
Inverse Reinforcement Learning
Methods allowing machines to learn human preferences by observing the choices humans make.
King Midas Problem
The risk of getting exactly what one literally asks for from an AI system, not what one actually wants (after Midas, who wished everything he touched turn to gold, then regretted it).
Laws of Thought
The tradition, originating with Aristotle, of codifying rational thinking through formal logic and correct inference via syllogisms.
Limited Rationality
Acting appropriately when there isn't enough time or computational resources to compute the exactly optimal action.
Lisp
The high-level AI programming language invented by John McCarthy in 1958; dominated the field for 30 years.
Logic Theorist (LT)
Newell and Simon's mathematical theorem-proving system presented at Dartmouth 1956; proved most of Chapter 2 of Principia Mathematica.
Markov Decision Process (MDP)
A formal framework for sequential decision problems (Bellman, 1957) where outcomes are partly random and partly under the agent's control.
Nash Equilibrium
A game-theory concept from economics where no player can improve their outcome by unilaterally changing strategy.
NP-Complete
A class of computationally intractable problems; reasoning over many logical facts is NP-complete.
Physical Symbol System Hypothesis
Newell and Simon's (1976) claim that "a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action" — that intelligence requires symbol manipulation.
Post-Facto Rationalization
The human tendency to act instinctively and then invent reasons for the action afterward.
Rational Agent
An agent that acts so as to achieve the best outcome (or best expected outcome under uncertainty), given available information.
Satisficing
Herbert Simon's concept of making "good enough" decisions rather than computing optimal ones — a better description of human behavior and the basis of his 1978 Nobel Prize in Economics.
Standard Model
The dominant AI paradigm: designing agents that optimize a fixed, pre-specified objective. Prevalent across AI, control theory, operations research, statistics, and economics.
Total Turing Test
An extension of the Turing test requiring interaction with real-world objects and people, adding computer vision and robotics to the required capabilities.
Transformer
The neural network architecture introduced in "Attention Is All You Need" (2017) that underpins modern LLMs (GPT, BERT, etc.).
Turing Test
A test of a machine's ability to exhibit intelligent behavior indistinguishable from a human, proposed by Alan Turing (1950).
Value Alignment Problem
The challenge of ensuring that the objectives we program into machines are aligned with human values — particularly as systems become more capable and deployed in the real world.
