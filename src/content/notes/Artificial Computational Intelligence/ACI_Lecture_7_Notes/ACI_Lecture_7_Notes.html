7.1 Neural Architecture Search and Neuroevolution

7.1.1 Evolution of Automated Architecture Design

Why do machine learning engineers spend months tuning layer counts, filter dimensions, and connection patterns by trial and error, when natural evolution has spent millions of years designing complex biological neural networks? Human intuition naturally imposes bias, restricting neural architectures to familiar, symmetric topologies and creating a severe design bottleneck.

Intuition & Analogy: Manual neural network design is like an architect drawing every room and hallway of a skyscraper by hand from scratch using familiar rectangular patterns. In contrast, automated architecture design acts like an evolutionary algorithm that tests hundreds of structural mutations, discovering non-intuitive, high-performance floor plans that a human designer would never consider. Human prior knowledge often acts as a restrictive bottleneck; neuroevolution breaks this limitation by exploring unconventional graph topologies.

Traditional artificial intelligence and deep learning historically relied on human experts to manually design neural network architectures. Designing neural network topologies manually presents significant challenges and creates a severe human bottleneck. Human engineers rely heavily on trial-and-error, prior domain knowledge, and established design heuristics. This reliance inherently constrains the exploration of non-intuitive or novel architectures. For instance, in complex fields like medical genomic analysis, automated search algorithms can uncover highly effective network structures that human designers would never conceive because human intuition is constrained by past design paradigms. Furthermore, as network complexity grows, optimizing deep architectures manually becomes computationally and practically intractable.

To overcome the human bottleneck, neuroevolution applies genetic algorithms to automatically search, design, and optimize neural network structures. Neuroevolution replaces manual engineering with an evolutionary search process inspired by biological natural selection. By framing architecture creation as an optimization problem over a search space of graphs \(G = (V, E)\), neuroevolution enables the discovery of tailored models without human intervention.

Formal Definition — Neuroevolution: Neuroevolution is a class of artificial intelligence algorithms that use evolutionary computation (such as genetic algorithms) to optimize neural network structures, parameters, or both. A neural network is represented as a genome (chromosome), and a population of candidate genomes undergoes rounds of selection, crossover, and mutation based on a calculated fitness score \(F(G)\).

7.1.2 NEAT and DeepNEAT Foundations

The development of neuroevolution for deep learning evolved through three major technological paradigms:

1. NEAT (NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies): NEAT is a foundational method that evolves neural network topologies and weights simultaneously using genetic algorithms. In NEAT, a neural network is represented as a directed graph \(G = (V, E)\) where:

  • Vertices \(V\) represent individual neurons.
  • Directed edges \(E\) represent individual synaptic connection weights \(w_{ij}\).

Evolution starts from a minimal baseline structure with minimal connectivity (only input and output nodes). Over successive generations, genetic operators like structural mutation gradually add new neurons and connection edges, increasing structural complexity incrementally. To prevent premature convergence and protect structural innovations, NEAT groups similar candidate networks into distinct species using historical markings, preserving topological diversity across the population.

Despite its effectiveness on small-scale control problems, NEAT exhibits a fundamental limitation: it operates strictly at the level of individual scalar neurons and single connection weights. Consequently, NEAT fails to scale to modern deep learning models (such as Convolutional Neural Networks or Long Short-Term Memory networks) that require multi-layer block architectures, multidimensional tensor operations, and millions of parameters.

2. DeepNEAT: DeepNEAT extends the NEAT algorithm to deep neural networks by shifting the semantic abstraction level of graph nodes. Instead of representing a single scalar neuron, each graph node \(v \in V\) in DeepNEAT represents an entire neural network layer (such as a 2D Convolutional layer, Dense layer, or LSTM layer).

  • Each node encapsulates complete layer metadata, including layer type and associated hyperparameters (e.g., filter size \(k \times k\), stride \(s\), activation function \(\sigma\)).
  • Graph edges \(e \in E\) define data flow tensors passing between layers.
  • When a node receives multiple incoming data flows, inputs are combined using tensor concatenation or summation operations. Mismatches in spatial dimensions or channel counts are resolved using pooling, stride adjustments, or downsampling layers.

However, DeepNEAT introduces a new structural bottleneck: the generated networks tend to be completely random, highly complex, and lack clear modular organization. Unlike modern hand-crafted deep architectures (such as ResNet or GoogLeNet) that rely on repeated, highly structured modular blocks, DeepNEAT fails to reuse learned structural patterns. Every generation creates arbitrary directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) from scratch without exploiting structural repetition.

3. CodeDeepNEAT: To resolve the random topology bottleneck of DeepNEAT and enable modular structural reuse, CodeDeepNEAT introduces a bi-level evolutionary search framework. CodeDeepNEAT decouples network design into two distinct evolving representations:

  1. High-level structural skeletons (blueprints).
  2. Reusable sub-networks (modules).

This bi-level separation mirrors modern deep learning design principles, enabling efficient, scalable, and structured architecture search across complex domains.

Key Takeaway: Neuroevolution addresses the human design bottleneck by automating topology discovery. While NEAT operated on single neurons and DeepNEAT scaled to whole layers, CodeDeepNEAT achieves modern structural modularity through a bi-level blueprint-module evolutionary paradigm.

Real-World & Domain Connection: Automated architecture search techniques like neuroevolution are actively deployed in medical imaging (e.g., discovering custom 3D UNet variants for brain tumor segmentation) and autonomous driving perception systems, where specialized sensor configurations demand non-standard network topologies optimized for low-latency edge inference hardware.

7.2 CodeDeepNeat Architecture and Bi-Level Evolution

7.2.1 Genotype Representation: Blueprints and Modules

How can an automated search algorithm discover deep learning architectures containing millions of parameters without falling into a chaotic mess of unstructured random connections? CodeDeepNEAT solves this by separating design into macro-architecture and micro-architecture.

Intuition & Analogy: Think of CodeDeepNEAT like designing a computer motherboard. The Blueprint is the motherboard layout that specifies where components go and how data buses connect them, but uses generic expansion slots. The Modules are the specific, interchangeable components (like RAM sticks, GPU cards, or audio cards) that fit into those slots. Instead of designing a custom circuit from scratch for every single chip, evolution reuses modular sub-circuits across different blueprint locations.

In CodeDeepNEAT, the genetic representation (genotype) is partitioned into two distinct hierarchical structures:

  1. Blueprint (Macro-structure): A directed acyclic graph (DAG) that defines the overall high-level architecture and macro data flow of the deep neural network. Nodes in a blueprint do not represent concrete neural network layers; instead, they act as slots associated with specific module species identifiers.
  2. Module (Micro-architecture): A small, fully functional neural sub-network composed of concrete layers and hyperparameters (e.g., a convolutional block with specific filter dimensions, stride, and activation function). Modules act as reusable building blocks that can be plugged into blueprint slots.

To track structural changes across generations and align parent structures during genetic operations, CodeDeepNEAT assigns a unique innovation number \(\text{Inn}_k \in \mathbb{N}\) to every structural mutation and module creation. Innovation numbers act as global primary keys in the search space, ensuring that crossover operations align corresponding structural components correctly and prevent invalid topological recombinations.

Misconception Correction: DeepNEAT randomly generates deep architectures without reusing learned blocks. CodeDeepNEAT explicitly resolves this by separating search into blueprints and modular building blocks, enabling structural reuse across the architecture.

7.2.2 Symbol Registry — CodeDeepNeat Optimization

Symbol Registry — CodeDeepNEAT Parameters & Metrics:

  • \(P_m\) — module population size — set of candidate sub-networks — \(P_m \subset \mathcal{G}_{\text{micro}}\)
  • \(P_b\) — blueprint population size — set of structural graphs — \(P_b \subset \mathcal{G}_{\text{macro}}\)

  • \(N_{\text{correct}}\) — number of correctly classified validation samples — non-negative integer — \(N_{\text{correct}} \in \{0, 1, \dots, N_{\text{total}}\}\)
  • \(N_{\text{total}}\) — total number of samples in the validation dataset — positive integer — \(N_{\text{total}} \in \mathbb{Z}^+\)
  • \(p_i\) — selection probability of candidate \(i\) in roulette wheel selection — scalar probability — \(p_i \in [0, 1]\)
  • \(\text{Inn}_k\) — historical innovation number identifier of structural mutation \(k\) — integer primary key — \(\text{Inn}_k \in \mathbb{N}\)
  • \(N\) — total population size of candidate architectures under selection — positive integer — \(N \in \mathbb{Z}^+\)

7.2.3 Genetic Algorithm Workflow in CodeDeepNeat

Procedural Overview — Bi-Level Genetic Optimization:

  1. Inputs: Initial hyperparameter bounds, module species count, population size \(N\), validation dataset \(D_{\text{val}}\), target generations \(G_{\text{max}}\).
  2. Outputs: An evolved, optimal neural network phenotype \(\mathcal{P}^*\) maximizing validation fitness.
  3. Execution Pipeline:
\[\text{Initialization} \longrightarrow \text{Phenotype Assembly} \longrightarrow \text{Fitness Evaluation} \longrightarrow \text{Selection} \longrightarrow \text{Crossover} \longrightarrow \text{Mutation} \longrightarrow \text{Speciation}\]

CodeDeepNEAT evolves networks through a multi-step bi-level genetic algorithm:

Step 1: Initialization

The algorithm initializes a population of simple, minimal blueprint graphs \(P_b\) and a sub-population of simple initial module sub-networks \(P_m\). Starting with minimal structural complexity prevents computational explosion and ensures efficient exploration from a clean baseline.

Step 2: Fitness Evaluation

To evaluate candidate fitness, each module and blueprint pair is assembled into a concrete neural network phenotype \(\mathcal{P}_i\) and trained using stochastic gradient descent.

Validation Accuracy Fitness Evaluation Formula: \[ f_i = \left( \frac{N_{\text{correct}}}{N_{\text{total}}} \right) \times 100 \] where \(N_{\text{correct}}\) is the number of correctly classified validation samples and \(N_{\text{total}}\) is the total number of validation samples.

To keep computation manageable during evolutionary search, training is conducted for a restricted number of epochs (e.g., 5 to 10 epochs) on a validation dataset (e.g., a 10,000 image cat-versus-dog classification dataset). The goal is to measure relative architectural suitability rather than full model convergence.

Worked Example 7.2.1 — Validation Accuracy Fitness Evaluation:

  • Problem Statement: A candidate neural network architecture assembled via CodeDeepNEAT is trained for 5 epochs on a cat-versus-dog image classification validation dataset containing \(N_{\text{total}} = 10,000\) images. Upon evaluation, the model correctly classifies \(N_{\text{correct}} = 9,000\) images. Compute the fitness score \(f_i\) for this candidate network.
  • Step 1: Identify given parameters.
  • \(N_{\text{correct}} = 9,000\)
  • \(N_{\text{total}} = 10,000\)
  • Step 2: Apply the fitness evaluation formula.
\[f_i = \left( \frac{N_{\text{correct}}}{N_{\text{total}}} \right) \times 100 = \left( \frac{9,000}{10,000} \right) \times 100\]
  • Step 3: Compute intermediate ratio.
\[\frac{9,000}{10,000} = 0.90\]
  • Step 4: Scale to percentage fitness score.
\[f_i = 0.90 \times 100 = \mathbf{90.0\%}\]
  • Sense Check: The network correctly classified \(90\%\) of validation samples, yielding a scalar fitness score of \(90.0\). Higher validation accuracy directly translates to a larger slice of the parent selection probability pool.

Step 3: Parent Selection

Parents are selected probabilistically using Roulette Wheel Selection (fitness proportionate selection) based on relative fitness scores.

Roulette Wheel Selection Probability Formula: \[ p_i = \frac{f_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} f_j} \] where \(p_i\) is the selection probability of candidate \(i\), \(f_i\) is its individual fitness score, and the denominator is the sum of fitness scores across all \(N\) candidates in the population.

Candidates with higher validation accuracy occupy larger proportions of the selection probability space, ensuring exploitation of high-performing architectures while maintaining exploration through occasional selection of lower-fitness candidates.

Worked Example 7.2.2 — Roulette Wheel Parent Selection Probabilities:

  • Problem Statement: Consider a candidate population of \(N = 4\) architectures with validation fitness scores: \(f_1 = 90.0\), \(f_2 = 80.0\), \(f_3 = 50.0\), and \(f_4 = 20.0\). Calculate the roulette wheel selection probability \(p_i\) for each candidate.
  • Step 1: Compute total population fitness \(\sum_{j=1}^{4} f_j\).
\[\sum_{j=1}^{4} f_j = f_1 + f_2 + f_3 + f_4 = 90.0 + 80.0 + 50.0 + 20.0 = 240.0\]
  • Step 2: Compute individual selection probabilities \(p_i = \frac{f_i}{240.0}\).
  • Candidate 1:
\[p_1 = \frac{90.0}{240.0} = \mathbf{0.3750} \quad (37.50\%)\]
  • Candidate 2:
\[p_2 = \frac{80.0}{240.0} = \mathbf{0.3333} \quad (33.33\%)\]
  • Candidate 3:
\[p_3 = \frac{50.0}{240.0} = \mathbf{0.2083} \quad (20.83\%)\]
  • Candidate 4:
\[p_4 = \frac{20.0}{240.0} = \mathbf{0.0833} \quad (8.33\%)\]
  • Step 3: Verification sum check.
\[\sum_{i=1}^{4} p_i = 0.3750 + 0.3333 + 0.2083 + 0.0833 = 1.0000 \quad (100.0\%)\]
  • Sense Check: Candidate 1 has the highest fitness (\(90.0\)) and secures the largest selection probability (\(37.5\%\)), making it most likely to pass its structural traits to offspring.

Step 4: Crossover

Selected parent architectures undergo structural crossover. For example, combining Parent 1 (\(\text{Conv} \to \text{LSTM} \to \text{Dense}\)) and Parent 2 (\(\text{Conv} \to \text{Conv} \to \text{Dense}\)) via 1-point crossover produces offspring like \(\text{Conv} \to \text{Conv} \to \text{Dense}\), inheriting feature extraction capabilities from both parents. Matching layer components are aligned using their unique innovation numbers \(\text{Inn}_k\).

Step 5: Mutation

Offspring undergo structural and hyperparameter mutations. Structural mutations add new layers or insert skip connections. Hyperparameter mutations modify layer properties (e.g., mutating a Convolutional filter size from \(3 \times 3\) to \(5 \times 5\)).

Step 6: Speciation

Child architectures are grouped into species based on topological similarity metrics. Speciation protects innovative new structures, allowing them time to optimize without competing directly against mature, dominant species.

Q: Is mutation optional in CodeDeepNEAT, or is it a mandatory step?

A: Mutation is a mandatory component of standard genetic algorithms and CodeDeepNEAT. While crossover recombines existing traits from parent networks, mutation is essential because it introduces entirely novel architectural traits (such as changing filter dimensions from \(3 \times 3\) to \(5 \times 5\) or adding skip connections) that were not present in either parent. Without mutation, the search space remains constrained to initial parent features.

7.2.4 Phenotype Construction and Assembly

The phenotype is the complete, fully assembled deep neural network used for final training and deployment. Phenotype construction bridges genotype representation and execution:

  1. Select an evolved blueprint defining macro data flow.
  2. Identify each slot node within the blueprint.
  3. For each slot node, pick an evolved module from the corresponding module species sub-population.
  4. Replace slot nodes with concrete module sub-networks.
  5. Connect module inputs and outputs according to blueprint edge topology, inserting pooling or dimensional matching layers at interface nodes where shape mismatches occur.

Scope & Assumptions: CodeDeepNEAT assumes that short-epoch validation fitness (e.g., 5 epochs) strongly correlates with fully trained convergence accuracy (e.g., 100 epochs). If this assumption fails (e.g., learning rates behave differently across architectures early on), fitness evaluations can become noisy.

Common Pitfall: Confusing genotype with phenotype. The genotype is the abstract graph representation (blueprints + modules with innovation numbers), whereas the phenotype is the actual compiled, executable neural network graph (e.g., in PyTorch or TensorFlow) ready for tensor processing.

Key Takeaway: CodeDeepNEAT achieves scalable neural architecture search through a bi-level evolutionary paradigm: macro-level blueprints define graph data flow, while micro-level modules supply reusable layer blocks.

Real-World & Domain Connection: CodeDeepNEAT architectures are deployed in automated vision pipelines, speech recognition, and medical diagnostic systems (such as automated cell classification), where bi-level evolution automatically discovers optimized skip connections and multi-scale feature extractors tailored to domain-specific datasets.

7.3 Adversarial Search and Game Formulations

7.3.1 Principles of Adversarial Environments

In classic single-agent search algorithms (like A* or Breadth-First Search), the environment is completely passive and predictable. What happens when the environment is an active, intelligent opponent attempting to defeat you at every turn?

Intuition & Analogy: Single-agent search is like running an obstacle course: the hurdles are fixed, and you simply plot the shortest path to the finish line. Adversarial search is like playing a game of Chess or Tic-Tac-Toe against a master player: every step you take triggers an immediate counter-move designed to ruin your strategy. You cannot simply ask "What path reaches my goal?"; you must ask "If I take step A, what is my opponent's absolute best counter-step B?"

Unlike single-agent search environments where an agent plans in isolation against a passive environment, adversarial search operates in multi-agent environments where two or more agents have opposing, conflicting goals. In an adversarial setting, one agent's gain is directly another agent's loss (a zero-sum game).

An agent in an adversarial environment cannot select actions purely by asking which move brings it closest to its own goal. Instead, the agent must continuously anticipate the counter-actions of its opponent: "What will my opponent do to reduce my chances of success?" Strategic play requires balancing offensive moves (maximizing one's own utility) with defensive moves (blocking the opponent's winning pathways).

Formal Definition — Zero-Sum Game: A two-player game is zero-sum if the total payout across both players is constant for every outcome. If Player 1 (Max) receives utility \(U_{\text{Max}}\) and Player 2 (Min) receives utility \(U_{\text{Min}}\), then for all terminal states \(s\): \[ U_{\text{Max}}(s) + U_{\text{Min}}(s) = 0 \implies U_{\text{Min}}(s) = -U_{\text{Max}}(s) \] Because Min's gain is exactly Max's loss, Max attempts to maximize utility while Min attempts to minimize Max's utility.

7.3.2 Formal Game Components (PEAS & Game State)

A game in artificial intelligence is formally defined by six fundamental components:

The 6 Formal Game Components:

  1. Initial State (\(S_0\)): The starting board configuration or initial setup of the game.
  2. Player Function (\(\text{Player}(s)\)): Defines which player's turn it is to move in state \(s\).
  3. Actions Function (\(\text{Actions}(s)\)): Returns the set of legal moves available to \(\text{Player}(s)\) in state \(s\).
  4. Result Function (\(\text{Result}(s, a)\)): The transition model returning the new state \(s'\) resulting from taking action \(a\) in state \(s\).
  5. Terminal Test (\(\text{Terminal-Test}(s)\)): A boolean test returning True if the game has ended (win, loss, or draw) and False otherwise. States where the game has ended are called terminal states.
  6. Utility Function (\(\text{Utility}(s, p)\)): A payoff function that assigns a numeric value to a terminal state \(s\) for a specific player \(p\). In a two-player zero-sum game between Max and Min, utilities are defined from Max's perspective: \(+1\) for a Max win, \(-1\) for a Min win (Max loss), and \(0\) for a draw.

7.3.3 Symbol Registry — Game Formulations

Symbol Registry — Game State & Formalization:

  • \(S_0\) — initial game state — empty or starting board state — \(S_0 \in \mathcal{S}\)
  • \(s\) — current game state — arbitrary board configuration — \(s \in \mathcal{S}\)
  • \(a\) — action / move — legal transition move — \(a \in \text{Actions}(s)\)
  • \(p\) — active player identifier — decision-making agent — \(p \in \{\text{Max}, \text{Min}\}\)
  • \(\text{Result}(s, a)\) — successor state transition — state resulting from action \(a\) — \(\text{Result}(s, a) \in \mathcal{S}\)
  • \(\text{Utility}(s, p)\) — numeric payoff of terminal state \(s\) for player \(p\) — scalar — \(\text{Utility}(s, p) \in \mathbb{R}\)

7.3.4 Worked Example: Tic-Tac-Toe Game Formulation

Worked Example 7.3.1 — Tic-Tac-Toe Game Formalization:

  • Problem Statement: Formulate the classic game of Tic-Tac-Toe as a formal 6-component adversarial search problem.
  • 1. Initial State (\(S_0\)): An empty \(3 \times 3\) grid containing 9 blank cells.
  • 2. Player Function (\(\text{Player}(s)\)): \(\text{Player}(S_0) = \text{Max (X)}\). Players alternate turns: Max plays X and Min plays O.
  • 3. Actions Function (\(\text{Actions}(s)\)):
  • At state \(S_0\), \(\text{Player}(S_0) = \text{Max}\) has 9 legal actions corresponding to placing X in any blank cell.
  • At depth 1, after Max places X, \(\text{Player}(s_1) = \text{Min}\) has 8 legal actions corresponding to placing O in the remaining 8 empty cells.
  • 4. Result Function (\(\text{Result}(s, a)\)): Placing symbol X or O in cell \((r, c)\) yields a new grid state \(s'\) with that cell marked as occupied.
  • 5. Terminal Test (\(\text{Terminal-Test}(s)\)): Returns True if three identical symbols (X or O) align horizontally, vertically, or diagonally, or if all 9 cells are filled with no winner.
  • 6. Utility Function (\(\text{Utility}(s)\)): Formulated from Max's perspective:
\[\text{Utility}(s) = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{if Max (X) wins} \\ 0 & \text{if game ends in a draw} \\ -1 & \text{if Min (O) wins} \end{cases}\]
  • Sense Check: The formulation covers all possible grid configurations, specifies explicit alternating turns, bounds legal move counts at each turn (\(9 \to 8 \to 7 \dots\)), and provides definitive terminal payoffs.

Scope & Assumptions: Adversarial search formulations assume deterministic, fully observable, two-player zero-sum environments with perfect information. They do not directly apply to games involving chance (e.g., Backgammon dice rolls) or hidden information (e.g., Poker card hands) without incorporating expected utility or probabilistic belief states.

Common Pitfalls:

  • Assuming the opponent will make a mistake or select sub-optimal moves. Adversarial algorithms must assume the opponent plays optimally to minimize your score.
  • Confusing utility values \(\text{Utility}(s)\) (which are exact numeric payoffs defined only for terminal states) with static evaluation heuristics (which estimate state values for non-terminal cutoff states).

Exam note: For assignment design documentation and exam questions on problem formulation, always specify the formal PEAS model (Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, Sensors) and explicit state transition functions (\(S_0, \text{Player}, \text{Actions}, \text{Result}, \text{Terminal-Test}, \text{Utility}\)) rather than hardcoding static rules. Mid-sem exams test adversarial search formulations and Minimax up to Lecture 8.

Key Takeaway: Adversarial search formalizes games via 6 core components: \(S_0\), \(\text{Player}(s)\), \(\text{Actions}(s)\), \(\text{Result}(s, a)\), \(\text{Terminal-Test}(s)\), and \(\text{Utility}(s)\). In zero-sum games, Max maximizes payoff while assuming Min will optimally minimize it.

Real-World & Domain Connection: Adversarial game formulations are used in automated contract negotiation bots, cyber-security defense allocation (modeling attacker vs defender interactions as zero-sum games), and algorithmic game theory applications in competitive market pricing.

7.4 The Minimax Algorithm and Game Trees

7.4.1 Minimax Decision Principle and Tree Structure

If you are playing Chess and see an aggressive move that lets you capture your opponent's Queen, should you instantly play it? Not if capturing that Queen leaves your King completely exposed to an immediate checkmate on the next turn! Naive greedy play fails in multi-agent games.

Intuition & Analogy: Think of the Minimax algorithm like a chess grandmaster engaging in pessimistic planning. Before making a move, Max looks ahead through all possible future turns. For every candidate move Max could make, Max assumes Min will respond with their absolute best counter-move to hurt Max. Instead of chasing a risky branch containing a huge peak payoff that Min can easily block, Max chooses the move whose worst-case outcome is as high as possible (the maximin principle).

A game tree is an explicit directed graph representation of all possible move sequences in a game, starting from the initial state root \(S_0\) down to terminal leaf nodes. Tree levels alternate between Max decision nodes (where Max selects a move to maximize score) and Min decision nodes (where Min selects a move to minimize Max's score).

The Minimax algorithm computes the optimal move for Max from any given state by recursively computing the minimax value of all successor states. Minimax operates strictly on the maximin principle: Max does not naively pick a branch simply because it contains the highest theoretical payoff peak; instead, Max selects the move that maximizes the guaranteed worst-case outcome under optimal opponent play.

Teaching Moment — Maximin Strategy: Minimax does not chase naive peak payoffs. Max chooses the branch whose worst-case outcome is best, assuming the opponent will play perfectly to minimize Max's payoff.

7.4.2 Symbol Registry — Game Tree and Minimax Decisions

Symbol Registry — Game Tree & Minimax Quantities:

  • \(s\) — game state node — arbitrary state configuration in game tree — \(s \in \mathcal{S}\)
  • \(a\) — action / move — valid move available from state \(s\) — \(a \in \text{Actions}(s)\)
  • \(\text{Result}(s, a)\) — successor state resulting from playing action \(a\) in state \(s\) — \(\text{Result}(s, a) \in \mathcal{S}\)
  • \(v(s)\) — minimax value of state \(s\) — backed-up utility score — \(v(s) \in \mathbb{R}\)
  • \(\text{Utility}(s)\) — terminal numeric payoff value of leaf state \(s\) — scalar payoff — \(\text{Utility}(s) \in \mathbb{R}\)
  • \(b\) — branching factor — number of legal moves available per state — \(b \in \mathbb{Z}^+\)
  • \(m\) — maximum depth of the search tree — non-negative integer — \(m \in \mathbb{N}\)

7.4.3 Mathematical Formulation and Backing-Up Values

Recursive Minimax Decision Equation: The minimax value \(v(s)\) of a state \(s\) is computed recursively bottom-up from the leaf nodes to the root: \[ v(s) = \begin{cases} \text{Utility}(s) & \text{if } \text{Terminal-Test}(s) \\ \max_{a \in \text{Actions}(s)} v(\text{Result}(s, a)) & \text{if } \text{Player}(s) = \text{Max} \\ \min_{a \in \text{Actions}(s)} v(\text{Result}(s, a)) & \text{if } \text{Player}(s) = \text{Min} \end{cases} \]

  • At Terminal nodes, \(v(s)\) equals the exact game utility \(\text{Utility}(s)\).
  • At Max nodes, Max maximizes over child values, initializing the running score to \(-\infty\).
  • At Min nodes, Min minimizes over child values, initializing the running score to \(+\infty\).
  • Values are backed up bottom-up following a Depth-First Search (DFS) in-order traversal (left child, root update, right child).

7.4.4 Worked Step-by-Step Computations

Worked Example 7.4.1 — Basic Depth-2 Game Tree Walkthrough:

  • Problem Statement: Consider a depth-2 game tree with root Max node \(A\), child Min nodes \(B\) (left child of \(A\)) and \(C\) (right child of \(A\)), and four terminal leaf nodes with payoffs:
  • Children of Min node \(B\): Leaf \(D = 4\), Leaf \(E = 7\)
  • Children of Min node \(C\): Leaf \(F = 2\), Leaf \(G = 6\)

Compute the backed-up minimax values at nodes \(B\), \(C\), and root \(A\), and identify Max's optimal move.

Root A (Max) [v = ?] / \ Min B [v = ?] Min C [v = ?] / \ / \ Leaf D (4) Leaf E (7) Leaf F (2) Leaf G (6)

  • Step 1: Initialize node comparison bounds.
  • Root \(A\) (Max): initialize \(v(A) = -\infty\).
  • Child \(B\) (Min): initialize \(v(B) = +\infty\).
  • Child \(C\) (Min): initialize \(v(C) = +\infty\).
  • Step 2: Compute Min node \(B\) value.
  • Evaluate left child Leaf \(D = 4\): \(v(B) = \min(+\infty, 4) = 4\).
  • Evaluate right child Leaf \(E = 7\): \(v(B) = \min(4, 7) = 4\).
  • Value at node \(B\) is \(v(B) = \mathbf{4}\).
  • Step 3: Update root Max node \(A\) from left child \(B\).
  • \(v(A) = \max(-\infty, v(B)) = \max(-\infty, 4) = 4\).
  • Step 4: Compute Min node \(C\) value.
  • Evaluate left child Leaf \(F = 2\): \(v(C) = \min(+\infty, 2) = 2\).
  • Evaluate right child Leaf \(G = 6\): \(v(C) = \min(2, 6) = 2\).
  • Value at node \(C\) is \(v(C) = \mathbf{2}\).
  • Step 5: Update root Max node \(A\) from right child \(C\).
  • \(v(A) = \max(4, v(C)) = \max(4, 2) = \mathbf{4}\).
  • Result & Move Selection: Max chooses move to left node \(B\), securing a guaranteed backed-up score of \(4\).
  • Sense Check: Although branch \(B\) contains leaf \(7\), Max cannot count on receiving \(7\) because opponent Min will play optimally to force outcome \(4\). If Max had chosen right branch \(C\), Min would have forced outcome \(2\). Choosing \(B\) maximizes the minimum payoff (\(\max(4, 2) = 4\)).

Worked Example 7.4.2 — In-Order Traversal with Bounds (\(-\infty\) / \(+\infty\)):

  • Problem Statement: Walk through a 3-level game tree evaluated via in-order depth-first search:
  • Root node \(A\) (Max, initialized to \(-\infty\))
  • Left child \(B\) (Min, initialized to \(+\infty\)); Right child \(C\) (Min, initialized to \(+\infty\))
  • Sub-children under \(B\): Max node \(D\) (leaves \(-1, 8\)), Max node \(E\) (leaves \(-3, -1\))
  • Sub-children under \(C\): Max node \(F\) (leaves \(2, 1\)), Max node \(G\) (leaves \(-3, 4\))

Root A (Max) / \ Min B Min C / \ / \ Max D Max E Max F Max G / \ / \ / \ / \ -1 8 -3 -1 2 1 -3 4

  • Step 1: Compute left subtree under Min node \(B\).
  • Max node \(D\): Evaluates leaves \(-1\) and \(8\).
\[v(D) = \max(-\infty, -1) \to \max(-1, 8) = \mathbf{8}\]
  • Min node \(B\): Updates from \(D\): \(v(B) = \min(+\infty, 8) = 8\).
  • Max node \(E\): Evaluates leaves \(-3\) and \(-1\).
\[v(E) = \max(-\infty, -3) \to \max(-3, -1) = \mathbf{-1}\]
  • Min node \(B\): Updates from \(E\): \(v(B) = \min(8, -1) = \mathbf{-1}\).
  • Step 2: Update Root Max node \(A\) from left subtree \(B\).
\[v(A) = \max(-\infty, v(B)) = \max(-\infty, -1) = -1\]
  • Step 3: Compute right subtree under Min node \(C\).
  • Max node \(F\): Evaluates leaves \(2\) and \(1\).
\[v(F) = \max(-\infty, 2) \to \max(2, 1) = \mathbf{2}\]
  • Min node \(C\): Updates from \(F\): \(v(C) = \min(+\infty, 2) = 2\).
  • Max node \(G\): Evaluates leaves \(-3\) and \(4\).
\[v(G) = \max(-\infty, -3) \to \max(-3, 4) = \mathbf{4}\]
  • Min node \(C\): Updates from \(G\): \(v(C) = \min(2, 4) = \mathbf{2}\).
  • Step 4: Update Root Max node \(A\) from right subtree \(C\).
\[v(A) = \max(-1, v(C)) = \max(-1, 2) = \mathbf{2}\]
  • Result: The backed-up minimax value at root \(A\) is \(2\). Max chooses the right move to node \(C\).
  • Sense Check: By selecting move \(C\), Max guarantees a payoff of \(2\). If Max had selected move \(B\), opponent Min would have picked node \(E\) to force payoff \(-1\).

Minimax Computational Complexity:

  • Time Complexity: \(O(b^m)\), where \(b\) is the average branching factor (number of legal moves per state) and \(m\) is the maximum depth of the game tree.
  • Space Complexity: \(O(bm)\) for depth-first search traversal, storing the path and successor nodes.

Q: Why do we initialize Max nodes to \(-\infty\) and Min nodes to \(+\infty\)?

A: Initialization sets the baseline comparison bound. A Max node seeks the largest possible value, so starting at \(-\infty\) ensures that any real score encountered during traversal will be greater and correctly update the maximum score. Conversely, a Min node seeks the smallest value, so starting at \(+\infty\) guarantees that any real leaf score will be smaller and correctly lower the minimum bound.

Q: Does Minimax guarantee that Max will always reach the highest possible leaf value in the tree?

A: No. Minimax does not guarantee reaching the absolute highest theoretical leaf score (e.g., a peak value of 15) because the opponent (Min) also plays strategically to block high payoffs. Minimax guarantees the best worst-case outcome (the highest guaranteed payoff under optimal opponent play), preventing the agent from falling into catastrophic traps.

Scope & Assumptions: Minimax assumes that the game tree is finite, fully observable, and that both players are perfectly rational utility maximizers/minimizers. If the game tree is too deep (e.g., Chess with \(b \approx 35, m \approx 80\)), full Minimax search is computationally impossible and requires depth cutoffs.

Common Pitfall:

  • Confusing tree level identities: forgetting that Max nodes update via \(\max()\) and Min nodes update via \(\min()\).
  • Reversing initialization bounds (initializing Max to \(+\infty\) or Min to \(-\infty\)), which corrupts all backed-up values.

Key Takeaway: Minimax computes the optimal strategy for zero-sum games by backing up values from leaf nodes to the root, using \(\max\) at Max nodes and \(\min\) at Min nodes. It guarantees the optimal worst-case outcome under perfect opponent play.

Real-World & Domain Connection: Minimax with game-tree search powers classic game engines (such as Chess, Checkers, and Othello engines), automated financial risk management (hedging against worst-case market drops), and defensive decision-making in autonomous robotics.

7.5 Static Evaluation Functions

7.5.1 Board Evaluation at Cutoff Depths

In complex games like Chess, the total number of possible game tree paths is estimated at \(10^{120}\) — far exceeding the total number of atoms in the observable universe. How can an AI choose the optimal move when expanding the tree down to terminal leaf nodes is physically impossible?

Intuition & Analogy: A static evaluation function is like taking a snapshot of a football game at halftime and checking the scoreboard. You cannot see the future moves that will happen in the second half, but by measuring current score, player stamina, and field position, you can make an educated estimate of which team is currently favored to win.

For complex real-world games like Chess, Checkers, or Go, the game tree is too vast to expand down to terminal leaf nodes. In such cases, search is truncated at a designated cutoff depth \(d\), and non-terminal board positions are evaluated using a static evaluation function \(e(s)\).

A static evaluation function estimates the favorability of a non-terminal state \(s\) from the perspective of the Max player. It acts as a heuristic metric combining piece values, positional advantage, domain rules, and tactical control.

Formal Definition — Static Evaluation Function: A static evaluation function \(e: \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}\) maps a non-terminal game state \(s\) to a real-valued score representing Max's relative advantage:

  • \(e(s) > 0\): State \(s\) favors Max.
  • \(e(s) = 0\): State \(s\) is neutral (even position).
  • \(e(s) < 0\): State \(s\) favors Min.

In general, static evaluation functions are expressed as linear weighted combinations of \(k\) domain features: \[ e(s) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i f_i(s) = w_1 f_1(s) + w_2 f_2(s) + \dots + w_k f_k(s) \] where \(f_i(s)\) represents a numerical board feature (e.g., material count, mobility, center control, king safety) and \(w_i\) represents the weight assigned to feature \(i\).

7.5.2 Symbol Registry — Static Board Evaluation

Symbol Registry — Static Evaluation Terms:

  • \(s\) — non-terminal board position state — \(s \in \mathcal{S}\)
  • \(e(s)\) — static evaluation score of state \(s\) — heuristic payoff estimate — \(e(s) \in \mathbb{R}\)
  • \(f_i(s)\) — numerical board feature \(i\) extracted from state \(s\) — real value — \(f_i(s) \in \mathbb{R}\)
  • \(w_i\) — relative weight coefficient for feature \(i\) — scalar weight — \(w_i \in \mathbb{R}\)
  • \(\text{Utility}_{\text{White}}\) — total material score of White pieces — scalar points — \(\text{Utility}_{\text{White}} \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\)
  • \(\text{Utility}_{\text{Black}}\) — total material score of Black pieces — scalar points — \(\text{Utility}_{\text{Black}} \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\)

7.5.3 Worked Example: Chess Board Static Evaluation

Consider a classic material-difference static evaluation function for Chess based on standard piece point values:

Piece Type Symbol Point Value
Queen Q 9 points
Rook / Bishop / Knight R / B / N 3 points
Pawn / Soldier P 1 point

Material-Difference Evaluation Formula: Evaluating the static board value from White's perspective: \[ e(s) = \text{Utility}_{\text{White}} - \text{Utility}_{\text{Black}} \] where \(\text{Utility}_{\text{White}}\) is the total point value of White pieces currently on the board, and \(\text{Utility}_{\text{Black}}\) is the total point value of Black pieces.

Worked Example 7.5.1 — Chess Board Material Evaluation:

  • Problem Statement: Evaluate the static board value \(e(s)\) from White's perspective for a mid-game Chess position with the following surviving pieces:
  • Black pieces: 1 Queen (9 pts), 1 Bishop (3 pts), 1 Pawn (1 pt).
  • White pieces: 2 Bishops (3 pts each), no Queen.
  • Step 1: Compute total material score for Black (\(\text{Utility}_{\text{Black}}\)).
\[\text{Utility}_{\text{Black}} = 9 (\text{Queen}) + 3 (\text{Bishop}) + 1 (\text{Pawn}) = 13 \text{ points}\]
  • Step 2: Compute total material score for White (\(\text{Utility}_{\text{White}}\)).
\[\text{Utility}_{\text{White}} = 3 (\text{Bishop 1}) + 3 (\text{Bishop 2}) = 6 \text{ points}\]
  • Step 3: Apply the static evaluation formula (White - Black).
\[e(s) = \text{Utility}_{\text{White}} - \text{Utility}_{\text{Black}} = 6 - 13 = \mathbf{-7}\]
  • Interpretation: The negative static evaluation value (\(-7\)) indicates that White is at a severe 7-point material deficit, and Black holds a commanding advantage in position.
  • Sense Check: Black holds a Queen (\(9\)) plus extra material, whereas White only has two minor pieces (\(6\)). \(6 - 13 = -7\) correctly reflects White's 7-point disadvantage.

Scope & Assumptions: Static evaluation functions assume that board features measured at cutoff depth \(d\) are quiet (quiescence assumption). If search is cut off right in the middle of a piece trade (e.g., White's Queen is about to capture Black's Queen on the very next ply), the static evaluation will severely miscalculate state quality (the horizon effect).

Common Pitfall: Confusing material count with total strategic value. While material count is a primary feature, advanced evaluation functions must also incorporate positional features (such as pawn structure, open files, and king safety) to avoid picking material-rich but strategically doomed states.

Key Takeaway: When game trees are too deep to expand fully, Minimax truncates search at a cutoff depth and relies on static evaluation functions \(e(s)\) to estimate non-terminal board favorability. Material difference (\(\text{Utility}_{\text{White}} - \text{Utility}_{\text{Black}}\)) serves as a foundational heuristic.

Real-World & Domain Connection: Modern chess engines (like Stockfish NNUE) and game AI algorithms replace hand-crafted linear evaluation weights with deep neural networks trained via reinforcement learning, enabling evaluation functions to assess subtle positional patterns in real time.
