Chapter 1: Local Search Foundations — Where We Left Off

6.1.1 Recap of Local Search Techniques

Local search is a family of optimization algorithms where every state is itself a complete solution. Unlike classical search algorithms that construct a solution step-by-step along a path, local search operates by starting with a complete (though possibly suboptimal) configuration and iteratively refining it through local modifications. This is called a complete-state formulation: every state has all the components of a solution, but they might not all be in the right place. For the 8-queens problem, for instance, every state has 8 queens on the board (one per column), and successors are generated by moving a single queen within its column.

We can think of the state space as a state-space landscape where each point (state) has an "elevation" defined by the objective function. If elevation corresponds to an objective we want to maximize, the aim is to find the highest peak — a global maximum — and the search process is called hill climbing. If elevation corresponds to cost we want to minimize, the aim is to find the lowest valley — a global minimum — and we call it gradient descent.

Local search is not systematic — it does not keep track of paths or visited states, so it might never explore a portion of the search space where a solution actually resides. However, it uses very little memory and can often find reasonable solutions in large or infinite state spaces for which systematic algorithms are unsuitable.

Local search techniques are broadly classified into two categories:

Single-Instance Methods: The algorithm maintains and improves a single candidate solution. Key variants include:
  • Hill climbing (steepest-ascent): At each step, move to the neighboring state with the highest value. Terminates when no neighbor has a higher value. Greedy — it never looks ahead beyond immediate neighbors. Gets stuck 86% of the time on 8-queens, solving only 14% of instances, but works quickly (4 steps on average when it succeeds).
  • Stochastic hill climbing: Chooses randomly from among the uphill moves, with selection probability proportional to the steepness of the uphill move. Usually converges more slowly than steepest ascent but may find better solutions.
  • First-choice hill climbing: Generates successors randomly until one is better than the current state. A good strategy when a state has many (e.g., thousands of) successors.
  • Random-restart hill climbing: Runs hill climbing repeatedly from randomly generated initial states until a goal is found. Complete with probability 1 (eventually a goal state will be generated as the initial state). If each run has success probability p, the expected number of restarts is 1/p. For 8-queens with sideways moves allowed (p ≈ 0.94), only about 1.06 iterations are needed on average.
  • Hill climbing with sideways moves: Allows horizontal moves on plateaus (hoping the plateau is actually a shoulder leading to a peak). A limit on consecutive sideways moves (e.g., 100) prevents infinite wandering on flat local maxima. This raises the 8-queens success rate from 14% to 94%.
  • Simulated annealing: Allows occasional "downhill" moves to escape local maxima. The probability of accepting a worse move decreases exponentially with the amount of worsening (ΔE) and the "temperature" T: \(P(\text{accept worse}) = e^{\Delta E / T}\). T starts high (more exploration) and decreases according to a cooling schedule. As T → 0, the algorithm converges to hill climbing.
Multi-Instance Methods: The algorithm maintains and evolves a population of candidate solutions simultaneously. This is where genetic algorithms and ant colony optimization belong. The critical distinction within multi-instance methods is whether the individuals collaborate with each other or merely run in parallel.

6.1.2 The Collaboration Gap

In local beam search, \(K\) random states are initialized. At each step, all successors of all \(K\) states are generated. If any is a goal, the algorithm halts. Otherwise, it selects the \(K\) best successors from the complete list and repeats. In effect, the \(K\) states share information through this shared selection: the states generating the best successors guide the search by saying "come over here, the grass is greener!" The algorithm quickly abandons unfruitful searches and moves resources where progress is being made.

However, after the selection step at each iteration, the states operate in isolation — each independently generates successors from its own neighborhood. There is no mechanism to combine genetic material from different states. This is precisely the gap that genetic algorithms fill. In GA, the population members actively share genetic material through crossover, allowing beneficial traits discovered by different individuals to combine and propagate through generations. This collaboration is the single most important distinguishing feature of genetic algorithms within the multi-instance family.

Key Insight: Beam search = parallelism with information sharing (via best successor selection). Genetic algorithms = parallelism with collaboration through genetic recombination. This fundamental distinction explains why GA can explore more diverse regions of the search space through crossover.

Local beam search can suffer from a lack of diversity — the \(K\) states can cluster in a small region of the state space, making the search little more than a \(K\)-times-slower version of hill climbing. A variant called stochastic beam search alleviates this by choosing successors with probability proportional to their value (rather than deterministically picking the top \(K\)), thus increasing diversity.

Chapter 2: Genetic Algorithms — Core Principles

6.2.1 Bio-Inspired Algorithms and Evolutionary Inspiration

Genetic algorithms belong to a broader class called nature-inspired or bio-inspired algorithms. These algorithms abstract principles observed in natural systems—evolution, swarm behavior, immune responses—and encode them as computational search procedures. GA specifically draws from Darwinian evolution: the process by which populations of organisms adapt to their environment over generations through variation (mutation and recombination) and selection (survival of the fittest).

Evolutionary algorithms can be seen as variants of stochastic beam search that are explicitly motivated by the metaphor of natural selection. There is a population of individuals (states), and the fittest (highest value) individuals produce offspring (successor states) that populate the next generation—a process called recombination. Evolutionary algorithms differ along several dimensions:

  • Representation: In genetic algorithms, each individual is a string over a finite alphabet (often a Boolean string), just as DNA is a string over ACGT. In evolution strategies, an individual is a sequence of real numbers. In genetic programming, an individual is a computer program.
  • Mixing number (ρ): The number of parents that combine to form offspring. The most common case is ρ = 2 (two parents). When ρ = 1, we have stochastic beam search (asexual reproduction).
  • Selection process: One possibility is fitness-proportionate selection (the roulette wheel). Another is to randomly select n individuals (n > ρ), then pick the ρ most fit as parents (tournament selection).
  • Crossover (recombination): For ρ = 2, randomly select a crossover point, split each parent, and recombine parts to form two children.
  • Mutation rate: How often random mutations flip bits in the offspring's representation. Once an offspring is generated, every bit is flipped with probability equal to the mutation rate.
  • Next-generation strategy: Can include elitism (top-scoring parents from the previous generation survive unchanged, guaranteeing fitness never decreases). Culling discards all individuals below a threshold to speed up the search.

In the computational analogue:

  • Individuals are candidate solutions to the optimization problem.
  • Population is a collection of individuals maintained simultaneously.
  • Fitness quantifies how good a solution is—analogous to how well an organism is adapted to its environment.
  • Generations are iterations of the algorithm where the population is replaced by offspring.
  • Genes are the atomic units of representation (bits, numbers, or vector components) that encode a solution.

6.2.2 The Seven-Step Genetic Algorithm Pipeline

The canonical genetic algorithm proceeds through seven well-defined steps. The first three are shared with other local search methods; the last four constitute the evolutionary core unique to GA.

Steps 1–3 (Familiar from prior techniques):
  1. Represent the problem — choose an encoding scheme (e.g., a vector for the \(n\)-Queens problem, a bit string for binary optimization).
  2. Generate an initial population — create \(K\) random individuals (states). The population size \(K\) is a parameter.
  3. Compute fitness — evaluate each individual using the fitness (objective) function.
Steps 4–7 (Unique to genetic algorithms):
  1. Parent Selection — choose which individuals will reproduce to form the next generation.
  2. Crossover (Recombination) — combine the genetic material of parent pairs to produce children.
  3. Mutation — introduce random, small changes to the children's genes.
  4. Replace and Repeat — the children become the new population. If the termination condition is not met, return to Step 4.

6.2.3 Representation and the 4-Queens Problem

For the \(n\)-Queens problem, the standard encoding uses a vector of length \(n\), where the index represents the column number and the value at that index represents the row position of the queen. For example, the vector \([1, 4, 2, 2]\) means:

  • Column 1: Queen in row 1
  • Column 2: Queen in row 4
  • Column 3: Queen in row 2
  • Column 4: Queen in row 2

This compact encoding naturally constrains each column to exactly one queen, reducing the search space from \(16 \choose 4\) to \(4^4 = 256\) configurations.

6.2.4 Fitness Function for the 4-Queens Problem

The fitness of a board configuration is defined as the number of non-attacking queen pairs. For 4 queens, there are \(\binom{4}{2} = 6\) possible pairs: (Q1,Q2), (Q1,Q3), (Q1,Q4), (Q2,Q3), (Q2,Q4), (Q3,Q4). For each pair, we check whether the two queens share the same row or the same diagonal, in which case they are attacking. The fitness value is the count of pairs that do not attack each other.

The maximum possible fitness is 6, corresponding to a configuration where all 6 pairs are non-attacking—that is, a valid solution to the 4-Queens problem.

Example: For board \([1, 4, 2, 2]\):
  • Q1 (row 1) and Q2 (row 4): different rows, diagonal |4−1| = 3 ≠ |2−1| = 1 → non-attacking
  • Q1 (row 1) and Q3 (row 2): different rows, diagonal |2−1| = 1 ≠ |3−1| = 2 → non-attacking
  • Q1 (row 1) and Q4 (row 2): different rows, diagonal |2−1| = 1 ≠ |4−1| = 3 → non-attacking
  • Q2 (row 4) and Q3 (row 2): different rows, diagonal |2−4| = 2 = |3−2| = 1 → attacking (same diagonal)
  • Q2 (row 4) and Q4 (row 2): different rows, diagonal |2−4| = 2 = |4−2| = 2 → attacking (same diagonal)
  • Q3 (row 2) and Q4 (row 2): same row → attacking
Fitness = 3 non-attacking pairs out of 6.

6.2.5 Stopping Criteria

A genetic algorithm terminates when any one of these three conditions is satisfied:

  1. Fitness threshold reached: An individual achieves the maximum possible fitness value (e.g., 6 for the 4-Queens problem, or 28 for the 8-Queens problem where there are \(\binom{8}{2} = 28\) queen pairs). This means the global optimum has been found.
  2. Generation limit exhausted: A predefined number of iterations (generations) or new states have been produced. The best individual found so far is returned.
  3. No change (stagnation): The fitness values of the population in iteration \(t\) are identical to those in iteration \(t+1\). The algorithm has converged or stalled, and further iterations would not produce improvement.
Elitism: A practice where the top-scoring parent(s) from the previous generation survive unchanged into the next generation. With elitism, the maximum population fitness is guaranteed to never decrease over time. Without elitism, even the best solution found so far could be lost if it is not selected for reproduction or if its children are less fit.

6.2.6 Why Crossover Works: Schema Theory

Genetic algorithms are similar to stochastic beam search, but with the addition of crossover. Why is crossover advantageous? The answer lies in the concept of a schema — a substring in which some positions are left unspecified. For example, the schema 246***** describes all 8-queens states where the first three queens are in rows 2, 4, and 6 (a non-attacking configuration). Strings matching a schema (like 24613578) are called instances of the schema.

If the average fitness of the instances of a schema is above the population mean, the number of instances of that schema will grow over time through selection. Crossover then allows useful schemas (good "building blocks") discovered independently in different individuals to be combined. This is the building block hypothesis: GAs work by discovering and recombining high-fitness building blocks.

However, it can be shown mathematically that if the encoding positions are randomly permuted, crossover conveys no advantage — the schemas must correspond to meaningful substructures in the problem domain. This is why the representation (encoding) choice is critical in GA applications.

Chapter 3: Parent Selection via the Roulette Wheel

6.3.1 Computing Selection Probabilities

Once the fitness of each individual in the population is known, selection probabilities are computed by normalizing fitness values against the total fitness of the population:

\[ P(\text{select } B_i) = \frac{\text{fitness}(B_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \text{fitness}(B_j)} \]

For our working example with 4 board configurations:

BoardConfigurationFitnessProbabilityPercentage
B1[1, 4, 2, 2]44/13 ≈ 0.308~31%
B2[1, 4, 3, 2]44/13 ≈ 0.308~31%
B3[2, 1, 4, 2]22/13 ≈ 0.154~15%
B4[2, 1, 4, 1]33/13 ≈ 0.231~23%
Critical observation: Fitter individuals occupy larger slices of the wheel (31% vs. 15%), giving them a higher probability of being selected. However, this is probabilistic, not deterministic. Even the least fit individual has a non-zero chance of being chosen, preserving diversity and preventing premature convergence to local optima.

6.3.2 The Roulette Wheel Mechanism

The roulette wheel (fitness-proportionate selection) works as follows:

  1. Construct a pie chart where each individual's slice is proportional to its selection probability.
  2. Place a fixed pointer at a position on the wheel.
  3. Spin the wheel — this is a random process. The individual whose slice lands under the pointer is selected as a parent.
  4. Repeat the spin as many times as needed to form the required number of parent pairs.

6.3.3 Forming Couples: Rules and Edge Cases

Suppose the problem specifies that two couples (four parents total) must be formed. Four spins of the roulette wheel are performed. In our example, the results are:

  • Spin 1: B4
  • Spin 2: B1
  • Spin 3: B1
  • Spin 4: B3

These four selected parents can be paired as: Couple 1 = (B4, B1) and Couple 2 = (B1, B3).

Duplicate Handling Rules:
  • Within a couple: The two parents forming a couple must be distinct. A couple like (B1, B1) is invalid because no meaningful crossover can occur between identical parents.
  • Between couples: Two couples must be distinct. Couples (B4, B1) and (B1, B4) are considered the same pairing and should be avoided.
  • If a spin produces a result that violates these constraints, re-spin the wheel until valid couples can be formed.
  • An individual can participate in multiple couples (as B1 does above, partnering with B4 in one couple and B3 in another). In biological terms, this corresponds to an organism having offspring with different mates—perfectly valid in the model.
Implementation note: The number of spins is not predetermined because duplicate spins may force re-rolls. The algorithm spins until enough valid, distinct couples are formed.

Chapter 4: Crossover — Generating the Next Generation

6.4.1 The Biological Analogy

In biological reproduction, offspring inherit a combination of genetic material from both parents. Crossover is the computational realization of this mechanism: segments of the two parent encodings are swapped to create children whose "DNA" is a hybrid of both parental genomes.

The number of children to generate per couple is a parameter specified in the problem statement. In our example, four children total are required across the two couples.

6.4.2 Single-Point Crossover

In single-point crossover:

  1. A random crossover point (cut position) is chosen along the length of the encoding.
  2. All genes before the cut point come from Parent 1.
  3. All genes after the cut point come from Parent 2.
  4. The complementary combination forms the second child.
Example (Couple 1 — single-point crossover after position 1):

Parent 1 (B4): [2], 1, 4, 1
Parent 2 (B1): [1], 4, 2, 2

Child 1: [2] (from B4) + [4, 2, 2] (from B1) = [2, 4, 2, 2]
Child 2: [1] (from B1) + [1, 4, 1] (from B4) = [1, 1, 4, 1]

Single-point crossover always produces two children. To generate more children (e.g., four), apply the crossover at additional positions or repeat the operation.

6.4.3 Multi-Point (Two-Point) Crossover

Two-point crossover uses two cut positions instead of one, dividing each parent into three segments: \(X\) (before cut 1), \(Y\) (between cuts), and \(Z\) (after cut 2).

Example (Couple 2 — two-point crossover):

Parent 1 (B1): [1] | [4] | [2, 2] → X1=1, Y1=4, Z1=2,2
Parent 2 (B3): [1] | [4] | [3, 2] → X2=1, Y2=4, Z2=3,2

Child 3: X1 + Y2 + Z2 = [1, 4, 3, 2]
Child 4: X2 + Y1 + Z1 = [1, 4, 2, 2]

With two-point crossover, the number of possible child combinations is larger than with single-point. The middle segment (\(Y\)) swaps between parents while the outer segments (\(X\) and \(Z\)) remain anchored.

6.4.4 Uniform Crossover

Uniform crossover treats each gene position independently. For each position, a random decision (or a predefined mask) determines whether the child inherits the gene from Parent 1 or Parent 2.

Example with 50% uniform crossover rate:

Parent 1: [1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0]
Parent 2: [0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1]

At each position, with 50% probability, the bit is swapped. The "uniform" nature means every bit has an independent, equal chance of being exchanged—there are no contiguous blocks as in one-point or two-point crossover.

In exam contexts, a percentage (e.g., "perform uniform crossover with 50%") specifies the proportion of bits to swap. This can also be implemented as swapping every alternate bit, swapping the first half and keeping the second half, or any other scheme that achieves the specified swap fraction.

Chapter 5: Mutation — Introducing Genetic Diversity

6.5.1 Why Mutation is Necessary

If crossover alone were used, children would be pure combinations of parental genes. Every gene in every child would trace back to some gene present in at least one parent from the initial population. This limits exploration to the convex hull of the initial gene pool, potentially missing regions of the search space where the optimal solution lies.

Mutation solves this by randomly altering individual genes, introducing genuinely novel genetic material that may not exist in any parent. In biological terms, this corresponds to random DNA mutations that produce traits absent in both parents.

6.5.2 Mutation Procedure

For each child produced by crossover:

  1. Randomly select one gene position (or a small number of positions—typically one or two). The choice of which position to mutate is itself random.
  2. Change the value of that gene to a different valid value from the domain. In the 4-Queens problem, the domain is {1, 2, 3, 4}, so a gene currently holding value 2 can mutate to 1, 3, or 4.
  3. The new value is chosen randomly from the remaining valid options.
Mutation of children from crossover:

C1 = [2, 4, 2, 2] → mutate position 1 (value 2) to 1 → [1, 4, 2, 2]
C2 = [1, 1, 4, 1] → mutate position 3 (value 4) to 3 → [1, 1, 3, 1]
C3 = [1, 4, 3, 2] → mutate position 4 (value 2) to 3 → [1, 4, 3, 3]
C4 = [1, 4, 2, 2] → mutate position 3 (value 2) to 3 → [1, 4, 3, 2]
Mutation rate: It is standard practice to mutate only one gene per child. Mutating all genes would effectively create a completely random individual, breaking the inheritance from parents entirely. One or two gene changes preserve the parental lineage while adding unique traits.

6.5.3 Completing One Iteration

After mutation, the children are complete. The final step of the iteration is to:

  1. Translate each child's vector representation back into its corresponding board configuration.
  2. Recompute fitness values for all children.
  3. Check the stopping criteria. If any child has fitness = 6 (for the 4-Queens problem), the algorithm terminates with a solution.
  4. If not, the children become the new population, and the cycle repeats: compute probabilities, spin the roulette wheel, select parents, perform crossover, apply mutation.

Note that the fitness of children can be worse than that of their parents. Genetic algorithms do not monotonically improve; the population's average fitness may oscillate or even decline across generations. The hope is that, over many generations, the population trends toward higher fitness through the selective pressure of the roulette wheel (which gives fitter individuals a higher chance to reproduce).

Genetic algorithms do not guarantee global optimality. Like all local search methods, GAs are heuristic techniques. They explore probabilistically and can get stuck in local optima. The algorithm may converge to a suboptimal solution or, in some cases, the fitness may degrade. This is an inherent property of the local search paradigm—every state is a solution; the goal is to find a good solution, not necessarily the best one.

6.5.4 Broader Applications of Genetic Algorithms

Beyond the \(n\)-Queens problem, genetic algorithms can be applied to a wide range of optimization tasks:

  • Solving systems of linear equations: Encode candidate values for variables as genes. The fitness function measures how closely the equation is satisfied. Through selection, crossover, and mutation, the population evolves toward values that solve the equation.
  • Scheduling and planning: Represent schedules as chromosomes and evolve toward conflict-free, efficient timetables.
  • Engineering design: Optimize parameters for structural, aerodynamic, or circuit designs.
  • Neural architecture search: Automatically evolve neural network topologies (covered later in this lecture).

Chapter 6: Ant Colony Optimization — Biological Motivation

6.6.1 How Ants Find the Shortest Path

Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) is another nature-inspired algorithm, this time modeled on the foraging behavior of real ant colonies. The phenomenon was studied empirically in the 1970s through controlled experiments with actual ants.

Consider a simplified scenario:

  • A nest containing many ants and a food source are separated by an obstacle.
  • There are two paths around the obstacle: a shorter (upper) path and a longer (lower) path.
  • Ants deposit a chemical hormone called pheromone as they walk. Other ants can detect pheromone concentration and tend to follow paths with higher pheromone density.

6.6.2 The Emergent Process

Here is how the collective intelligence emerges from simple individual behaviors:

  1. Initial exploration: Two ants (A1, A2) leave the nest. Before the decision point, they travel the same path, both depositing pheromones. The pheromone density on this shared segment becomes 2 (two ants contributed).
  2. Decision point: At the fork, with no prior pheromone bias, each ant randomly chooses a path. A1 takes the shorter upper path; A2 takes the longer lower path.
  3. Asymmetric arrival: Because the upper path is shorter, A1 reaches the food source before A2 even completes its journey along the lower path. A1 finds the food and begins its return journey.
  4. Pheromone-based decision: On the return, A1 reaches the decision point again. It compares pheromone densities:
    • Upper path: pheromone density = 1 (from A1's own outbound trail).
    • Lower path: pheromone density = 0 (A2 has not yet reached this point from below).
    A1 chooses the upper path (higher pheromone), reinforcing it further on its return.
  5. Reinforcement: A2 eventually reaches the food, returns, and at the decision point also finds the upper path has higher pheromone density (now further increased by A1's return trip). A2 also chooses the upper path.
  6. Positive feedback loop: Subsequent ants encounter an increasingly biased pheromone landscape. The upper path accumulates more pheromone with each traversal, attracting more ants, which deposit even more pheromone.
  7. Evaporation: Meanwhile, pheromones on the unused lower path slowly evaporate, tending toward zero. The colony converges on the shorter path.
This is not infallible. If the ant on the shorter path is slow (injured, hesitant) and the ant on the longer path is fast, the longer path could accumulate more pheromone first, causing the entire colony to converge on the suboptimal route. ACO, like GA, does not guarantee global optimality.

6.6.3 From Biology to Algorithm

ACO encodes this behavior into a computational procedure for solving combinatorial optimization problems, most famously the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP). In the TSP:

  • A complete graph of \(N\) cities is given.
  • The goal is to find the shortest Hamiltonian cycle: a tour that visits every city exactly once and returns to the starting city.
  • Each ant constructs a complete tour by iteratively choosing the next city based on a probabilistic rule that balances pheromone intensity against distance.

6.7.2 Transition Probability Formula

When an ant is at city \(i\), the probability of choosing city \(j\) as the next destination is:

\[ P_{ij} = \frac{ [\tau_{ij}]^{\alpha} \cdot [\eta_{ij}]^{\beta} }{ \sum_{h \notin \text{visited}} [\tau_{ih}]^{\alpha} \cdot [\eta_{ih}]^{\beta} } \]

where the summation in the denominator runs over all unvisited cities \(h\). This ensures ants do not revisit cities they have already been to.

Intuition: The numerator is a weighted product of "how much pheromone is on this edge" (raised to \(\alpha\)) and "how short this edge is" (raised to \(\beta\)). The denominator normalizes across all feasible next cities so that the result is a valid probability. The edge with the highest \(P_{ij}\) is chosen as the next move.

6.7.3 Pheromone Update Formula

After each ant completes a move (or a full tour), the pheromone matrix is globally updated for all edges:

\[ \tau_{ij}^{\text{new}} = (1 - \rho) \cdot \tau_{ij}^{\text{old}} + \Delta\tau_{ij}^{k} \]

where:

\[ \Delta\tau_{ij}^{k} = \begin{cases} \frac{Q}{L_k}, & \text{if ant } k \text{ traversed edge } (i, j) \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]
Two simultaneous mechanisms at work:
  1. Evaporation: The term \((1 - \rho) \cdot \tau_{ij}^{\text{old}}\) reduces pheromone on every edge. This prevents unbounded accumulation and allows the colony to "forget" poor paths.
  2. Reinforcement: The term \(\Delta\tau_{ij}^{k}\) adds pheromone only to edges that were actually traversed. The amount is inversely proportional to the tour length \(L_k\)—shorter tours deposit more pheromone per edge.

Chapter 9: Neural Architecture Search (NAS)

6.9.1 The Problem with Manual Neural Network Design

Designing deep neural network architectures has traditionally been a labor-intensive process relying on human expertise, intuition, and extensive trial-and-error. The design space is vast—choices include the number of layers, layer types (convolutional, recurrent, fully-connected, attention), connections between layers (sequential, skip, dense), hyperparameters (kernel sizes, strides, activation functions), and training configurations. Exhaustively exploring this space manually is infeasible. NAS is the process of automating architecture engineering and can be seen as a subfield of AutoML, with significant overlap with hyperparameter optimization and meta-learning.

6.9.2 The Three Dimensions of NAS

All NAS methods can be categorized according to three dimensions:

  1. Search Space: Defines which architectures can be represented in principle. Incorporating prior knowledge reduces the search space size but introduces human bias that may prevent discovering novel building blocks.
  2. Search Strategy: Details how to explore the (often exponentially large or unbounded) search space. Encompasses the exploration-exploitation trade-off.
  3. Performance Estimation Strategy: Evaluates how good a candidate architecture is. The simplest option — full training and validation — is computationally expensive (thousands of GPU days). Much recent research focuses on speeding this up.

6.9.3 Types of Search Spaces

Chain-structured networks: The simplest search space. A neural network is a sequence of n layers \(A = L_n \circ \dots \circ L_1 \circ L_0\) where each layer receives input only from the previous layer. Parameterized by: (i) number of layers n, (ii) the type of each layer (convolution, pooling, fully-connected), and (iii) hyperparameters per layer (number of filters, kernel size, strides). This is a conditional space because hyperparameters depend on the layer type chosen.

Multi-branch networks: Incorporate modern design elements like skip connections, allowing layers to receive input from multiple previous layers via a combining function \(g_i\). Special cases include Residual Networks (ResNet), where previous outputs are summed, and DenseNets, where they are concatenated. These offer significantly more degrees of freedom.

Cell-based search spaces: Instead of searching for whole architectures, search for reusable cells (or blocks) that are repeated to build the full network. For example, a normal cell preserves input dimensionality and a reduction cell reduces spatial dimension. The final architecture stacks these cells in a predefined pattern. This approach has three advantages:

  • Drastically reduced search space (cells have fewer layers than full architectures; estimated 7× speed-up).
  • High transferability — cells optimized on CIFAR-10 can be transferred to ImageNet and achieve state-of-the-art performance.
  • Aligns with the proven design principle of repeating building blocks (like LSTM blocks in RNNs or residual blocks).

The macro-architecture (how many cells and how they connect) and micro-architecture (the internal structure of each cell) ideally should be optimized jointly.

6.9.4 Search Strategies

Reinforcement Learning (RL): The generation of a neural architecture is treated as the agent's action, with the action space equal to the search space. The reward is based on validation performance. Approaches include:
  • Using an RNN controller to sequentially sample architecture strings, trained via REINFORCE policy gradient or Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Used by Zoph & Le (2017) with 800 GPUs to achieve competitive CIFAR-10 results.
  • Q-learning: Train a policy that sequentially chooses layer types and hyperparameters.
  • Network morphism-based RL: Start from a trained network and sequentially apply function-preserving mutations that add capacity, with the state being the current partially-trained architecture.
Neuro-evolutionary methods: Use evolutionary algorithms to optimize neural architectures. Modern approaches use gradient descent for weight training and evolution only for architecture. Key operations:
  • Mutations: Add/remove layers, alter layer hyperparameters, add skip connections, modify training hyperparameters.
  • Selection: Tournament selection, or sampling from a multi-objective Pareto front using inverse density.
  • Population management: Remove worst (Real et al. 2017), remove oldest to decrease greediness (Real et al. 2019), or keep all (Liu et al. 2018b).
  • Lamarckian inheritance: Child networks inherit learned weights from parents via network morphisms, rather than training from scratch. This speeds up evaluation but can only make architectures larger.
Comparison: RL and evolution perform equally well in final test accuracy, with evolution having better anytime performance and finding smaller models. Both consistently outperform random search, though the margin is often modest (e.g., ~2% test error difference on non-augmented CIFAR-10). NAS became mainstream after Zoph & Le (2017) showed competitive CIFAR-10 and Penn Treebank results with RL.
Gradient-based optimization (DARTS): Instead of discrete search, use a continuous relaxation: instead of picking a single operation at each layer, compute a convex combination of candidate operations \(y = \sum_i \alpha_i o_i(x)\) where \(\alpha_i \ge 0\) and \(\sum \alpha_i = 1\). Both network weights and architectural parameters (α) are optimized by alternating gradient descent on training and validation data. A discrete architecture is obtained by choosing arg max over α for each layer. This dramatically reduces search cost to a few GPU days.
Other strategies:
  • Bayesian optimization: Using Gaussian processes or tree-based models (tree Parzen estimators, random forests) to effectively search high-dimensional conditional spaces. Historically the first successful approach, achieving state-of-the-art vision architectures in 2013.
  • Monte Carlo Tree Search: Exploits the tree structure of architecture search spaces.
  • Hill climbing: Greedily moves toward better architectures via network morphisms, surprisingly effective without sophisticated exploration.

6.9.5 Evolutionary NAS in Practice: NEAT, DeepNEAT, and CoDeepNEAT

NEAT (NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies) is a foundational algorithm that evolves both weights and topology. It starts with simple networks and complexifies them by adding nodes and connections through mutation. NEAT's key innovation is historical markings — innovation numbers assigned to genes that enable meaningful crossover between networks of different topologies by aligning genes that share evolutionary origins. The population is divided into species (subpopulations) based on a similarity metric; each species grows proportionally to its fitness.

DeepNEAT extends NEAT to deep networks. Each node in the chromosome represents a layer (not a neuron), containing a table of hyperparameters (layer type, number of neurons, kernel size, activation function). Edges indicate how layers connect. The chromosome also contains global hyperparameters (learning rate, training algorithm, data preprocessing). When layers have multiple parent layers, a merge layer (concatenation or element-wise sum) adjusts dimensions; if sizes mismatch, downsampling via max-pooling or bottleneck layers is inserted adaptively.

CoDeepNEAT co-evolves two populations: modules (small DNN graphs representing reusable building blocks) and blueprints (graphs where each node points to a module species). During fitness evaluation, blueprints and modules are assembled: each blueprint node is replaced with a module from the species it points to. If multiple blueprint nodes point to the same species, the same module is reused — creating the repetitive modular structure seen in ResNet and GoogLeNet. Fitness of the assembled network is attributed back to both the blueprint and the modules as the average fitness across all assemblies containing them.

CoDeepNEAT Results:
  • CIFAR-10: 7.3% test error, comparable to Bayesian optimization baseline (6.4%). The evolved network trains much faster — 12 epochs to 20% error vs. 30+ epochs for baseline. The population used 25 blueprints and 45 modules, assembling 100 CNNs per generation over 72 generations.
  • Language Modeling (Penn Treebank): 5% improvement over vanilla LSTM (perplexity 78). Discovered a feedback skip connection between memory cells of two LSTM layers, similar to hand-designed architectures. Used 50 networks per generation over 25 generations with two LSTM-specific mutations.
  • Image Captioning: Evolved architectures outperform the "Show and Tell" baseline. The best architecture included six LSTM layers, four summing merge layers, and several skip connections — motifs similar to residual architectures. Converged 6× faster than baseline during training, demonstrating that limited training during evolution creates pressure toward fast-converging architectures.

6.9.6 Performance Estimation Strategies

Training each candidate architecture from scratch would require thousands of GPU days. Researchers have developed several speed-up methods:

Lower fidelity estimates: Reduce training cost by training for fewer epochs, on a data subset, on lower-resolution images, or with fewer filters/cells. This introduces bias (performance is underestimated), but may be acceptable if the relative ranking of architectures remains stable. Recent results show that relative ranking can change dramatically when the fidelity gap is too large, arguing for gradual increase in fidelities.
Learning curve extrapolation: Train briefly, then predict final performance based on the initial learning curve using Bayesian models. Architectures predicted to perform poorly are terminated early. Can also predict performance based on architectural properties and extrapolate to larger architectures than those seen during training. The main challenge: making good predictions in a large search space based on relatively few evaluations.
Weight inheritance / Network morphisms: Instead of training from scratch, initialize new architectures by inheriting weights from trained parent networks. Network morphisms modify an architecture while leaving the function represented by the network unchanged, allowing capacity increase without performance loss. Lamarckian inheritance passes learned weights from parent to child. This reduces search cost to a few GPU days. A limitation: strict network morphisms only make architectures larger, which can lead to overly complex networks. Approximate network morphisms allow shrinking, mitigating this issue.
One-shot models / Weight sharing: Treat all architectures as subgraphs of a single supergraph (the one-shot model). Train the one-shot model once, then evaluate architectures by inheriting their weights from it — no separate training needed. Examples:
  • ENAS (Pham et al., 2018): RNN controller samples architectures; one-shot model trained via REINFORCE approximate gradients.
  • DARTS (Liu et al., 2019): Continuous relaxation with mixture of candidate operations on each edge; all weights optimized jointly via alternating gradient descent.
  • SNAS (Xie et al., 2019): Optimizes a distribution over operations using the concrete distribution and reparameterization trick for differentiability.
  • ProxylessNAS (Cai et al., 2019): "Binarizes" architectural weights, masking out all but one edge per operation, enabling GPU-memory-efficient search.
A key risk: the one-shot model introduces bias. If the sampling distribution is optimized during training, weights may become better adapted to certain architectures, reinforcing the bias toward those parts of the search space (premature convergence). Currently, it is not fully understood under what conditions one-shot performance correlates well with true performance.

6.9.7 Why NAS Matters and Future Directions

NAS offers several advantages over manual design:

  • It can discover novel architectures free from human bias toward familiar patterns.
  • Population-based search explores the architecture space more broadly.
  • It naturally handles multi-objective optimization (e.g., maximizing accuracy while minimizing model size or latency) — closely related to network compression.
  • When training is limited during search, evolution creates pressure toward fast-converging architectures that are efficient to fine-tune.
  • Discovered architectures often reveal design principles (e.g., skip connections with summation merges resembling residual networks) that inform future manual design.

Current frontiers in NAS include: extending beyond image classification to semantic segmentation, machine translation, reinforcement learning, and generative models; developing more general and flexible search spaces not tied to specific domain knowledge; defining common benchmarks for fair comparison (e.g., NAS-Bench-101 with 423K pre-evaluated architectures); and understanding why specific discovered architectures work well to extract generalizable design principles.

Computational context: NAS is extremely demanding — the CoDeepNEAT experiments used ~100 GPUs per generation. With distributed AI computing systems now reaching petaflop-scale (e.g., Sentient's DarkCycle with 2M CPUs and 5000 GPUs), evolutionary NAS can leverage massive parallelism since each individual can be trained independently. The search space can be extended to more components, topologies, and hyperparameters as more compute becomes available, making evolutionary optimization of neural networks a promising approach for the future.
