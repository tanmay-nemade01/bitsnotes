2.2 Definitions of Artificial Intelligence and Modern Consensus

Hook: What separates true artificial intelligence from routine automated computation? Is an AI system successful when it mimics human behavior—complete with human flaws—or when it makes mathematically optimal decisions?

Intuition & Analogy: Think of artificial intelligence like aviation. Early pioneers attempted to build flying machines by mimicking bird wings (flapping feathered wings). Modern aeronautical engineering succeeded not by copying biological birds, but by mastering aerodynamic principles (lift, drag, thrust). Similarly, modern AI focuses on mastering principles of rational action rather than duplicating human biological thought patterns.

2.2.1 The Four Quadrants of AI Definitions

Historically, definitions of Artificial Intelligence are categorized across two dimensions: human performance versus rationality, and internal thought processes versus external behavior.

Combining these axes yields the classic Four Quadrants of AI Definitions (Russell & Norvig, Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach (4th Edition)):

Dimension Human-Centric Standard Rationality-Centric Standard
Thought Processes & Reasoning Thinking Humanly
Cognitive modeling, psychological introspection, and neural simulation.		 Thinking Rationally
Formal logic, syllogisms, and mathematical laws of thought.
External Actions & Behavior Acting Humanly
Turing Test benchmark, behavioral naturalness, and conversational empathy.		 Acting Rationally
Rational agents, expected utility maximization, and goal-directed action.

Detailed Breakdown of the Four Quadrants:

  1. Thinking Humanly (Cognitive Modeling):
  • Focuses on determining how humans think by inspecting internal neural and psychological mechanics (via cognitive science, brain imaging, and psychological introspection).
  • A system in this quadrant must not only output correct decisions, but also match the step-by-step cognitive timing and error patterns of human subjects (e.g., Newell & Simon's General Problem Solver - GPS).
  • Example: A system estimating an answer around 50 because it recalls a similar problem from yesterday, relying on heuristic memory retrieval rather than systematic computation.
  1. Thinking Rationally (Laws of Thought):
  • Grounded in formal logic, mathematical deduction, and Aristotelian syllogisms ("laws of thought").
  • Knowledge is represented as logical assertions, and conclusions are derived through step-by-step sound inference rules.
  • Example: Deductive reasoning stating: "All assignments due tomorrow must be completed today. The AI assignment is due tomorrow. Therefore, the AI assignment must be completed today."
  • Limitation: Hard to express informal or uncertain knowledge in strict logic, and computing formal logical deductions can be computationally intractable in large state spaces.
  1. Acting Humanly (The Operational Turing Test):
  • Defined by Alan Turing (1950) in Computing Machinery and Intelligence. The objective is to produce outward behavior indistinguishable from a human being when interrogated by a human judge over a text interface.
  • To pass the Turing Test, a system requires: Natural Language Processing, Knowledge Representation, Automated Reasoning, and Machine Learning. The Total Turing Test adds Computer Vision and Robotics for physical interaction.
  • Example: A customer support bot responding to user distress by stating: "That sounds frustrating, let me help you resolve this right away."
  1. Acting Rationally (The Rational Agent Approach):
  • Focuses on constructing rational agents that act to achieve the best outcome or, under uncertainty, the best expected outcome.
  • Operates by receiving environmental percepts, deliberating over available actions, and executing decisions that maximize a performance measure.

Pitfall — Anthropomorphism in AI Design:

Anthropomorphism is the psychological tendency of humans to attribute human thoughts, feelings, emotions, and intentions to non-human entities.

  • In modern AI interface design, conversational agents tuned with empathetic phrasing can lead users to treat software systems as emotional companions or informal therapists.
  • Engineers must remember that conversational naturalness does not imply internal awareness, emotional understanding, or factual reliability.

2.2.2 The Modern Consensus: Rational Action

The contemporary academic and industrial consensus overwhelmingly prioritizes Acting Rationally over the other three quadrants:

  • Human performance is not always ideal: humans make emotional errors, suffer from cognitive fatigue, succumb to memory biases, and make irrational choices.
  • Standard engineering goals require systems that consistently optimize objective performance measures rather than replicating human flaws.
  • Rational action encompasses logical deduction (Thinking Rationally) as one mechanism, but extends to handling decisions under uncertainty where strict logic cannot yield a definitive proof.

Real-World Application — Autonomous Route Navigation:

Modern navigation systems (such as Google Maps or ride-share routing engines) embody rational action. When asked to route a vehicle across a crowded city:

  1. The system ingests real-time sensor streams (GPS points, traffic speed data, road closures).
  2. It formulates a graph search problem minimizing travel duration and fuel consumption.
  3. It selects and executes optimal routes independently of human stress or emotional state.

2.3 Rational Agents and the PEAS Framework

Hook: How does an autonomous system translate sensory signals into effective real-world decisions? What makes an agent "rational" rather than merely lucky or omniscient?

2.3.1 Definition of an Agent

An agent is an entity that perceives its environment through sensors and acts upon that environment through actuators.

Sensory-Actuator Interaction Loop
+-------------------------------------------------------+
|                                                       |
|   +-----------+      Percepts      +--------------+   |
|   |           |<-------------------|              |   |
|   |   Agent   |                    | Environment  |   |
|   |           |------------------->|              |   |
|   +-----------+      Actions       +--------------+   |
|                                                       |
+-------------------------------------------------------+

  • A percept refers to the agent's perceptual inputs at any given instant.
  • A percept sequence is the complete history of everything the agent has ever perceived from initial startup up to the current time step.

2.3.2 Agent Taxonomy across Physical and Digital Domains

Agents exist across physical, digital, and human domains:

Agent Type Sensors Actuators Primary Operating Medium
Human Agent Eyes, ears, skin, nose, taste buds Hands, legs, vocal cords, muscles Physical world & social environments
Robotic Agent Cameras, LIDAR, sonar, infrared sensors, joint encoders Electric motors, hydraulic arms, wheels, grippers Industrial assembly, physical navigation
Software Agent Keyboard buffers, file streams, network packets, API calls Display monitors, file outputs, HTTP requests, code execution Digital file systems, databases, cloud APIs

2.3.3 Distinguishing AI Agents from Agentic AI

While both paradigms leverage foundation models and automated decision logic, they differ fundamentally in structural autonomy and multi-agent coordination:

Comparison: Single AI Agent vs. Agentic AI Networks

  • AI Agent: A single autonomous entity operating via predefined prompts, learned heuristics, or single model scripts. It accepts an input, processes the query, and emits a response within bounded parameters (e.g., a standalone customer support chatbot).
  • Agentic AI: A multi-agent ecosystem featuring high operational autonomy, dynamic tool selection, persistent state memory, and inter-agent communication (e.g., via Model Context Protocol / MCP). Agentic systems dynamically break complex user objectives into sub-tasks, delegate work to specialized sub-agents, execute multi-step background workflows, and self-correct based on intermediate feedback.

2.3.4 Student Questions and Answers on Agent Autonomy

Q: Is the main difference between an AI agent and agentic AI the reasoning component?

A: Reasoning exists in both paradigms, but the crucial distinction lies in the level of autonomy, delegation, and orchestration. An AI agent operates with bounded autonomy using a single script or model to generate answers, whereas Agentic AI consists of an orchestrated network of agents dynamically calling external tools, delegating tasks to sub-agents, managing long-term memory, and executing complex workflows without continuous human intervention.

2.3.5 Agent Functions and Agent Programs

Mathematical Formulation of Agent Behavior:

Symbol registry:

  • \(P\): Set of all possible single percepts.
  • \(P^*\): Set of all possible percept sequences (histories) of length \(t \ge 1\).
  • \(A\): Set of all legal actions available to the agent.
  • \(f\): Abstract agent function mapping percept histories to legal actions.

The agent function \(f\) is an abstract mathematical mapping from any given percept sequence history to an executable action:

\[ f: P^* \to A \]

The agent program is the concrete algorithm or code implementation running on a physical computing architecture:

\[ \text{Agent} = \text{Architecture} + \text{Program} \]

Why Lookup Tables are Infeasible (Mathematical Proof)

Consider a simple agent operating over a discrete time horizon of \(T\) steps. At each step, it receives one of \(|P|\) possible percepts and can execute one of \(|A|\) possible actions.

  1. The number of possible percept sequences of length \(T\) is \(|P|^T\).
  2. The cumulative number of percept sequences of length up to \(T\) is:

\[ |P^*| = \sum_{t=1}^T |P|^t = \frac{|P|(|P|^T - 1)}{|P| - 1} \]

  1. For an automated taxi driving for just 1 hour with a camera generating 30 frames per second (\(T = 108,000\)), \(|P^*|\) is astronomically larger than the number of atoms in the observable universe.
  2. Storing \(f\) as a explicit lookup table would require infinite memory, take infinite time to construct, and fail to generalize to novel percept sequences. Thus, the central challenge of AI is writing compact agent programs that compute \(f\) efficiently.

Worked Example 1 — Vacuum-Cleaner World Tabular Agent Function:

Consider a simplified 2-room vacuum cleaner world with rooms \(A\) and \(B\).

  • Percepts: \(\langle \text{Location}, \text{Status} \rangle\), e.g., \(\langle A, \text{Clean} \rangle\) or \(\langle A, \text{Dirty} \rangle\).
  • Actions: \(\{\text{Right}, \text{Left}, \text{Suck}, \text{NoOp}\}\).

Partial Agent Function Lookup Table:

Current Percept Sequence History (\(P^*\)) Executed Action (\(A\)) Rationale / State Logic
\([\langle A, \text{Clean} \rangle]\) \(\text{Right}\) Room A clean; move to inspect Room B.
\([\langle A, \text{Dirty} \rangle]\) \(\text{Suck}\) Room A dirty; clean current room.
\([\langle B, \text{Clean} \rangle]\) \(\text{Left}\) Room B clean; return to inspect Room A.
\([\langle B, \text{Dirty} \rangle]\) \(\text{Suck}\) Room B dirty; clean current room.
\([\langle A, \text{Clean} \rangle, \langle B, \text{Dirty} \rangle]\) \(\text{Suck}\) Arrived at B after cleaning A; clean B.
\([\langle A, \text{Clean} \rangle, \langle B, \text{Clean} \rangle]\) \(\text{Left}\) Both rooms clean; continue monitoring sweep.

2.3.6 Rationality vs. Omniscience

A rational agent is one that operates to select an action expected to maximize its performance measure, based on the evidence provided by the percept sequence so far and whatever built-in knowledge the agent possesses.

Scope & Distinctions:

  • Rationality \(\neq\) Omniscience: Omniscience requires knowing the actual outcome of actions in advance, which is impossible in uncertain real-world environments. Rationality maximizes expected performance based on available information.
  • Rationality \(\neq\) Perfection: Rationality rewards best-possible choices given current data, even if unexpected external events cause a bad outcome.
  • Information Gathering & Exploration: A rational agent must undertake exploration and information-gathering actions (e.g., turning on headlights at night, running diagnostic pings) to gather missing percepts before committing to critical decisions.

2.3.7 The PEAS Specification Framework

Designing any rational agent requires explicitly defining four critical components using the PEAS framework:

  • P: Performance Measure — The quantitative metric defining success.
  • E: Environment — The external domain context in which the agent operates.
  • A: Actuators — The physical or software mechanisms through which the agent modifies the environment.
  • S: Sensors — The instruments or input channels through which the agent receives environmental data.

  • Environment: Urban streets, highways, varying weather, traffic signals, pedestrians, erratic surrounding vehicles.
  • Actuators: Steering wheel, electronic throttle/accelerator, hydraulic brakes, turn signals, horn, cabin display.
  • Sensors: Multi-camera array, LIDAR scanners, RADAR units, GPS receiver, wheel speed encoders, IMU accelerometer.

2. Medical Diagnostic System

  • Performance Measure: Patient recovery rate, diagnostic accuracy, treatment cost minimization, risk reduction.
  • Environment: Hospital ward, patient history databases, clinical pathology lab streams.
  • Actuators: Screen display presenting diagnostic reports, ordered lab tests, recommended drug dosages.
  • Sensors: Touchscreen/keyboard interface for symptom entry, lab telemetry feeds, electronic health record (EHR) APIs.

3. Part-Picking Industrial Conveyor Robot

  • Performance Measure: Percentage of components correctly binned, sorting speed (parts/min), mechanical uptime, energy efficiency.
  • Environment: High-speed industrial conveyor belt, unsorted manufactured parts, sorting bins, factory lighting.
  • Actuators: Articulated 6-axis robotic arm, pneumatic vacuum gripper, conveyor belt velocity control.

2.4 Taxonomy of Environment Properties

Hook: Why is designing a chess-playing bot fundamentally easier than building an autonomous driving agent, even though chess grandmasters consider chess a supreme test of intellect? The answer lies in the properties of the environment.

Environments dictate the complexity of the agent program. They are classified across six core dimensions:

Taxonomy of Environment Dimensions
+-----------------------+-------------------------------------------------------+
| Dimension             | Spectrum / Classifications                            |
+-----------------------+-------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. Observability      | Fully Observable  <--->  Partially Observable / Blind |
| 2. Determinism        | Deterministic     <--->  Stochastic / Strategic       |
| 3. Episodic Structure | Episodic          <--->  Sequential                   |
| 4. Temporal Dynamics  | Static            <--->  Semi-Dynamic  <---> Dynamic  |
| 5. State Continuity   | Discrete          <--->  Continuous                   |
| 6. Agent Count        | Single-Agent      <--->  Multi-Agent                  |
+-----------------------+-------------------------------------------------------+


Symbol registry — Environment Taxonomy
- s      — environment state                — \(s \in S\)      — current world state
- a      — executed action                  — \(a \in A\)      — action selected by agent
- s'     — resulting environment state      — \(s'\)           — state after action execution
- Result — state transition function        — \(\text{Result}(s,a)\) — deterministic transition mapping

2.4.1 Observability: Fully Observable vs. Partially Observable vs. Unobservable

  • Fully Observable: An agent's sensors give it access to the complete, exact state of the environment at each point in time. The agent needs no internal memory or state tracking to know what is happening elsewhere.
  • Examples: Chess, Tic-Tac-Toe, 8-puzzle.
  • Partially Observable: The environment state is incomplete due to noisy sensors, physical obstructions, range limits, or unobserved hidden variables. The agent must maintain an internal state representation to track unobserved aspects.
  • Examples: Autonomous driving (blind spots, weather interference), Poker (opponent cards hidden), Medical diagnosis.
  • Unobservable (Sensorless / Blind): The agent has no sensory input whatsoever (due to sensor hardware failure or operating in total darkness without active rangefinders). The agent must compute belief states purely from action sequences.

2.4.2 Student Questions and Answers on Environment Observability

Q: Why is autonomous driving categorized as partially observable rather than fully observable?

A: Autonomous driving is partially observable because physical sensors (cameras, LIDAR, RADAR) have local range limits and line-of-sight constraints. Buildings and large trucks block view of intersections, heavy rain or fog degrades camera perception, and traffic dynamics miles down the road or around blind turns remain entirely unobserved.

2.4.3 Determinism: Deterministic vs. Stochastic

  • Deterministic: The next state of the environment is completely determined by the current state and the action executed by the agent. If the agent executes action \(a\) in state \(s\), the outcome \(s' = \text{Result}(s, a)\) is guaranteed.
  • Examples: Chess, 8-puzzle, standard calculator operations.
  • Stochastic: The next state involves uncertainty due to random environmental elements or unobserved dynamics. The same action in the same state can lead to variable outcomes according to a probability distribution.
  • Examples: Weather forecasting, autonomous driving, rolling dice in Backgammon.
  • Strategic / Non-deterministic: In multi-agent games, if uncertainty arises solely from the actions of other competing agents, the environment is called strategic rather than purely stochastic.

2.4.4 Episodic Structure: Episodic vs. Sequential

  • Episodic: The agent's experience is divided into independent, atomic episodes. In each episode, the agent receives a percept, executes a single action, and receives feedback. Crucially, the action taken in one episode does not affect future episodes.
  • Examples: Automated defect inspection on an assembly line, spam email classification, image recognition.
  • Sequential: Current decisions directly alter future environment states, available actions, and long-term utility. The agent must plan ahead and consider future consequences.
  • Examples: Chess, autonomous taxi driving, maze navigation.

2.4.5 Temporal Dynamics: Static vs. Semi-Dynamic vs. Dynamic

  • Static: The environment does not change while the agent is deliberating or computing its next action. Time spent thinking carries no penalty.
  • Examples: Offline Sudoku solver, turn-based crosswords without clocks.
  • Semi-Dynamic: The environment state itself does not change while the agent deliberates, but the agent's performance score decreases as time passes.
  • Examples: Timed chess (board positions do not move during your turn, but the clock ticks down).
  • Dynamic: The environment continuously changes while the agent is deliberating. If the agent hesitates, the state changes automatically.
  • Examples: Autonomous taxi driving (traffic keeps moving while the planner runs), real-time robot navigation.

Teaching Moment — Dynamic Deliberation Trap:

In a dynamic environment, deliberation time is an active cost. If a self-driving vehicle takes 3 seconds to compute an optimal evasive maneuver at 60 mph, the vehicle travels over 260 feet before executing the action. Real-time dynamic agents must balance deliberation quality with execution latency.

2.4.6 State Continuity: Discrete vs. Continuous

  • Discrete: The state space, time steps, percepts, and action sets are divided into a finite or countable number of distinct values.
  • Examples: Chess (64 discrete board squares, discrete turns), 8-puzzle.
  • Continuous: State variables, time, or action parameters vary continuously over real numbers.
  • Examples: Autonomous vehicle steering angle (\(\theta \in [-\pi, \pi]\)), velocity controls, chemical plant temperature regulation.

2.4.7 Agent Count: Single-Agent vs. Multi-Agent

  • Single-Agent: An agent operating by itself in an environment where no other decision-making entities exist.
  • Examples: Solitaire card game, crossword puzzle, 8-puzzle.
  • Multi-Agent: The environment contains other agents whose actions affect the performance measure of the main agent. Multi-agent environments can be competitive (zero-sum, like Chess) or cooperative (like multi-vehicle traffic communication).

2.5 Five Fundamental Agent Architectures

Hook: How do we construct software architectures capable of handling environments ranging from simple vacuum rooms to complex autonomous driving? Agent programs fall into five primary structural categories based on internal complexity and decision-making logic.

2.5.1 Structural Overview of Agent Types

Agent programs fall into five primary structural categories based on internal complexity and decision-making logic:

Architectural Evolution of Agent Programs

1. Simple Reflex Agent:
Percept ---> [Sensors] ---> [Condition-Action Rules] ---> [Actuators] ---> Action

2. Model-Based Reflex Agent:
Percept ---> [Sensors] ---> [Internal State + World Model] ---> [Rules] ---> [Actuators] ---> Action

3. Goal-Based Agent:
Percept ---> [Sensors] ---> [State] ---> [Search / Planning toward Goal] ---> [Actuators] ---> Action

4. Utility-Based Agent:
Percept ---> [Sensors] ---> [State] ---> [Utility Function Maximization U(s)] ---> [Actuators] ---> Action

5. Learning Agent:
Percept ---> [Sensors] ---> [Performance Element] ---> Action
                               ^        |
                               | feedback
                            [Learning Element] <--- [Critic]
                               |
                        [Problem Generator] (Exploration)


Symbol registry — Agent Architectures
- s      — environment state                — \(s \in S\)      — world state representation
- s'     — outcome environment state        — \(s'\)           — state resulting from action
- a      — executable action                — \(a \in A\)      — candidate action
- o      — sensory observation / percept    — \(o \in P\)      — current observation
- U(s)   — utility function                 — \(U(s) \in \mathbb{R}\) — numerical measure of state desirability

2.5.2 Simple Reflex and Model-Based Agents

1. Simple Reflex Agent:

  • Mechanism: Selects actions based solely on the current percept, ignoring all prior percept history.
  • Decision Logic: Operates via explicit condition-action rules (e.g., `IF car-in-front-is-braking THEN initiate-braking`).
  • Limitation: Can only operate successfully in fully observable environments. In partially observable environments, simple reflex agents frequently enter infinite loops because they cannot disambiguate states that generate identical current percepts.

2. Model-Based Reflex Agent:

  • Mechanism: Maintains an internal state representation to track unobserved aspects of the current world state.
  • Core Components:
  1. Transition Model: Knowledge of how the world evolves independently of the agent, and how the agent's own actions affect the world.
  2. Sensor Model: Knowledge of how the world state maps to incoming sensory percepts.
  • Decision Logic: Updates internal state using incoming percepts plus previous action, then applies condition-action rules. Handles partially observable environments.

2.5.3 Goal-Based, Utility-Based, and Learning Agents

3. Goal-Based Agent:

  • Mechanism: Combines internal state tracking with explicit goal descriptions (desirable target states).
  • Decision Logic: Evaluates how candidate action sequences alter the state and whether resulting states achieve the goal. Employs search algorithms and planning workflows.
  • Flexibility: Highly adaptable. If the target destination changes, a goal-based agent simply updates its goal specification and re-plans without rewriting underlying condition-action rules.

4. Utility-Based Agent:

  • Mechanism: Uses a mathematical utility function \(U(s) \in \mathbb{R}\) that maps an environment state \(s\) to a real number measuring the degree of "desirability" or "happiness" of that state.
  • Decision Logic: Evaluates action sequences under uncertainty by computing Expected Utility:

\[ \text{Expected Utility}(a \mid o) = \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid o, a) \cdot U(s') \]

Selects the action \(a^*\) that maximizes expected utility.

  • Advantage: Handles trade-offs between competing goals (e.g., balancing travel speed vs. fuel consumption vs. passenger safety) and makes rational choices when no action guarantees 100% success.

5. Learning Agent:

  • Mechanism: Conceptual division of agent architecture into four distinct functional modules to enable continuous performance improvement:
  1. Learning Element: Responsible for making improvements to the agent program over time based on performance feedback.
  2. Performance Element: The operational core responsible for selecting external actions based on percepts (can be any of the four previous agent types).
  3. Critic: Evaluates the agent's behavior against a fixed external performance standard and provides feedback signals to the learning element.
  4. Problem Generator: Suggests exploratory actions that lead to new experiences rather than exploiting current known paths.

2.5.4 Architectural Comparison Matrix

Comparison of the 5 Agent Architectures:

Architecture Type Internal State Representation Suitable Environment Decision Logic Basis Core Advantage Main Limitation
Simple Reflex None (Current percept only) Fully Observable Condition-Action Rules (`IF-THEN`) Extremely fast execution Fails in partial observability; loops
Model-Based World state + transition model Partially Observable State update + Condition-Action Rules Handles unobserved variables Hard to hand-craft full world models
Goal-Based State + Goal specifications Partially / Fully Observable Search & Planning toward goals Flexible when goals change Slower due to search computation

2.6 Problem-Solving Agents and Search Problem Formulation

Hook: When an immediate action is insufficient to achieve an objective, how can an agent plan a sequence of steps toward a goal? Problem-Solving Agents transform real-world tasks into structured graph search problems to find optimal paths.

2.6.1 The Problem-Solving Agent (PSA) Workflow

A Problem-Solving Agent is a goal-based agent that decides what to do by finding sequences of actions that lead to desirable states in deterministic, fully observable environments.

Problem-Solving Agent Operational Workflow
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. Formulate Goal     --->  Set target objective state descriptions             |
| 2. Formulate Problem  --->  Define states, actions, transitions, costs, goals   |
| 3. Search Algorithm   --->  Find optimal action sequence in state graph         |
| 4. Execution Phase    --->  Execute actions sequentially in real environment    |
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

Problem-Solving Agent Pseudocode

function SIMPLE-PROBLEM-SOLVING-AGENT(percept) returns an action
  persistent: state, an environment state description
              seq, an action sequence, initially empty
              goal, a goal state description
              problem, a search problem formulation

  state <- UPDATE-STATE(state, percept)
  if seq is empty then
    goal <- FORMULATE-GOAL(state)
    problem <- FORMULATE-PROBLEM(state, goal)
    seq <- SEARCH(problem)
    if seq = failure then return a null action
  end if
  action <- FIRST(seq)
  seq <- REST(seq)
  return action

Assumptions & Scope of Problem-Solving Agents:

  1. Atomic States: States are treated as black boxes with no internal structure during high-level graph search.
  2. Deterministic & Static: The environment does not change during deliberation, and actions produce guaranteed outcomes.
  3. Open-Loop Execution: The agent computes the full action sequence `seq` during the offline search phase, then executes the sequence without monitoring sensors between steps (open-loop execution).

2.6.2 Student Questions and Answers on Goal Specification

Q: Where is the target goal defined within a problem-solving agent search formulation?

A: The goal is explicitly defined prior to search initiation within the Goal Test function and initial problem specification. The agent formulates a goal based on its current state and performance measure, and the search algorithm uses this goal test to evaluate whether candidate state nodes represent valid solution endpoints.

2.6.3 The Five Components of a Search Problem Formulation

Converting any domain into a computable search problem requires defining exactly five components:

Symbol registry:

  • \(s_0\): Initial state (\(s_0 \in S\)).
  • \(S\): State space (set of all reachable states).
  • \(A(s)\): Action space (set of legal actions executable in state \(s\)).
  • \(\text{Result}(s, a)\): Transition model returning resulting state \(s'\).
  • \(\text{IsGoal}(s)\): Goal test function returning \(\text{True}\) or \(\text{False}\).
  • \(c(s, a, s')\): Step cost function (non-negative cost of taking action \(a\) in state \(s\) to reach \(s'\)).
  • \(g(n)\): Path cost function (total cumulative cost from initial state to node \(n\)).
  1. Initial State (\(s_0\)): The starting state in which the agent begins.
  2. Actions (\(A(s)\)): The set of legal actions applicable when the agent is in state \(s\).
  3. Transition Model (\(\text{Result}(s, a)\)): A function returning state \(s'\) resulting from executing action \(a\) in state \(s\):

\[ \text{Result}(s, a) = s' \]

  1. Goal Test (\(\text{IsGoal}(s)\)): A boolean function determining whether a given state \(s\) satisfies the goal condition.
  2. Path Cost (\(g(n)\)): A numeric function measuring the cost of a path, computed as the sum of individual step costs:

\[ g(n) = \sum_{i=1}^k c(s_{i-1}, a_i, s_i) \]

2.7 Search Strategies: Uninformed vs. Informed Search

Hook: In a game like Chess or the 15-puzzle, the state space contains trillions of states. How can an agent find the shortest path to a goal without searching the entire graph? We use Informed (Heuristic) Search to guide the search tree toward the goal.

2.7.1 Overview of Search Strategies

Once a problem is formulated as a search graph, an agent executes a search algorithm to locate a goal state. Search algorithms fall into two foundational paradigms based on whether they utilize domain-specific heuristic knowledge beyond the problem formulation:

Search Strategy Taxonomy

+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. Uninformed (Blind) Search                                          |
|    - No domain-specific heuristic evaluation                          |
|    - Evaluates nodes using only path cost g(n) or tree depth          |
|    - Algorithms: Breadth-First (BFS), Depth-First (DFS), Uniform-Cost |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 2. Informed (Heuristic) Search                                        |
|    - Employs heuristic function h(n) estimating remaining cost to goal|
|    - Combines path history g(n) with heuristic estimate h(n)          |
|    - Algorithms: Greedy Best-First Search, A* Search, AO* Search      |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+

2.7.2 Uninformed (Blind) Search Strategies

Uninformed search algorithms have no extra information about states beyond what is provided in the problem definition. They can only generate successors and distinguish goal states from non-goal states.

1. Breadth-First Search (BFS):

  • Mechanism: Expands the shallowest unexpanded node first using a FIFO (Queue) data structure.
  • Properties:
  • Completeness: Complete (if branching factor \(b\) is finite).
  • Optimality: Cost-optimal if all step costs are equal (\(c=1\)).
  • Time Complexity: \(O(b^d)\), where \(b\) is branching factor and \(d\) is goal depth.
  • Space Complexity: \(O(b^d)\) — high exponential memory requirements because all frontier nodes must remain stored in memory.

2. Depth-First Search (DFS):

  • Mechanism: Expands the deepest unexpanded node first using a LIFO (Stack) data structure.
  • Properties:
  • Completeness: Incomplete in infinite-depth spaces or graphs with loops (without cycle checking).
  • Optimality: Non-optimal (may find a deep sub-optimal goal before a shallow optimal goal).
  • Time Complexity: \(O(b^m)\), where \(m\) is maximum tree depth.
  • Space Complexity: \(O(b \cdot m)\) — modest linear memory requirements.

3. Uniform-Cost Search (UCS):

  • Mechanism: Expands the node with the lowest cumulative path cost \(g(n)\) first using a Priority Queue sorted by \(g(n)\). (Equivalent to Dijkstra's algorithm).
  • Properties:
  • Completeness: Complete (if step costs \(\epsilon > 0\)).
  • Optimality: Cost-optimal for any non-negative step costs.
  • Time Complexity: \(O(b^{1 + \lfloor C^* / \epsilon \rfloor})\), where \(C^*\) is optimal goal cost.
  • Space Complexity: \(O(b^{1 + \lfloor C^* / \epsilon \rfloor})\).

2.7.3 Informed (Heuristic) Search Strategies

Informed search algorithms utilize domain-specific knowledge through a heuristic function \(h(n)\).

Symbol registry:

  • \(n\): Search tree node.
  • \(g(n)\): Exact cumulative cost from initial state to node \(n\).
  • \(h(n)\): Estimated cost of cheapest path from node \(n\) to a goal state (\(h(n) \ge 0\), with \(h(\text{Goal}) = 0\)).
  • \(f(n)\): Total evaluation function score for node \(n\).

1. Heuristic Function \(h(n)\)

Verbal description: The heuristic function \(h(n)\) estimates the remaining step cost from node \(n\) to the nearest goal state.

\[ h(n) = \text{estimated cost from node } n \text{ to goal} \]

2. Greedy Best-First Search

  • Evaluation Function: \(f(n) = h(n)\).
  • Mechanism: Expands the node that appears closest to the goal.
  • Properties: Highly efficient in practice, but not optimal because it ignores cumulative path cost \(g(n)\).

3. \(A^*\) Search

  • Evaluation Function: \(f(n) = g(n) + h(n)\).
  • Mechanism: Evaluates nodes using the estimated total path cost through node \(n\) to the goal.

Verbal description: The evaluation function \(f(n)\) for \(A^*\) search equals the sum of exact path cost \(g(n)\) and heuristic estimate \(h(n)\).

\[ f(n) = g(n) + h(n) \]

Conditions for \(A^*\) Optimality:

  1. Admissibility: A heuristic \(h(n)\) is admissible if it never overestimates the true cost to reach the goal. That is, for all nodes \(n\):

\[ 0 \le h(n) \le h^*(n) \]

where \(h^*(n)\) is the actual true cost from \(n\) to the goal. An admissible heuristic is optimistic.

  1. Consistency (Monotonicity): A heuristic \(h(n)\) is consistent if, for every node \(n\) and every successor \(n'\) generated by action \(a\):

\[ h(n) \le c(n, a, n') + h(n') \]

Consistency enforces the triangle inequality, ensuring that the estimated total cost \(f(n)\) is non-decreasing along any path.
