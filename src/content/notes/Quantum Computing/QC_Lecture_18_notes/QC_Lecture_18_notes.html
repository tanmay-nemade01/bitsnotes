# Quantum Phase Estimation and Shor's Algorithm

18.1 Foundations: From Classical Fourier Transform to Quantum State Transformation

18.1.1 Classical vs Quantum Fourier Transform: Coordinate Output vs Quantum State

Can a quantum computer solve the Discrete Fourier Transform exponentially faster than classical supercomputers? While the classical Fast Fourier Transform requires \(O(N \log N)\) operations to compute all coordinates of a vector of size \(N\), the Quantum Fourier Transform performs the transformation in only \(O((\log N)^2)\) quantum gate operations—an exponential reduction in gate complexity. However, there is a fundamental conceptual catch: the quantum algorithm produces an amplitude-encoded quantum state rather than a readable list of numerical coordinates.

The classical Fourier transform and the quantum Fourier transform share identical algebraic foundations, yet they perform fundamentally distinct computational tasks. In classical scientific computing and signal processing, the discrete Fourier transform maps an explicit input vector \(x \in \mathbb{C}^N\) with coordinate entries \(x = (x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{N-1})^\top\) to an output vector \(y \in \mathbb{C}^N\) with coordinates \(y = (y_0, y_1, \dots, y_{N-1})^\top\). A classical algorithm, such as the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), receives every coordinate of the input vector as accessible digital memory values. The algorithm processes these entries and writes out all \(N\) numerical coordinates of the output vector into classical storage.

In quantum computation, the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) does not output numerical vector coordinates. Instead, it transforms a quantum state. Given an input quantum state: \[ |x\rangle = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} x_j |j\rangle \] where \(x_j \in \mathbb{C}\) satisfy the normalization condition \(\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} |x_j|^2 = 1\), the quantum Fourier transform acts as a unitary operator \(F_N\) to produce the transformed state: \[ |y\rangle = F_N |x\rangle = \sum_{l=0}^{N-1} y_l |l\rangle \] Here, the quantum amplitudes \(y_l\) store the Fourier coefficients. Because quantum measurement is inherently probabilistic and immediately collapses the wave function, observing the output register reveals only a single basis state \(|l\rangle\) with probability \(|y_l|^2\). One cannot read out the full set of coordinates from \(|y\rangle\) in a single execution. Obtaining all \(N\) amplitudes to high precision would require repeating state preparation and tomography at least \(O(N)\) times, which forfeits the algorithmic advantage.

This classical coordinate vector output contrasts with quantum state preparation across many advanced quantum algorithms. For example, the Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd (HHL) algorithm solves systems of linear equations \(A x = b\) in logarithmic time \(O(\log N)\) with respect to dimension \(N\), whereas standard classical matrix inversion methods scale with \(O(N)\) or \(O(N^3)\). However, the Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd algorithm does not output the classical solution vector coordinate by coordinate. It prepares a quantum state \(|x\rangle\) whose amplitudes encode the solution vector. The two approaches solve fundamentally different tasks, so one must not view the runtime comparison as a direct exponential speedup for reading out coordinates.

Scope: The Quantum Fourier Transform provides an exponential speedup in unitary gate complexity, reducing circuit depth from \(O(N \log N)\) to \(O((\log N)^2)\) for \(N = 2^k\). However, this advantage applies exclusively to tasks where the Fourier coefficients serve as intermediate quantum phases or interference weights inside a larger quantum circuit—such as period finding, phase estimation, and order finding. The algorithm cannot be used as an in-place drop-in replacement for classical FFT routines that require inspecting every individual frequency amplitude.

To understand this distinction intuitively, consider an everyday acoustic analogy. Think of the classical Fast Fourier Transform like an audio engineer printing out a complete spreadsheet listing the exact decibel volume for every musical frequency in a song. You can read, modify, and graph every individual cell in that spreadsheet. The Quantum Fourier Transform, by contrast, is like striking a bell or tuning a guitar body: all resonant frequencies vibrate simultaneously in the structure of the instrument, but you cannot extract the entire spreadsheet of amplitudes at once. Plucking the string or measuring the sound wave yields only a single sound sample at a given moment. The analogy breaks if you assume you can access individual amplitudes without collapsing the delicate superposition.

18.1.2 Cyclic Group Matrix Representation and Character Basis

We formulate the Quantum Fourier Transform mathematically over the cyclic group \(\mathbb{Z}_N\) (or \(\mathbb{Z}_{2^k}\)), where the Hilbert space dimension is \(N = 2^k\) for a register composed of \(k\) qubits. The transformation maps the standard computational basis to the character basis of the cyclic group.

The classical discrete Fourier transform acts on standard basis vectors by matrix multiplication: \[ y = F_N x \] where \(x \in \mathbb{C}^N\) is the input vector and \(y \in \mathbb{C}^N\) is the transformed output vector. The matrix entries of the unitary Fourier operator \(F_N\) are defined using the primitive \(N\)-th root of unity: \[ \omega_N = e^{2\pi i / N} \] Here, \(i = \sqrt{-1}\) denotes the imaginary unit, and \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) represents the total Hilbert space dimension. The entry in row \(l\) and column \(j\) of matrix \(F_N\) (with indices \(l, j \in \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}\)) equals: \[ (F_N)_{l, j} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \omega_N^{l j} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} e^{2\pi i l j / N} \] When applied to a computational basis state \(|j\rangle\), where \(j \in \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}\), the Quantum Fourier Transform produces a balanced, uniform superposition across all computational basis states \(|l\rangle\) with specific phase shifts: \[ |j\rangle \mapsto \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{l=0}^{N-1} \omega_N^{l j} |l\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{l=0}^{N-1} e^{2\pi i l j / N} |l\rangle \] Each basis state \(|l\rangle\) receives an amplitude weighted by the phase factor \(\omega_N^{l j}\). Because \(F_N^\dagger F_N = I\), the operator preserves vector norms and constitutes a valid quantum gate operation.

When the group structure is the boolean hypercube \(\mathbb{Z}_2^k\), the Fourier transform takes a very simple form: it factorizes into independent single-qubit Hadamard gates applied to each qubit. For the cyclic group \(\mathbb{Z}_{2^k}\), however, the Fourier transform requires entangling controlled phase rotations between qubits.

Exam note: The Quantum Fourier Transform on bit strings over \(\mathbb{Z}_2^k\) uses independent Hadamard gates applied to each qubit (as used in Simon's algorithm and the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm). In contrast, the Quantum Fourier Transform over the cyclic group \(\mathbb{Z}_{2^k}\) requires controlled phase rotations between qubits with progressively smaller angles to account for binary radix carry-overs.

18.1.3 Binary Expansion and Tensor Product State Factorization

The foundation of efficient quantum circuit design for the Quantum Fourier Transform is the tensor product state factorization. Without factorization, implementing an arbitrary \(N \times N\) unitary matrix requires \(O(N^2) = O(2^{2k})\) elementary gates. Factorization reveals that the output state is completely unentangled, allowing us to build the circuit using only \(O(k^2)\) gates.

We represent each basis integer \(j \in \{0, 1, \dots, 2^k-1\}\) in its binary expansion: \[ j = j_1 j_2 \dots j_k = \sum_{m=1}^k j_m 2^{k-m} = j_1 2^{k-1} + j_2 2^{k-2} + \dots + j_k 2^0 \] where each bit \(j_m \in \{0, 1\}\). We also define binary fractions using decimal-style radix notation: \[ 0.j_1 j_2 \dots j_m = \sum_{p=1}^m j_p 2^{-p} = \frac{j_1}{2} + \frac{j_2}{4} + \dots + \frac{j_m}{2^m} \] To understand binary fractions, compare them with standard base-10 decimal expansions. For example, consider the decimal number \(10.85\): \[ 10.85 = 1 \cdot 10^1 + 0 \cdot 10^0 + 8 \cdot 10^{-1} + 5 \cdot 10^{-2} \] In binary fractional notation, digits after the binary point scale as decreasing negative powers of two: one half, one quarter, one eighth, and so forth.

We now derive the tensor product state factorization step by step. Let \(l = \sum_{p=1}^k l_p 2^{k-p}\) be the binary expansion of integer \(l\). The fraction \(\frac{l}{2^k}\) can be written as: \[ \frac{l}{2^k} = \sum_{p=1}^k l_p 2^{-p} = 0.l_1 l_2 \dots l_k \] Substituting this expansion into the Quantum Fourier Transform definition yields: \[ \begin{aligned} |j\rangle &\mapsto \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^k}} \sum_{l=0}^{2^k-1} e^{2\pi i j l / 2^k} |l\rangle \\ &= \frac{1}{2^{k/2}} \sum_{l_1=0}^1 \sum_{l_2=0}^1 \dots \sum_{l_k=0}^1 e^{2\pi i j \left( \sum_{p=1}^k l_p 2^{-p} \right)} |l_1 l_2 \dots l_k\rangle \\ &= \frac{1}{2^{k/2}} \sum_{l_1=0}^1 \dots \sum_{l_k=0}^1 \prod_{p=1}^k e^{2\pi i j l_p 2^{-p}} |l_p\rangle \\ &= \frac{1}{2^{k/2}} \bigotimes_{p=1}^k \left( \sum_{l_p=0}^1 e^{2\pi i j l_p 2^{-p}} |l_p\rangle \right) \\ &= \frac{1}{2^{k/2}} \bigotimes_{p=1}^k \left( |0\rangle + e^{2\pi i j 2^{-p}} |1\rangle \right) \end{aligned} \] Next, analyze the exponent \(j 2^{-p}\). Shifting the binary integer \(j = j_1 j_2 \dots j_k\) by \(p\) positions to the right gives: \[ j 2^{-p} = j_1 j_2 \dots j_{k-p} . j_{k-p+1} \dots j_k = \text{integer} + 0.j_{k-p+1} \dots j_k \] Since \(e^{2\pi i \cdot \text{integer}} = 1\), the integer portion drops out entirely from the phase: \[ e^{2\pi i j 2^{-p}} = e^{2\pi i \cdot \text{integer}} \cdot e^{2\pi i 0.j_{k-p+1} \dots j_k} = e^{2\pi i 0.j_{k-p+1} \dots j_k} \] Setting \(p = 1, 2, \dots, k\) explicitly provides the complete tensor product state factorization: \[ |j_1 j_2 \dots j_k\rangle \mapsto \frac{1}{2^{k/2}} (|0\rangle + e^{2\pi i 0.j_k} |1\rangle) \otimes (|0\rangle + e^{2\pi i 0.j_{k-1} j_k} |1\rangle) \otimes \dots \otimes (|0\rangle + e^{2\pi i 0.j_1 j_2 \dots j_k} |1\rangle) \] Every computational basis state transforms into an unentangled product state. Each individual qubit sits in an equal superposition of \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) with a relative phase dictated by a binary fraction of the input bits.

Worked Example: Binary fractional expansion for basis integer \(j = 7\) in a register of \(k = 3\) qubits.

  1. Binary expansion:

The dimension is \(N = 2^3 = 8\). The integer is \(j = 7\). Compute the binary bits: \[ 7 = 1 \cdot 2^2 + 1 \cdot 2^1 + 1 \cdot 2^0 \implies j_1 = 1, \quad j_2 = 1, \quad j_3 = 1 \] So \(|j\rangle = |111\rangle_2\).

  1. Calculate binary fractional phases for each qubit:
  • For output qubit 1:

\[ 0.j_3 = 0.1_2 = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5 \] Phase factor: \[ e^{2\pi i (0.j_3)} = e^{2\pi i (1/2)} = e^{\pi i} = \cos(\pi) + i \sin(\pi) = \mathbf{-1} \] First qubit state: \[ |\psi_1\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle - |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \]

  • For output qubit 2:

\[ 0.j_2 j_3 = 0.11_2 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4} = 0.75 \] Phase factor: \[ e^{2\pi i (0.j_2 j_3)} = e^{2\pi i (3/4)} = e^{3\pi i / 2} = \cos\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}\right) + i \sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}\right) = \mathbf{-i} \] Second qubit state: \[ |\psi_2\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle - i |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \]

  • For output qubit 3:

\[ 0.j_1 j_2 j_3 = 0.111_2 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} = \frac{7}{8} = 0.875 \] Phase factor: \[ e^{2\pi i (0.j_1 j_2 j_3)} = e^{2\pi i (7/8)} = e^{7\pi i / 4} = \cos\left(\frac{7\pi}{4}\right) + i \sin\left(\frac{7\pi}{4}\right) = \mathbf{\frac{1 - i}{\sqrt{2}}} \] Third qubit state: \[ |\psi_3\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle + e^{7\pi i / 4} |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \]

  1. Composite transformed state:

Assemble the three factors into the unentangled tensor product: \[ |111\rangle \mapsto \frac{1}{2^{3/2}} (|0\rangle - |1\rangle) \otimes (|0\rangle - i |1\rangle) \otimes \left(|0\rangle + e^{7\pi i / 4} |1\rangle\right) \] Sense-check: Expanding this tensor product yields 8 computational basis states, each with amplitude magnitude \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{8}}\), matching the uniform magnitude required by the Quantum Fourier Transform.

Visually, each qubit state in the product representation corresponds to a vector lying directly on the equator of the Bloch sphere (polar angle \(\theta = \pi/2\)). The azimuthal angle \(\phi = 2\pi (0.j_{k-p+1}\dots j_k)\) points in a specific direction determined by the binary fraction. Qubit 1 rotates by \(\pi\) (pointing to \(-X\)), qubit 2 rotates by \(3\pi/2\) (pointing to \(-Y\)), and qubit 3 rotates by \(7\pi/4\) (pointing between \(+X\) and \(-Y\)).

18.1.4 Gate Implementation, Commutativity, and Qubit Reversal

We construct the quantum circuit implementing this product state factorization using two elementary components: the single-qubit Hadamard gate \(H\) and two-qubit controlled phase rotation gates \(R_m\).

The Hadamard gate creates an equal superposition and provides the initial half-turn phase: \[ H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \] When applied to basis state \(|j_m\rangle\), the Hadamard transform produces: \[ H |j_m\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle + (-1)^{j_m} |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{|0\rangle + e^{2\pi i (0.j_m)} |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \] To introduce the finer fractional phase corrections, we define the controlled phase rotation matrix \(R_m\): \[ R_m = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{2\pi i / 2^m} \end{pmatrix} \] When \(m = 1\), \(R_1 = \text{diag}(1, -1) = Z\), the Pauli \(Z\) gate. When \(m = 2\), \(R_2 = \text{diag}(1, i) = S\), the standard phase gate. When \(m = 3\), \(R_3 = T = \text{diag}(1, e^{i\pi/4})\).

The circuit synthesizes each qubit factor sequentially:

  1. Apply \(H\) to qubit 1 carrying bit \(j_1\), producing \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + e^{2\pi i 0.j_1}|1\rangle)\).
  2. Apply controlled-\(R_2\) with qubit 2 as control, adding phase \(e^{2\pi i 0.0 j_2}\) if \(j_2 = 1\), upgrading the phase to \(e^{2\pi i 0.j_1 j_2}\).
  3. Apply controlled-\(R_3\) with qubit 3 as control, upgrading the phase to \(e^{2\pi i 0.j_1 j_2 j_3}\), continuing down to controlled-\(R_k\).
  4. Repeat this sequence for qubit 2, qubit 3, down to qubit \(k\).

Notice that the first output qubit receives phase \(0.j_k\), whereas the last output qubit receives phase \(0.j_1 j_2 \dots j_k\). The output bit order is reversed relative to the input order. To restore the correct bit significance, we append swap gates across opposite pairs of qubits: wire 1 swaps with wire \(k\), wire 2 swaps with wire \(k-1\), requiring \(\lfloor k/2 \rfloor\) swap gates.

Each swap gate decomposes into three elementary controlled-NOT gates: \[ \text{SWAP}_{1, 2} = \text{CNOT}_{1 \to 2} \cdot \text{CNOT}_{2 \to 1} \cdot \text{CNOT}_{1 \to 2} \] The total gate count sums to \(k\) Hadamard gates, \(\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\) controlled phase gates, and \(\lfloor k/2 \rfloor\) swap gates. The overall circuit complexity is: \[ \text{Total Gates} = k + \frac{k(k-1)}{2} + \left\lfloor \frac{k}{2} \right\rfloor = O(k^2) = O((\log_2 N)^2) \] This quadratic scaling in qubit count \(k\) delivers the exponential speedup over the classical \(O(N \log N) = O(k 2^k)\) FFT algorithm.

Common Pitfalls:

  • Reversed output bit order: Forgetting the SWAP gates at the end of the circuit leads to bit-reversed frequency indices, corrupting downstream algorithms.
  • Premature control qubit modification: Applying a Hadamard gate to qubit \(m\) before it has finished acting as a control for earlier qubits destroys the computational basis state \(|j_m\rangle\), kicking back erroneous superposition phases.
  • Confusing classical and quantum complexity: Claiming that the QFT provides an exponential speedup for classical FFT applications ignores the state preparation and measurement readout bottlenecks.

18.1.5 Student Questions and Pedagogical Insights

Q: Do the controlled phase rotation gates commute, and does the gate execution order matter across different qubits?

A: The controlled phase gates commute with one another on a given target qubit because diagonal phase matrices commute under matrix multiplication. You can apply controlled-\(R_2\), controlled-\(R_3\), and higher rotations in any order on the same target qubit. However, transformations on earlier qubits must finish before using subsequent qubits as controls: a control qubit must remain in its definite computational basis state \(|j_m\rangle \in \{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}\) before its own Hadamard transformation alters it into a superposition.

The Quantum Fourier Transform maps computational basis states into factorized product states with binary-fraction phase encodings in \(O((\log N)^2)\) gate operations. This exact phase-encoding structure serves as the foundation for our next major topic: Quantum Phase Estimation.

Modern quantum hardware platforms like IBM Quantum and Rigetti implement Quantum Fourier Transform circuits using native single-qubit rotations and entangling gates (such as CNOT or CZ). Circuit compilers optimize swap sequences to accommodate physical qubit coupling graphs, frequently routing swaps into neighboring hardware nodes.

18.2 Quantum Phase Estimation: Problem Formulation and Geometric Setup

18.2.1 Unitary Matrices, Eigenvalues, and the Eigenphase Definition

If quantum measurements must always yield real numbers, why is the foundational eigenvalue problem in quantum algorithms built around complex numbers on the unit circle? In Quantum Phase Estimation, unitary time-evolution operators do not stretch quantum states; they rotate them, embedding characteristic frequencies into a geometric angle.

Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE) is one of the most powerful and widely used subroutines in quantum computation. It serves as the algorithmic engine driving Shor's factoring algorithm, quantum counting, and quantum algorithms for quantum chemistry and materials science. To formulate the problem, we first examine the spectral properties of an arbitrary unitary operator \(U\).

In linear algebra, an operator \(U\) acting on a complex Hilbert space is unitary if its adjoint (conjugate transpose) equals its inverse: \[ U^\dagger U = U U^\dagger = I \] where \(I\) is the identity operator. Let \(|u\rangle\) be an eigenvector of \(U\) with associated eigenvalue \(\lambda \in \mathbb{C}\): \[ U |u\rangle = \lambda |u\rangle \] The operator \(U\) acting on state vector \(|u\rangle\) scales the vector by scalar factor \(\lambda\). We determine the magnitude of \(\lambda\) by evaluating the inner product: \[ \langle u| U^\dagger U |u\rangle = \langle u| I |u\rangle = \langle u|u\rangle \] Substituting the eigenvalue relation \(U |u\rangle = \lambda |u\rangle\) and its dual bra \(\langle u| U^\dagger = \lambda^* \langle u|\) into the left-hand side yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \langle u| U^\dagger U |u\rangle &= (\lambda^* \langle u|) (\lambda |u\rangle) \\ &= \lambda^* \lambda \langle u|u\rangle \\ &= |\lambda|^2 \langle u|u\rangle \end{aligned} \] Equating both sides and assuming \(|u\rangle\) is a normalized physical quantum state with \(\langle u|u\rangle = 1\), we obtain: \[ |\lambda|^2 = 1 \implies |\lambda| = 1 \]

Because \(|\lambda| = 1\), unitary operator eigenvalues strictly lie on the complex unit circle. Every eigenvalue of a unitary operator can be written in polar form: \[ \lambda = e^{2\pi i \theta} \] where \(\theta \in [0, 1)\) is the eigenphase of the unitary operator. Any integer multiple of \(2\pi\) corresponds to a complete counterclockwise rotation around the circle, so we restrict \(\theta\) to the unit interval \([0, 1)\). The complex eigenvalue \(\lambda\) and the real eigenphase \(\theta\) exist in strict one-to-one correspondence. Standard textbooks like Nielsen and Chuang formulate the phase estimation problem as the task of estimating this parameter \(\theta\).

To understand eigenphases intuitively, consider a mechanical clock. Imagine a clock hand of fixed unit length rotating smoothly around a circular dial. A unitary operator \(U\) acts like an elapsed second: when applied to its eigenvector \(|u\rangle\), it does not stretch or shrink the hand—its length remains identically 1. Instead, it advances the hand by an angle \(\phi = 2\pi \theta\). The goal of Quantum Phase Estimation is to act as a digital stopwatch that measures this rotational angle and prints its fractional value \(\theta \in [0, 1)\) into quantum registers. The analogy holds because both conserve magnitude and track pure phase rotation; it breaks if you assume you can continuously inspect the dial without quantum measurement collapse.

Visually, the complex unit circle has key landmarks: \(\lambda = 1\) corresponds to eigenphase \(\theta = 0\); \(\lambda = i\) corresponds to \(\theta = 0.25\) (a quarter turn); \(\lambda = -1\) corresponds to \(\theta = 0.5\) (a half turn); and \(\lambda = -i\) corresponds to \(\theta = 0.75\) (a three-quarter turn).

18.2.2 Formal Black-Box Formulation and Register Specifications

The Quantum Phase Estimation problem is stated formally as follows:

  • Input requirements:
  1. A target quantum register storing the eigenvector state \(|u\rangle\). If \(|u\rangle\) lives in an \(N\)-dimensional Hilbert space \(\mathbb{C}^N\), the target register requires \(m = \lceil \log_2 N \rceil\) qubits.
  2. Quantum black-box (oracle) access to controlled unitary gates implementing powers of \(U\): specifically, \(\text{controlled-}U^{2^j}\) operations for integers \(j = 0, 1, \dots, k-1\).
  • Output goal:

Produce an approximation of the eigenphase \(\theta\) accurate to \(k\) bits of precision: \[ \theta \approx 0.\theta_1 \theta_2 \dots \theta_k = \sum_{p=1}^k \theta_p 2^{-p} \] where each binary bit \(\theta_p \in \{0, 1\}\).

Worked Example: Register qubit allocation for a \(4 \times 4\) unitary matrix.

  1. Target register dimension:

Let the unitary operator \(U\) have matrix dimension \(N = 4\). The eigenvector \(|u\rangle\) is a vector in \(\mathbb{C}^4\). Compute target register size \(m\): \[ m = \lceil \log_2 4 \rceil = \mathbf{2} \text{ qubits} \] A 2-qubit register with computational basis \(\{|00\rangle, |01\rangle, |10\rangle, |11\rangle\}\) spans the 4-dimensional state space.

  1. Control register precision:

Suppose the application requires \(k = 3\) bits of precision for estimating eigenphase \(\theta\). The control register requires: \[ k = \mathbf{3} \text{ qubits} \] These 3 qubits can distinguish \(2^3 = 8\) discrete phase values: \(\{0, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{3}{8}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{5}{8}, \frac{3}{4}, \frac{7}{8}\}\).

  1. Total register requirement:

The composite quantum circuit operates on: \[ \text{Total Qubits} = k + m = 3 + 2 = \mathbf{5} \text{ qubits} \] The complete state space has dimension \(2^5 = 32\). Sense-check: 3 control qubits provide phase resolution \(\Delta \theta = 2^{-3} = 0.125\), while 2 target qubits provide sufficient degrees of freedom to store any eigenvector in \(\mathbb{C}^4\).

Scope: Quantum Phase Estimation assumes that the quantum circuit has access to efficient implementations of \(\text{controlled-}U^{2^j}\). If evaluating \(U^{2^j}\) requires chaining \(2^j\) elementary \(U\) gates sequentially, the overall gate complexity becomes exponential in the number of precision bits (\(O(2^k)\)). In practice, an exponential speedup is achieved only when the mathematical structure of \(U\) allows repeated squaring—such as modular exponentiation in Shor's algorithm—or when \(U = e^{-i H t}\) is simulated via Hamiltonian simulation algorithms.

Common Pitfalls:

  • Confusing eigenvalue \(\lambda\) with eigenphase \(\theta\): The eigenvalue \(\lambda = e^{2\pi i \theta}\) is a complex number on the unit circle with \(|\lambda| = 1\), whereas the eigenphase \(\theta \in [0, 1)\) is a dimensionless real number representing the fractional angle.
  • Conflating control and target register scaling: The target register size \(m\) depends on the physical dimension of the system (\(m = \lceil \log_2 N \rceil\)), whereas the control register size \(k\) depends solely on the desired numerical precision of the eigenphase estimate.

18.2.3 Student Inquiries on Inputs, Oracles, and Global Phase

Q: What is the input format for the unitary matrix in the phase estimation problem? Are all matrix entries provided explicitly?

A: The unitary matrix is not given as a full classical table of \(N \times N\) matrix entries. In quantum computation, reading an entire \(N \times N\) classical matrix would take \(O(N^2)\) time, forfeiting any quantum advantage. Instead, \(U\) is provided as an operational quantum black box or hardware circuit capable of executing controlled unitary transformations on quantum state registers.

Q: Which specific eigenphase does phase estimation estimate when a unitary has multiple distinct eigenvalues?

A: The algorithm estimates the unique eigenphase corresponding specifically to the input eigenvector quantum state. The provided state selects the targeted eigenvalue. If the target register is prepared in eigenvector \(|u_a\rangle\), QPE outputs \(\theta_a\); if prepared in \(|u_b\rangle\), it outputs \(\theta_b\).

Exam note: Expect questions on how register size relates to unitary dimension. Control register size depends on desired phase precision \(k\), while target register size depends on the dimension of the unitary matrix \(N = 2^m\).

Quantum Phase Estimation formulates the extraction of unitary eigenvalues as a geometric angle measurement on the complex unit circle. The control register acts as a digital readout dial, while the target register holds the physical eigenvector.

Real-world: In quantum chemistry and quantum materials science, the unitary operator represents time evolution under a molecular Hamiltonian, \(U = e^{-i H t / \hbar}\). Quantum Phase Estimation extracts ground-state and excited-state energy eigenvalues from molecular systems, offering potential polynomial-time solutions to electronic structure problems that are intractable for classical Hartree-Fock or coupled-cluster methods.

18.3 Quantum Phase Estimation Circuit: Phase Kickback and Inverse QFT

18.3.1 Target State Preparation and Phase Kickback via Controlled Unitaries

How can an unentangled target register transfer its secret eigenvalue phase to a control qubit without altering its own state or ever being directly measured? This counterintuitive mechanism—known as phase kickback—inverts the traditional operational roles of control and target registers in quantum circuits.

The Quantum Phase Estimation circuit couples a control register of \(k\) qubits to a target register storing the eigenvector state \(|u\rangle\). The control register is initialized to the all-zero state \(|0\rangle^{\otimes k}\), while the target register is prepared in state \(|u\rangle\).

Applying Hadamard gates across all \(k\) control qubits creates an equal, balanced superposition: \[ |0\rangle^{\otimes k} |u\rangle \xrightarrow{H^{\otimes k} \otimes I} \frac{1}{2^{k/2}} \sum_{L=0}^{2^k-1} |L\rangle |u\rangle \] To understand the phase kickback mechanism, analyze a single control qubit initialized in superposition \(\frac{|0\rangle + |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\) interacting with the target state \(|u\rangle\) through a controlled-\(U\) operation: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{controlled-}U \left( \frac{|0\rangle + |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} |u\rangle \right) &= \frac{|0\rangle |u\rangle + |1\rangle U |u\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \\ &= \frac{|0\rangle |u\rangle + |1\rangle e^{2\pi i \theta} |u\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \\ &= \left(\frac{|0\rangle + e^{2\pi i \theta} |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right) |u\rangle \end{aligned} \]

Phase kickback shifts the eigenvalue phase factor \(e^{2\pi i \theta}\) directly from the target register into the relative phase of the control qubit, while leaving the target eigenvector state \(|u\rangle\) completely unentangled and invariant.

To understand phase kickback intuitively, consider an everyday mechanical recoil analogy. Imagine standing on a light skateboard (the control qubit) and pushing against a massive concrete wall (the target eigenvector). The concrete wall does not move, collapse, or deform under your push because it is an immovable eigenstate of the interaction. Instead, by Newton's third law of equal and opposite reaction, the entire kinetic recoil pushes you backward on the skateboard. In the quantum circuit, because \(|u\rangle\) is an eigenvector of \(U\), the target register acts like an immovable wall: it cannot change its quantum state, so the unitary eigenvalue phase factor kicks back entirely onto the lightweight control qubit. The analogy breaks if the target is not a pure eigenvector, in which case the target shifts state and becomes entangled with the control.

18.3.2 Multi-Qubit Register Scaling via Powers of Two and Binary Shifts

When we apply repeated powers of the unitary matrix to the eigenvector, the eigenvalue phase multiplies directly according to standard exponent rules. For an operator \(U^{2^j}\), we compute: \[ U^{2^j} |u\rangle = (e^{2\pi i \theta})^{2^j} |u\rangle = e^{2\pi i (2^j \theta)} |u\rangle \] Applying the operator \(U\) raised to the power \(2^j\) produces a relative phase angle scaled by \(2^j\).

Now consider the eigenphase expressed in its terminating \(k\)-bit binary expansion: \[ \theta = 0.\theta_1 \theta_2 \dots \theta_k = \sum_{p=1}^k \theta_p 2^{-p} \] Multiplying this binary fraction by \(2^j\) shifts the binary point exactly \(j\) places to the right: \[ 2^j \theta = (\theta_1 \theta_2 \dots \theta_j).\theta_{j+1} \dots \theta_k \] Split this expression into its integer part and its fractional part: \[ 2^j \theta = \text{integer} + 0.\theta_{j+1} \dots \theta_k \] where the integer part is \(\sum_{m=1}^j \theta_m 2^{j-m}\). Because the complex exponential function has period \(2\pi i\), integer multiples of \(2\pi\) evaluate to unity: \[ e^{2\pi i \cdot \text{integer}} = 1 \] Therefore, the integer component contributes no relative phase shift: \[ e^{2\pi i (2^j \theta)} = e^{2\pi i \cdot \text{integer}} \cdot e^{2\pi i 0.\theta_{j+1} \dots \theta_k} = e^{2\pi i 0.\theta_{j+1} \dots \theta_k} \] Each controlled-\(U^{2^j}\) gate imprints a progressively shifted binary fraction onto control qubit \(k-j\).

18.3.3 State Recovery via Inverse Quantum Fourier Transform

By cascading controlled-\(U^{2^j}\) operations for \(j = 0, 1, \dots, k-1\), the control register transforms into the composite tensor product state: \[ |\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{2^{k/2}} (|0\rangle + e^{2\pi i 0.\theta_k} |1\rangle) \otimes (|0\rangle + e^{2\pi i 0.\theta_{k-1} \theta_k} |1\rangle) \otimes \dots \otimes (|0\rangle + e^{2\pi i 0.\theta_1 \dots \theta_k} |1\rangle) \] Comparing this state with the Quantum Fourier Transform factorization derived in Section 18.1 reveals an exact structural match: \[ |\psi\rangle = \text{QFT}(|2^k \theta\rangle) = \text{QFT}(|\theta_1 \theta_2 \dots \theta_k\rangle) \] The cascading controlled unitary operations have effectively performed a Quantum Fourier Transform of the computational basis state carrying the binary digits of \(\theta\).

Because quantum circuits are reversible and unitary, the inverse operation exists and is easily constructed by reversing the gate sequence and conjugating all phase rotations. Applying the Inverse Quantum Fourier Transform \(\text{QFT}^\dagger\) maps the product state directly back into the computational basis: \[ \text{QFT}^\dagger |\psi\rangle = |\theta_1 \theta_2 \dots \theta_k\rangle \] Measuring the \(k\) control qubits in the computational basis produces the exact classical bitstring \(\theta_1 \theta_2 \dots \theta_k\).

18.3.4 Worked Walkthrough: Phase Estimation Circuit Operations

We perform a worked numerical walkthrough of phase estimation circuit operations step by step.

Worked Example: Circuit execution trace for two control qubits (\(k = 2\)) with quarter phase \(\theta = 0.01_2 = \frac{1}{4}\).

  1. System specifications:

Number of control qubits: \(k = 2\). Eigenphase: \(\theta = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25\). Eigenvalue: \(\lambda = e^{2\pi i (1/4)} = e^{\pi i / 2} = i\).

  1. Step-by-step circuit state evolution:
  • Step 1: Initialization:

Prepare the control register in \(|0\rangle_1 |0\rangle_2\) and the target in \(|u\rangle\): \[ |\psi_0\rangle = |0\rangle_1 |0\rangle_2 |u\rangle \]

  • Step 2: Superposition creation:

Apply Hadamard gates to both control qubits: \[ |\psi_1\rangle = \left(\frac{|0\rangle_1 + |1\rangle_1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \left(\frac{|0\rangle_2 + |1\rangle_2}{\sqrt{2}}\right) |u\rangle = \frac{1}{2} (|0\rangle_1 + |1\rangle_1)(|0\rangle_2 + |1\rangle_2)|u\rangle \]

  • Step 3: Controlled-\(U^1\) execution:

Qubit 2 acts as the control for \(U^{2^0} = U^1\) on target \(|u\rangle\). Since \(U |u\rangle = i |u\rangle\): \[ |\psi_2\rangle = \frac{1}{2} (|0\rangle_1 + |1\rangle_1)(|0\rangle_2 + i |1\rangle_2)|u\rangle \]

  • Step 4: Controlled-\(U^2\) execution:

Qubit 1 acts as the control for \(U^{2^1} = U^2\) on target \(|u\rangle\). Since \(U^2 |u\rangle = i^2 |u\rangle = -1 |u\rangle\): \[ |\psi_3\rangle = \frac{1}{2} (|0\rangle_1 - |1\rangle_1)(|0\rangle_2 + i |1\rangle_2)|u\rangle \]

  • Step 5: Inverse Quantum Fourier Transform (\(\text{QFT}_4^\dagger\)):

The 2-qubit inverse QFT executes the following sequence:

  1. Swap qubits 1 and 2:

\[ \frac{1}{2} (|0\rangle_2 + i |1\rangle_2)(|0\rangle_1 - |1\rangle_1) \]

  1. Apply Hadamard to qubit 1:

\[ H \left(\frac{|0\rangle_1 - |1\rangle_1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = |1\rangle_1 \]

  1. Apply controlled-\(R_2^\dagger\) (\(S^\dagger = \text{diag}(1, -i)\)) to qubit 2 controlled by qubit 1. Because qubit 1 is in state \(|1\rangle\), the gate applies \(-i\) to \(|1\rangle_2\):

\[ S^\dagger (|0\rangle_2 + i |1\rangle_2) = |0\rangle_2 + (-i)(i)|1\rangle_2 = |0\rangle_2 + |1\rangle_2 \]

  1. Apply Hadamard to qubit 2:

\[ H \left(\frac{|0\rangle_2 + |1\rangle_2}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = |0\rangle_2 \] The final state of the control register is: \[ |\psi_4\rangle = |0\rangle_1 |1\rangle_2 = \mathbf{|01\rangle} \]

  1. Measurement readout:

Measuring the two control qubits produces the deterministic bitstring \(01\). Converting from binary fractional notation: \[ 0.\theta_1 \theta_2 = 0.01_2 = \frac{0}{2} + \frac{1}{4} = \mathbf{0.25} \] Sense-check: The measured integer is \(1\). The reconstructed phase is \(\frac{1}{2^2} = \frac{1}{4}\), matching \(\theta = 0.25\) with zero error.

Visually, the circuit flows from left to right along horizontal qubit wires: the top \(k\) wires form the control register and the bottom \(m\) wires form the target register. A column of Hadamard gates spans the control wires, followed by a cascade of controlled unitary gates where the control connections sweep across qubits with powers \(U^1, U^2, \dots, U^{2^{k-1}}\). The control wires then enter the \(\text{QFT}^\dagger\) block before terminating at measurement meters.

Scope: The deterministic recovery of \(\theta\) requires that \(\theta\) can be expressed as an exact terminating binary fraction of length \(k\). If \(\theta\) cannot be represented with \(k\) bits, measurement yields the nearest dyadic approximations probabilistically.

Common Pitfalls:

  • Reversing control qubit index connections: Connecting \(U^1\) to control qubit 1 instead of control qubit \(k\) inverts the binary significance of the register, leading to erroneous phase decoding.
  • Neglecting \(\text{QFT}^\dagger\) swap gates: Omitting swap gates in the inverse QFT results in a reversed output bitstring (\(10_2\) instead of \(01_2\)).

18.3.5 Student Questions and Gate Ordering Clarifications

Q: How does multiplying the phase angle by powers of two shift the binary decimal representation?

A: Binary digits shifted past the decimal point become integers. In the complex exponential \(e^{2\pi i (2^j \theta)}\), any integer multiple of \(2\pi\) satisfies \(e^{2\pi i \cdot \text{integer}} = \cos(2\pi \cdot \text{integer}) + i \sin(2\pi \cdot \text{integer}) = 1\). Therefore, all integer parts drop out automatically, leaving only the fractional binary tail.

Exam note: Master the inputs, internal operations, and outputs of phase estimation as a general subroutine:

  • Inputs: Target register holding eigenvector \(|u\rangle\) and black-box controlled-\(U^{2^j}\) gates.
  • Operations: Hadamard wall on control qubits, cascading controlled unitary gates, and inverse QFT decoding.
  • Outputs: An accurate \(k\)-bit binary expansion of eigenphase \(\theta\).

Phase kickback imprints the unitary eigenphase onto control superpositions, allowing the Inverse Quantum Fourier Transform to decode the phase into computational basis states.

In practical implementations on quantum software platforms such as Qiskit, Cirq, and Pennylane, the controlled unitary cascade is constructed by decomposing matrix powers into elementary CNOT and rotation gates. Hardware transpilers optimize these sequences using pulse-level scheduling to minimize two-qubit gate error rates on superconducting transmon chips.

18.4 Analysis of Phase Estimation: Inexact Phases and Eigenvector Superpositions

18.4.1 Inexact Phase Estimation and Ancilla Register Bounds

What happens when the true eigenphase is an irrational number or a non-terminating fraction like \(1/3\), which can never be represented as a finite binary expansion? Furthermore, how can the algorithm succeed if we do not know how to prepare a pure eigenvector \(|u\rangle\)?

In practical applications, the true eigenphase \(\theta\) rarely terminates in an exact \(k\)-bit binary expansion. Instead, \(\theta\) possesses an infinite binary expansion: \[ \theta = 0.\theta_1 \theta_2 \dots \theta_k \theta_{k+1} \dots \] When \(\theta\) is not an exact dyadic rational of the form \(\frac{b}{2^k}\), the output state of the control register after the Inverse Quantum Fourier Transform (\(\text{QFT}^\dagger\)) is not a single computational basis state. The probability amplitudes spread across neighboring basis states, producing a discrete sinc-like probability distribution centered at the optimal dyadic approximation.

With high probability, measuring the control register yields the integer \(b\) whose binary fraction \(\frac{b}{2^k}\) is closest to \(\theta\). However, there is a non-zero probability of measuring neighboring integers. To guarantee that the algorithm outputs the closest \(k\)-bit approximation with success probability at least \(1 - \epsilon\), we allocate additional ancilla cushion qubits to the control register.

Let \(k\) denote the desired precision (accurate to \(2^{-k}\)), and let \(\epsilon \in (0, 1)\) be the maximum allowable failure probability. The total number of control qubits \(t\) is set to: \[ t = k + f(\epsilon) \] where the number of extra cushion qubits \(f(\epsilon)\) satisfies the bound: \[ f(\epsilon) = \left\lceil \log_2 \left( 2 + \frac{1}{2\epsilon} \right) \right\rceil \]

The extra ancilla cushion qubits depend only on confidence tolerance not precision bits. The cushion parameter \(f(\epsilon)\) is completely independent of the desired precision bits \(k\). For example, choosing failure tolerance \(\epsilon = 0.1\) (a 90% success probability) requires: \[ f(0.1) = \left\lceil \log_2 \left( 2 + \frac{1}{2(0.1)} \right) \right\rceil = \lceil \log_2(2 + 5) \rceil = \lceil \log_2 7 \rceil = \mathbf{3} \text{ cushion qubits} \] Regardless of whether we seek \(k = 10\) bits or \(k = 1000\) bits of precision, adding exactly 3 extra cushion qubits guarantees a 90% probability of obtaining the closest \(k\)-bit estimate.

To understand inexact phase estimation intuitively, consider a digital camera lens focusing on a thin slit of light. If the light ray strikes the dead center of a physical pixel, that single sensor lights up cleanly with 100% intensity. But if the light lands on the dividing boundary between two pixels (an inexact phase), the light spills over into adjacent pixels, creating a narrow diffraction pattern. The brightest pixel still indicates the closest coordinate, while neighboring pixels show declining intensity. Adding cushion qubits is like upgrading the camera sensor with extra boundary pixels: it sharpens the optical resolution so that rounding to the nearest target pixel succeeds with guaranteed confidence.

Visually, plotting the measurement probability \(P(y)\) against basis integer \(y \in \{0, 1, \dots, 2^t-1\}\) produces a Dirichlet kernel. The distribution reaches a sharp peak at \(2^t \theta\) and falls off rapidly as \(O(1/(\Delta y)^2)\) as the distance \(\Delta y = |y - 2^t \theta|\) increases, ensuring that states far from the true phase have negligible probability.

18.4.2 Linear Combination of Eigenvectors and Measurement Statistics

An essential feature of Quantum Phase Estimation is its behavior when the input state is not a pure individual eigenvector. Suppose the target register is prepared in an arbitrary linear combination of orthonormal eigenvectors \(|u_j\rangle\) of \(U\), with associated eigenphases \(\theta_j\): \[ |\psi\rangle = \sum_j \alpha_j |u_j\rangle \] where \(\alpha_j \in \mathbb{C}\) are complex amplitudes satisfying normalization \(\sum_j |\alpha_j|^2 = 1\), and \(U |u_j\rangle = e^{2\pi i \theta_j} |u_j\rangle\).

By the linearity of unitary quantum evolution, the phase estimation circuit acts independently on each eigenvector component in the superposition: \[ |0\rangle^{\otimes t} |\psi\rangle = \sum_j \alpha_j |0\rangle^{\otimes t} |u_j\rangle \xrightarrow{\text{QPE}} \sum_j \alpha_j |\widetilde{\theta_j}\rangle |u_j\rangle \] where \(|\widetilde{\theta_j}\rangle\) represents the \(t\)-bit quantum state estimating eigenphase \(\theta_j\).

When we measure the control register in the computational basis, the Born rule determines the measurement probability: \[ P(\widetilde{\theta_j}) = |\alpha_j|^2 \] Upon observing outcome \(\widetilde{\theta_j}\), the measurement projection collapses the target register into the corresponding pure eigenvector \(|u_j\rangle\).

Exam note: Superposition of eigenvectors yields phase outcomes with probabilities equal to squared amplitude magnitudes \(|\alpha_j|^2\). Even if one does not measure the target register, tracing it out leaves the measurement statistics of the control register completely unchanged.

18.4.3 Unified Operator Representation of Controlled Unitary Cascades

We can express the entire cascade of controlled operations compactly. Consider the control register in computational basis state \(|L\rangle = |L_1 L_2 \dots L_k\rangle\), where integer \(L \in \{0, 1, \dots, 2^k-1\}\) has binary value: \[ L = \sum_{m=1}^k L_m 2^{k-m} \] The conditional application of gates \(U^{2^0}, U^{2^1}, \dots, U^{2^{k-1}}\) performs the combined operation: \[ |L\rangle |u\rangle \mapsto |L\rangle U^L |u\rangle \] Treating the control register as an integer \(L\) applies the operator \(U\) raised to power \(L\) on the target register.

Worked Example: Unified controlled unitary application for control state five (\(L = 5\)).

  1. Control state specification:

Consider a 3-qubit control register in computational basis state \(|L\rangle = |101\rangle_2\). The register length is \(k = 3\).

  1. Evaluate integer value \(L\):

The binary bits are \(L_1 = 1\), \(L_2 = 0\), and \(L_3 = 1\). Compute integer \(L\): \[ L = 1 \cdot 2^2 + 0 \cdot 2^1 + 1 \cdot 2^0 = 4 + 0 + 1 = \mathbf{5} \]

  1. Trace conditional gate firing:
  • Control qubit 3 (least significant bit, weight \(2^0 = 1\)):

\(L_3 = 1\), so gate \(U^{2^0} = U^1\) fires on the target.

  • Control qubit 2 (middle bit, weight \(2^1 = 2\)):

\(L_2 = 0\), so gate \(U^{2^1} = U^2\) does not fire (acts as identity \(I\)).

  • Control qubit 1 (most significant bit, weight \(2^2 = 4\)):

\(L_1 = 1\), so gate \(U^{2^2} = U^4\) fires on the target.

  1. Composite operator evaluation:

Because powers of the same unitary commute (\(U^a U^b = U^{a+b}\)), the net operation on the target register is: \[ U^4 \cdot I \cdot U^1 = U^{4+0+1} = \mathbf{U^5} \] The transformation on the state evaluates to: \[ |101\rangle |u\rangle \mapsto |101\rangle U^5 |u\rangle \] Sense-check: The binary index directly selects the power \(5\), verifying the unified relation \(|L\rangle |u\rangle \mapsto |L\rangle U^L |u\rangle\).

Scope: The ancilla cushion formula \(f(\epsilon) = \lceil \log_2(2 + 1/(2\epsilon)) \rceil\) assumes standard rounding to the nearest \(k\)-bit fraction. If multiple eigenvalues of \(U\) are degenerate or separated by less than the grid resolution \(2^{-k}\), the output register will superpose multiple unresolved phase outcomes.

Common Pitfalls:

  • Assuming cushion qubits scale with precision \(k\): The number of extra ancilla qubits \(f(\epsilon)\) depends only on the error probability \(\epsilon\), not on the number of precision bits \(k\).
  • Believing target measurement is required: The measurement statistics of the phase register are completely invariant to whether the target register is measured or traced out.

18.4.4 Mathematical Duality Between QFT and Phase Estimation on Shift Operators

Quantum Phase Estimation and the Quantum Fourier Transform possess a profound mathematical duality. Consider the cyclic shift operator \(S\) acting on an \(N\)-dimensional basis: \[ S |x\rangle = |(x+1) \bmod N\rangle \] for \(x \in \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}\). The eigenvectors of the cyclic shift operator \(S\) are the character basis states of the cyclic group, which are the Quantum Fourier basis states: \[ |v_l\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{x=0}^{N-1} e^{-2\pi i \frac{l x}{N}} |x\rangle \] Applying \(S\) to \(|v_l\rangle\) yields: \[ \begin{aligned} S |v_l\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{x=0}^{N-1} e^{-2\pi i \frac{l x}{N}} |(x+1) \bmod N\rangle \\ &= e^{2\pi i \frac{l}{N}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{x=0}^{N-1} e^{-2\pi i \frac{l (x+1)}{N}} |(x+1) \bmod N\rangle \\ &= e^{2\pi i \frac{l}{N}} |v_l\rangle \end{aligned} \] The corresponding eigenvalue is \(\lambda_l = e^{2\pi i l / N}\), which gives the exact eigenphase \(\theta_l = \frac{l}{N}\). Applying Quantum Phase Estimation to the shift operator \(S\) extracts the integer \(l\). This duality reveals that QFT and QPE are dual representations of the abelian group shift operator.

18.4.5 Student Questions and Measurement Invariance

Q: What happens to the measurement statistics if we do not measure the target eigenvector register?

A: The measurement statistics of the phase register remain completely unchanged whether the target register is measured or not. Tracing out the target register preserves the exact probability distribution where each eigenphase appears with probability proportional to the squared magnitude of its amplitude \(|\alpha_j|^2\).

Phase estimation operates robustly on inexact eigenphases using a small, constant number of cushion qubits, and naturally processes superpositions of eigenvectors according to the Born rule.

Real-world: Many quantum algorithms use this measurement invariance property, relying on phase register readout without measuring internal ancilla states. In quantum chemistry and quantum materials simulation, preparing exact molecular ground states is often difficult; instead, algorithms prepare approximate variational states with substantial overlap \(|\alpha_0|^2 > 0.5\), allowing QPE to project onto the true ground state with guaranteed probability.

18.5 The Factoring Problem and Reduction to Order Finding

18.5.1 Factoring Composite Integers and Euclid's Algorithm

Why does modern global cybersecurity—safeguarding online banking, encrypted messaging, and digital signatures—rest on the mathematical difficulty of factoring composite integers? And how did Peter Shor prove that factoring large composite numbers is mathematically identical to finding the hidden period of a clock?

The integer factoring problem takes an odd composite integer \(N \in \mathbb{N}\) and seeks a non-trivial factor \(d\) satisfying \(1 < d < N\). Once a single non-trivial factor is discovered, dividing \(N\) by \(d\) yields the cofactor, allowing the complete prime factorization to be obtained recursively. The fastest known classical factoring algorithm, the General Number Field Sieve (GNFS), requires sub-exponential heuristic runtime: \[ O\left( \exp\left( \left( \sqrt[3]{\frac{64}{9}} + o(1) \right) (\ln N)^{1/3} (\ln \ln N)^{2/3} \right) \right) \] Because this classical runtime grows much faster than any polynomial in the number of bits \(n = \lceil \log_2 N \rceil\), factoring large integers with 2048 to 4096 bits remains computationally intractable for classical supercomputers, providing the security bedrock for the RSA public-key cryptosystem.

To find factors of \(N\), we choose a random integer \(a\) uniformly from the range \(1 < a < N\). The first step is purely classical: compute the greatest common divisor \(\gcd(a, N)\) using Euclid's algorithm: \[ \gcd(a, N) \] Euclid's algorithm runs efficiently on classical hardware in \(O(\log N)\) arithmetic steps. If \(\gcd(a, N) > 1\), we have stumbled upon a non-trivial factor of \(N\) by sheer luck, without needing a quantum computer. If \(\gcd(a, N) = 1\), the integer \(a\) is co-prime to \(N\). It belongs to the multiplicative group of integers modulo \(N\), denoted \(\mathbb{Z}_N^*\).

18.5.2 Modular Exponentiation and Definition of Order

When \(a\) is co-prime to \(N\), consider the sequence of successive powers of \(a\) modulo \(N\): \[ a^1 \bmod N, \quad a^2 \bmod N, \quad a^3 \bmod N, \quad \dots \] Because \(\mathbb{Z}_N^*\) is a finite group containing \(\phi(N) < N\) elements (where \(\phi\) is Euler's totient function), the pigeonhole principle guarantees that the powers must eventually repeat. Euler's totient theorem states that \(a^{\phi(N)} \equiv 1 \pmod N\), ensuring that the sequence returns to 1.

The order of \(a\) modulo \(N\), denoted \(r = \text{ord}_N(a)\), is the smallest positive integer such that: \[ a^r \equiv 1 \pmod N \] The order \(r\) is the smallest positive exponent that returns the modular power to unity. All intermediate powers \(a^1, a^2, \dots, a^{r-1} \pmod N\) are strictly distinct and different from \(1\). The sequence of powers repeats periodically with period \(r\): \[ a^{x + r} \equiv a^x \cdot a^r \equiv a^x \cdot 1 \equiv a^x \pmod N \]

To understand the order intuitively, imagine an analog clock face marked with integers \(0, 1, \dots, N-1\). Choosing a base \(a\) and repeatedly multiplying by \(a\) modulo \(N\) is like hopping between numbers on this dial. Because the clock face has a finite number of positions, the hopping sequence cannot wander forever; it must eventually loop back to \(1\). The number of hops required to complete one full cycle and return to \(1\) is the order \(r\). The analogy holds because modular multiplication generates a closed, deterministic periodic cycle; it breaks if \(\gcd(a, N) > 1\), which would trap the sequence in a sub-loop that never visits \(1\).

Once we determine the order \(r\), we can factor \(N\) using elementary number theory. Suppose \(r\) is an even integer. We can rewrite the congruence \(a^r \equiv 1 \pmod N\) as a difference of squares: \[ a^r - 1 \equiv 0 \pmod N \iff (a^{r/2} - 1)(a^{r/2} + 1) \equiv 0 \pmod N \] This congruence means that \(N\) divides the product \((a^{r/2} - 1)(a^{r/2} + 1)\). As long as \(a^{r/2} \not\equiv -1 \pmod N\), \(N\) does not divide either term individually. Therefore, the factors of \(N\) must be distributed between the two terms. Computing the classical greatest common divisors: \[ d_1 = \gcd(a^{r/2} - 1, N), \quad d_2 = \gcd(a^{r/2} + 1, N) \] using Euclid's algorithm yields at least one non-trivial factor of \(N\). Number theory proves that for an odd composite \(N\) with at least two distinct prime factors, choosing \(a\) uniformly at random yields an even order \(r\) satisfying \(a^{r/2} \not\equiv -1 \pmod N\) with probability at least \(1 - 2^{-(m-1)} \ge 1/2\), where \(m \ge 2\) is the number of distinct prime factors.

18.5.3 Worked Examples: Computing Orders in Modular Arithmetic

Worked Example 1: Order calculation of four modulo seven yielding order three.

  • Modulus: \(N = 7\).
  • Base: \(a = 4\).
  • Check co-primality: \(\gcd(4, 7) = 1\).
  • Compute successive powers modulo 7:
  • First power: \(4^1 = 4 \equiv \mathbf{4} \pmod 7\).
  • Second power: \(4^2 = 16 = 2 \cdot 7 + 2 \equiv \mathbf{2} \pmod 7\).
  • Third power: \(4^3 = 4 \cdot 4^2 \equiv 4 \cdot 2 = 8 = 1 \cdot 7 + 1 \equiv \mathbf{1} \pmod 7\).
  • The sequence of distinct modular values is \(\{4, 2, 1\}\).
  • The order of \(4\) modulo \(7\) is:

\[ \mathbf{r = 3} \] Sense-check: \(4^3 = 64 = 9 \cdot 7 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 7\). Since 3 is the smallest positive integer exponent giving 1, \(r = 3\).

Worked Example 2: Order calculation of five modulo seven yielding order six.

  • Modulus: \(N = 7\).
  • Base: \(a = 5\).
  • Compute successive powers modulo 7:
  • First power: \(5^1 = 5 \equiv \mathbf{5} \pmod 7\).
  • Second power: \(5^2 = 25 = 3 \cdot 7 + 4 \equiv \mathbf{4} \pmod 7\).
  • Third power: \(5^3 \equiv 5 \cdot 4 = 20 = 2 \cdot 7 + 6 \equiv \mathbf{6} \pmod 7\).
  • Fourth power: \(5^4 \equiv 5 \cdot 6 = 30 = 4 \cdot 7 + 2 \equiv \mathbf{2} \pmod 7\).
  • Fifth power: \(5^5 \equiv 5 \cdot 2 = 10 = 1 \cdot 7 + 3 \equiv \mathbf{3} \pmod 7\).
  • Sixth power: \(5^6 \equiv 5 \cdot 3 = 15 = 2 \cdot 7 + 1 \equiv \mathbf{1} \pmod 7\).
  • The distinct powers form the sequence \(\{5, 4, 6, 2, 3, 1\}\).
  • The order of \(5\) modulo \(7\) is:

\[ \mathbf{r = 6} \] Sense-check: The sequence visits all 6 non-zero elements of \(\mathbb{Z}_7^*\), confirming that 5 is a primitive root modulo 7 with maximal order \(r = \phi(7) = 6\).

18.5.4 Unifying Fourier Transform Framework: Period Finding Across Groups

Order finding is an instance of hidden period finding. Define a modular exponentiation function \(f: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}_N^*\) by: \[ f(x) = a^x \bmod N \] Evaluate the function at \(x + r\): \[ f(x + r) = a^{x + r} \bmod N = (a^x \cdot a^r) \bmod N = (a^x \cdot 1) \bmod N = f(x) \] The function \(f(x)\) is periodic over the integers with period \(r\).

This establishes a unified algebraic pattern across quantum algorithms:

  1. Simon's Algorithm finds the period \(s \in \{0, 1\}^n\) over group \(\mathbb{Z}_2^n\) using Hadamard transforms \(H^{\otimes n}\).
  2. Standard Period Finding finds an integer period over cyclic group \(\mathbb{Z}_{2^k}\) using the Quantum Fourier Transform \(\text{QFT}_{2^k}\).
  3. Shor's Algorithm seeks the period over the infinite group of integers \(\mathbb{Z}\). Because we cannot construct an infinite Fourier transform, we choose a finite dimension \(M = 2^k\) where \(N^2 \le 2^k < 2N^2\), and run \(\text{QFT}\) over \(\mathbb{Z}_{2^k}\).

Order finding generalises period finding across diverse group structures, demonstrating that quantum speedups in algebraic problems stem from character transforms over abelian groups.

Scope: The reduction of factoring to order finding applies to any composite integer \(N\) that is not a prime power (\(N \neq p^k\)). If \(N\) is a prime power, classical root extraction algorithms can factor \(N\) in polynomial time. When \(N\) has at least two distinct prime factors, repeated random sampling of \(a\) guarantees that an even order \(r\) yielding non-trivial factors is found in a small number of trials.

Common Pitfalls:

  • Assuming odd order \(r\) can always be used: If \(r\) is odd, \(r/2\) is not an integer, so the difference-of-squares factorization \((a^{r/2}-1)(a^{r/2}+1)\) cannot be formed; one must pick a new random base \(a\).
  • Ignoring the trivial factor case: If \(a^{r/2} \equiv -1 \pmod N\), then \(a^{r/2} + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod N\), so \(\gcd(a^{r/2}+1, N) = N\), which yields only the trivial factor \(N\).

18.5.5 Student Questions on Function Domains and Periods

Q: Why can we not define the periodic modular exponentiation function on the finite domain modulo r instead of the integers?

A: The order r is the exact unknown value the algorithm must compute. If the domain were restricted to integers modulo r, the oracle would already require knowing r, eliminating the need for a quantum algorithm. We must define the function over a large domain of integers \(\mathbb{Z}\) and let the Quantum Fourier Transform extract the hidden period \(r\).

Exam note: Factoring reduces to order finding by identifying the period of modular exponentiation. The quantum computer's sole role in Shor's algorithm is to compute the order \(r\); all factoring steps before and after are classical.

The integer factoring problem reduces to finding the order \(r\) of a random base \(a\) modulo \(N\). This period finding task fits cleanly into the general Quantum Fourier Transform framework.

Real-world: The reduction of factoring to order finding is the theoretical engine behind the vulnerability of RSA-2048 and RSA-4096. A quantum computer capable of evaluating modular exponentiation for 2048-bit integers could break RSA keys in hours, motivating the global cybersecurity transition to NIST post-quantum cryptography standards.

18.6 Shor's Algorithm via Phase Estimation

18.6.1 The Modular Multiplication Unitary Operator

If deploying Quantum Phase Estimation requires preparing an eigenvector \(|u\rangle\), but every eigenvector of the modular multiplication operator explicitly requires knowing the order \(r\) in advance, does Shor's algorithm trap us in a circular paradox? The breakthrough of Shor's discovery is that preparing the trivial computational basis state \(|1\rangle = |00\dots 01\rangle\) simultaneously prepares all \(r\) eigenvectors in a balanced superposition.

Shor's algorithm implements order finding using Quantum Phase Estimation as its central quantum engine. Given an odd composite integer \(N\) and a coprime base \(a\) (\(\gcd(a, N) = 1\)), we define a modular multiplication operator \(U_a\) acting on computational basis states \(|y\rangle\) for \(y \in \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}\): \[ U_a |y\rangle = |(a y) \bmod N\rangle \] For basis states with \(N \le y \le 2^m - 1\), the operator acts as the identity \(U_a |y\rangle = |y\rangle\).

Because \(\gcd(a, N) = 1\), the map \(y \mapsto (a y) \bmod N\) is a bijective permutation of the integers in \(\mathbb{Z}_N^*\). Permutation operators preserve vector inner products: \[ \langle a x \bmod N | a y \bmod N \rangle = \delta_{x, y} \] Therefore, \(U_a\) is a unitary operator (\(U_a^\dagger U_a = I\)).

Controlled powers of this operator, \(\text{controlled-}U_a^{2^j}\), evaluate repeated squaring: \[ U_a^{2^j} |y\rangle = |(a^{2^j} y) \bmod N\rangle \] Classical reversible arithmetic circuits implement modular exponentiation using repeated modular squaring in polynomial gate complexity \(O((\log N)^3)\).

18.6.2 Spectral Decomposition: Eigenvectors and Eigenphases of Modular Multiplication

We determine the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of \(U_a\). For each integer \(s \in \{0, 1, \dots, r-1\}\), where \(r = \text{ord}_N(a)\), define the quantum state: \[ |u_s\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{j=0}^{r-1} e^{-2\pi i \frac{s j}{r}} |a^j \bmod N\rangle \] The state \(|u_s\rangle\) is a Fourier superposition across the computational basis states generated by the cyclic powers of \(a\) modulo \(N\).

We now verify that \(|u_s\rangle\) is an eigenvector of \(U_a\) by evaluating the operation step by step: \[ U_a |u_s\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{j=0}^{r-1} e^{-2\pi i \frac{s j}{r}} U_a |a^j \bmod N\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{j=0}^{r-1} e^{-2\pi i \frac{s j}{r}} |a^{j+1} \bmod N\rangle \] Perform a change of summation index by defining \(l = j + 1 \iff j = l - 1\). When \(j = 0\), \(l = 1\); when \(j = r - 1\), \(l = r\). Substituting \(j = l - 1\) into the phase factor yields: \[ e^{-2\pi i \frac{s j}{r}} = e^{-2\pi i \frac{s (l-1)}{r}} = e^{2\pi i \frac{s}{r}} \cdot e^{-2\pi i \frac{s l}{r}} \] Factoring out the term \(e^{2\pi i \frac{s}{r}}\) produces: \[ U_a |u_s\rangle = e^{2\pi i \frac{s}{r}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{l=1}^{r} e^{-2\pi i \frac{s l}{r}} |a^l \bmod N\rangle \] Now inspect the boundary term at \(l = r\):

  • The basis state is \(|a^r \bmod N\rangle\). Since \(a^r \equiv 1 \equiv a^0 \pmod N\), we have \(|a^r \bmod N\rangle = |a^0 \bmod N\rangle = |1\rangle\).
  • The phase factor is \(e^{-2\pi i \frac{s r}{r}} = e^{-2\pi i s} = 1\), since \(s\) is an integer.

Because \(e^{-2\pi i \frac{s \cdot 0}{r}} = 1\), the term at \(l = r\) is identical to the term at \(l = 0\): \[ e^{-2\pi i \frac{s r}{r}} |a^r \bmod N\rangle = e^{-2\pi i \frac{s \cdot 0}{r}} |a^0 \bmod N\rangle \] We can therefore replace the summation from \(l = 1\) to \(r\) with a summation from \(l = 0\) to \(r - 1\): \[ \begin{aligned} U_a |u_s\rangle &= e^{2\pi i \frac{s}{r}} \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{l=0}^{r-1} e^{-2\pi i \frac{s l}{r}} |a^l \bmod N\rangle \right) \\ &= e^{2\pi i \frac{s}{r}} |u_s\rangle \end{aligned} \]

The state \(|u_s\rangle\) is an exact eigenvector of \(U_a\) with eigenvalue: \[ \lambda_s = e^{2\pi i \frac{s}{r}} \] and associated eigenphase: \[ \theta_s = \frac{s}{r} \] Each eigenphase encodes the unknown order \(r\) in its denominator, scaled by integer index \(s \in \{0, 1, \dots, r-1\}\).

To understand this spectral decomposition intuitively, consider a prism separating a beam of light. Think of the computational basis state \(|1\rangle\) like a beam of white light entering the Quantum Phase Estimation circuit. White light appears uniform, but passing it through the prism splits it into all constituent spectral frequencies \(s/r\). Each frequency corresponds to an eigenvector \(|u_s\rangle\). Measuring the register catches one photon at a specific frequency \(s/r\), allowing classical post-processing to extract the denominator \(r\).

18.6.3 The Eigenvector Superposition Identity: Resolving Unknown Orders

A serious practical obstacle appears: constructing the state \(|u_s\rangle\) requires prior knowledge of the order \(r\), which is precisely the unknown integer we are trying to compute. Peter Shor solved this obstacle by proving that summing all \(r\) eigenvectors reproduces the easily prepared computational basis state \(|1\rangle\).

Worked Example: Orthogonality summation reducing eigenvector superposition to state one.

  1. Sum all \(r\) eigenvectors over \(s \in \{0, 1, \dots, r-1\}\):

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} |u_s\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{j=0}^{r-1} e^{-2\pi i \frac{s j}{r}} |a^j \bmod N\rangle \right) \]

  1. Exchange summation order:

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} |u_s\rangle = \frac{1}{r} \sum_{j=0}^{r-1} \left( \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} e^{-2\pi i \frac{s j}{r}} \right) |a^j \bmod N\rangle \]

  1. Evaluate the inner sum over \(s\) using the orthogonality of roots of unity:

The inner sum is a finite geometric series \(\sum_{s=0}^{r-1} \left( e^{-2\pi i j / r} \right)^s\):

  • For \(j = 0\):

\[ \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} e^{-2\pi i \frac{s \cdot 0}{r}} = \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} 1 = \mathbf{r} \]

  • For \(j \neq 0\) (with \(1 \le j \le r-1\)):

The ratio \(q = e^{-2\pi i j / r} \neq 1\). Using the geometric series formula: \[ \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} q^s = \frac{1 - q^r}{1 - q} = \frac{1 - e^{-2\pi i j}}{1 - e^{-2\pi i j / r}} = \frac{1 - 1}{1 - e^{-2\pi i j / r}} = \mathbf{0} \]

  1. Combine surviving terms:

Only the \(j = 0\) term survives the summation: \[ \frac{1}{r} \left( r \cdot |a^0 \bmod N\rangle \right) = |a^0 \bmod N\rangle = \mathbf{|1\rangle} \] Sense-check: The equal superposition of all \(r\) eigenvectors collapses to the single computational basis state \(|1\rangle = |00\dots 01\rangle\) with total probability \(\sum_{s=0}^{r-1} |1/\sqrt{r}|^2 = r \cdot \frac{1}{r} = 1\).

Computational basis state one decomposes into an equal superposition of all eigenvectors: \[ |1\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} |u_s\rangle \] Preparing the input state \(|1\rangle = |00\dots 01\rangle\) requires no knowledge whatsoever of the unknown order \(r\).

18.6.4 Phase Estimation Execution and Measurement Probabilities

To execute Shor's algorithm, we initialize the target register to \(|1\rangle\) and the control register of \(t\) qubits to \(|0\rangle^{\otimes t}\). Using the eigenvector superposition identity, the input state expands as: \[ |0\rangle^{\otimes t} |1\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} |0\rangle^{\otimes t} |u_s\rangle \] Executing phase estimation on this state evolves each component independently by linearity: \[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} |0\rangle^{\otimes t} |u_s\rangle \xrightarrow{\text{QPE}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} \left|\widetilde{\frac{s}{r}}\right\rangle |u_s\rangle \] where \(|\widetilde{s/r}\rangle\) denotes the control register state approximating eigenphase \(\theta_s = \frac{s}{r}\).

Measuring the control register in the computational basis produces an estimate of the phase \(\frac{s}{r}\) for an integer \(s\) selected uniformly at random from \(\{0, 1, \dots, r-1\}\). The probability of observing any specific phase estimate is: \[ P(s) = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\right|^2 = \frac{1}{r} \]

18.6.5 Classical Post-Processing via Continued Fractions and Factoring Completion

The measurement yields a rational value \(\phi \approx \frac{s}{r}\) accurate to \(t\) bits of precision. We choose the control register size to be \(t = 2n + 1\) qubits (where \(n = \lceil \log_2 N \rceil\)), guaranteeing \(2^t \ge N^2 > r^2\).

We run the classical Continued Fractions Algorithm on \(\phi\). A foundational theorem of Diophantine approximation states that if: \[ \left| \phi - \frac{s}{r} \right| \le \frac{1}{2 r^2} \] then \(\frac{s}{r}\) is a convergent of the continued fraction expansion of \(\phi\). Continued fractions efficiently produces an irreducible fraction: \[ \frac{s'}{r'} \] in \(O((\log N)^3)\) classical time.

  • If \(\gcd(s, r) = 1\), then \(s' = s\) and the denominator gives the exact order: \(r' = r\).
  • If \(\gcd(s, r) > 1\), then \(r'\) is a proper divisor of \(r\). By repeating the quantum circuit a few times (typically 2 to 3 runs) to obtain independent samples \(s_1/r\) and \(s_2/r\), the least common multiple of their denominators recovers the true order:

\[ r = \text{lcm}(r'_1, r'_2, \dots) \]

Once the order \(r\) is determined, we complete the factorization:

  1. If \(r\) is odd, pick a new random base \(a\) and repeat.
  2. If \(r\) is even, compute \(x = a^{r/2} \bmod N\).
  3. If \(x \equiv -1 \pmod N\), pick a new random base \(a\) and repeat.
  4. If \(x \not\equiv -1 \pmod N\), rewrite \(a^r - 1 \equiv 0 \pmod N\) as a difference of squares:

\[ a^r - 1 = (a^{r/2} - 1)(a^{r/2} + 1) \equiv 0 \pmod N \] Compute the classical greatest common divisors: \[ d_1 = \gcd(a^{r/2} - 1, N), \quad d_2 = \gcd(a^{r/2} + 1, N) \] using Euclid's algorithm. At least one of \(d_1\) or \(d_2\) is guaranteed to be a non-trivial factor of \(N\).

Scope: Continued fractions requires the control register size to satisfy \(2^t \ge N^2\) (i.e., \(t \ge 2\lceil \log_2 N \rceil\)). This quadratic precision bound ensures that the interval around \(s/r\) contains no other rational fraction with denominator less than \(N\), guaranteeing unique convergent identification.

Common Pitfalls:

  • Assuming a single run always returns the order: If \(\gcd(s, r) > 1\), continued fractions simplifies the fraction, returning a proper divisor \(r'\). One must compute the least common multiple of multiple measurement outcomes.
  • Attempting to prepare \(|u_s\rangle\) directly: Preparing an individual eigenvector requires knowing \(r\). Initializing the target register to \(|1\rangle\) circumvents this requirement.
