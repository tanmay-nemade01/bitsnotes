# Entanglement, Bell States, and Bell Inequalities

Superposition is only the first of three quantum effects used to build quantum computers. The other two are entanglement and interference, and they supply the power behind real quantum algorithms. A single qubit in superposition already behaves strangely, but the striking effects appear once several qubits act together.

This lecture follows one arc. It starts by separating states that split across qubits from states that refuse to split, then meets the four maximally entangled Bell states and the circuits that build and measure them. Those circuits lead to teleportation, which moves a qubit state using prior entanglement plus two classical bits, and to the no-cloning theorem, which explains why teleportation must destroy the original. Interference then shows how algorithms pick right answers out of large superpositions. The last part replays the Einstein-Bohm-Bell story: the paradox, the classical ceiling on correlations, its quantum violation, and the projector math that turns the philosophy into counted outcomes.

8.1 Product States, Entangled States, and Measurement Correlations

Two coins tossed by two friends can land in any combination, and knowing one result tells you nothing about the other. Now picture two coins that always land the same way up, no matter how far apart the friends stand. What kind of link could force that agreement without any message passing between them?

That puzzle is the entry point to entanglement. One qubit in superposition is strange on its own, but the real break with classical thinking shows up when several qubits are described together. Some joint states split neatly into one description per qubit, and some refuse to split at all. The ones that refuse are entangled, and their measurement outcomes carry correlations that no classical arrangement can mimic.

8.1.1 The Tensor Product and Product States

A joint object made of more than one qubit is called a multi-qubit state, meaning one mathematical object that describes the whole register at once. The tool that builds it is the tensor product, also called the Kronecker product. When qubit 1 sits in some state \(|q_1\rangle\) and qubit 2 sits in some state \(|q_2\rangle\), the joint state is written \(|q_1\rangle \otimes |q_2\rangle\), shortened to \(|q_1 q_2\rangle\). In words: "q1 tensor q2." The single-system descriptions \(|q_1\rangle\) and \(|q_2\rangle\) are called local states, meaning each one is confined to its own Hilbert space and does not spread across the other system. Each single-system space is called a subsystem, and a space built from two of them, written \(H_A \otimes H_B\), is called a bipartite space, meaning a space with two parties.

Think of the tensor product the way you think of forming words from letters. The letter "c" and the letter "a" each have their own identity, and writing them side by side as "ca" creates a larger object without changing either letter. The tensor product works the same way: it places two independent qubit descriptions side by side into one larger description. Where the analogy breaks: letters in a word never develop joint properties, while qubit pairs can become entangled, at which point the "letters" can no longer be read separately at all.

A product state is any multi-qubit state that can be written as a tensor product of defined local states, for instance \(|q_1\rangle \otimes |q_2\rangle\). The picture is direct: register \(A\) holds a fully specified state on its own, register \(B\) holds a fully specified state on its own, and the joint state is just their product. Nothing about the pair goes beyond what the two local descriptions already say.

A two-qubit register lives in the space \(\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2\), the tensor product of two two-dimensional complex spaces, which has the four basis strings \(00\), \(01\), \(10\), and \(11\) as its basis. Each of these four basis states is itself a product state, since \(|00\rangle = |0\rangle \otimes |0\rangle\), \(|01\rangle = |0\rangle \otimes |1\rangle\), and so on. Each one factors into one local state on register \(A\) and one on register \(B\), so it carries no joint information beyond what each qubit already states.

Worked check: factoring a basis state. Take \(|01\rangle\) and test whether it splits. Try \(|0\rangle \otimes |1\rangle\): the first factor fixes register \(A\) at \(0\) and the second fixes register \(B\) at \(1\), and their tensor product is exactly \(|01\rangle\). The split works, so \(|01\rangle\) is a product state. Try the same game with the state \((|00\rangle + |11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) in the next subsection: no choice of single-qubit factors reproduces both terms at once, which is the first hint that something new is going on.

Recap: product states are joint states that factor into local states, one per subsystem, with the tensor product as the glue. Bridge: the next step asks what happens when no such factorization exists, which is where entanglement begins.

8.1.2 Entangled States and the Multipartite Definition

An entangled state is the opposite of a product state: a multi-qubit state that cannot be written as a tensor product of any two local states. Stated with care for two qubits: given a two-qubit state \(\beta_{XY}\), there do not exist a state \(|i\rangle_A\) on the first register and a state \(|j\rangle_B\) on the second register with the whole state equal to \(|i\rangle_A \otimes |j\rangle_B\). In words: "no pair of single-system states multiplies to give this state." When no such factorization exists, the state is entangled.

The definition reaches past two qubits without change. Picture a state \(|\Psi\rangle\) spread over systems \(A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots, A_N\), a ten-qubit system, for instance. The state is entangled when it cannot be written as a product of one local state per system:

\[ |\Psi\rangle \neq |\psi_1\rangle_{A_1} \otimes |\psi_2\rangle_{A_2} \otimes \cdots \otimes |\psi_N\rangle_{A_N}. \]

In words: "psi is not a product of local psis." Building such an \(N\)-party state means repeating the Kronecker product: whatever rule combined a pair of vectors keeps getting applied to bring in the next system. A state with this product form is not entangled. A state that cannot be cast in this form is an entangled \(N\)-qubit state. Note the negation covers every possible factorization: a three-qubit state counts as entangled in the full sense only when it splits in no grouping at all, though states can also be entangled across one cut while factoring across another.

Reading entanglement off a written state is sometimes quick and sometimes hard. Handed a general two-qubit state \(\alpha|00\rangle + \beta|01\rangle + \gamma|10\rangle + \delta|11\rangle\), where \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), \(\gamma\), and \(\delta\) are complex amplitudes, inspection alone may not settle whether it factors. A useful determinant test exists for two qubits: the state factors exactly when \(\alpha\delta - \beta\gamma = 0\), and any nonzero value of that combination signals entanglement. But real mathematical machinery can be needed in general, and the problem grows harder as systems grow because the range of possible entangled states grows with dimension. Still, a state such as \((|00\rangle + |01\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) gives itself away at once, since it rewrites as \(|0\rangle \otimes (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\), a direct product.

Scope: the tensor-product definition of entanglement stated here applies to pure states, which are the states in use through most of this lecture. For mixed states the definition needs extra care, as discussed in the Q&A below. Assumption: the argument also assumes the subsystems are fixed in advance. Entanglement is always relative to a chosen split of the system into parties; regroup the parties and the verdict can change.

Picture the distinction as the difference between two separate instruction sheets and one shared secret. A product state is like two sealed envelopes, each holding one friend's instructions, readable independently. An entangled state is like a single sheet torn in half so that neither half carries a readable instruction on its own, yet fitting the halves together reveals a message neither half contained. Where the picture breaks: paper halves can be read without changing them, while measuring an entangled pair collapses it, as the next subsection shows.

Recap: entanglement means no factorization into local states exists, for two qubits or for any number of parties. Bridge: the definition is mathematical, but its physical punch lands at measurement time, which is where the correlations appear.

8.1.3 Measurement Correlations and the Product-State Contrast

The physical punch of entanglement lands at measurement time. Take a two-qubit register and measure both qubits in the \(01\) basis, the same basis as the \(\sigma_z\) spin basis. Each qubit returns one classical bit, an outcome of \(0\) or \(1\). Call the two outcomes \(B_1\) and \(B_2\). In general each can be either value, so four classical strings are possible: \(00\), \(01\), \(10\), and \(11\).

Now place the qubits in the entangled state

\[ |\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\bigr). \]

In words: "zero zero plus one one, all over root two." Not all four outcomes occur. Because the state superposes only \(|00\rangle\) and \(|11\rangle\), the only live outcomes are \(00\) and \(11\). A \(0\) on the first qubit forces a \(0\) on the second; a \(1\) on the first forces a \(1\) on the second. In spin language: spin up on the first measurement forces spin up on the second, spin down forces spin down. With values \(\pm \hbar/2\), where \(\hbar\) is the reduced Planck constant, a plus-half on one side forces a plus-half on the other, and a minus-half forces a minus-half. The two classical outcomes are fully correlated.

Probabilities sharpen the point. Two independent qubits would spread weight over four outcomes. Here only two survive, each with probability \(1/2\), computed by the Born rule as the squared size of each amplitude: \(|1/\sqrt{2}|^2 = 1/2\). The output distribution is uniform over \(00\) and \(11\). That equal weighting is one signature of maximal entanglement: the spread of the distribution is as wide as it can be, so uncertainty about the outcome is at its peak. Maximal uncertainty in the joint outcomes marks the maximally entangled signature.

Worked contrast: entangled versus product outcomes. Start both registers from \(|00\rangle\) and run two preparations. Preparation one builds \(|\psi\rangle = (|00\rangle + |11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) and measures both qubits one hundred times: about fifty rounds read \(00\) and about fifty read \(11\), with \(01\) and \(10\) never appearing. Preparation two builds \(|\chi\rangle = |0\rangle \otimes |+\rangle\) below and measures one hundred times: all one hundred rounds read \(0\) on the first qubit, while the second qubit reads \(0\) about fifty times and \(1\) about fifty times. The first table shows perfect correlation; the second shows none. Sense-check: the entangled table has two live outcomes with equal weight, matching the two nonzero amplitudes, while the product table factorizes into independent per-qubit statistics.

Contrast this with a product state,

\[ |\chi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|00\rangle + |01\rangle\bigr) = |0\rangle \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\bigr) = |0\rangle \otimes |+\rangle, \]

where \(|+\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) is the plus state. In words: "zero zero plus zero one, over root two, equals zero tensor plus." The only possible outcomes are \(00\) and \(01\). The first qubit always reads \(0\), yet that fixed value says nothing about the second qubit, which returns \(0\) or \(1\) with equal probability \(1/2\) each. The outcomes are not correlated. Three regimes result: perfectly correlated outcomes (\(00\) and \(11\) only), perfectly anti-correlated outcomes (\(01\) and \(10\) only, from states built on \(|01\rangle\) and \(|10\rangle\)), and uncorrelated outcomes as just shown.

A middle case shows that entanglement comes in degrees. The state

\[ |\phi\rangle = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}|00\rangle + \frac{1}{2}|11\rangle \]

is valid and normalized, since \((\sqrt{3}/2)^2 + (1/2)^2 = 3/4 + 1/4 = 1\). In words: "root three over two times zero zero, plus one half times one one." The live outcomes are again \(00\) and \(11\), so correlation persists, but with probabilities \(3/4\) and \(1/4\). The bias toward \(00\) lowers the uncertainty, marking a non-maximally entangled state: correlation survives, at reduced strength.

To see the correlation pattern at a glance, plot the outcome probabilities as a bar chart with the four strings \(00\), \(01\), \(10\), \(11\) along the horizontal axis and probability on the vertical axis. For the maximally entangled state the chart shows two equal tall bars at \(00\) and \(11\) with flat zeros elsewhere. For the product state the tall bars sit at \(00\) and \(01\). For the non-maximally entangled state the bar at \(00\) stands three times taller than the one at \(11\). The takeaway: entanglement concentrates the probability onto joint outcomes that move together.

Two traps catch beginners here. First, correlation is not causation and not communication: seeing \(0\) on the first qubit tells you the second will read \(0\), but nothing you do to the first qubit sends a message to the second. Second, a fixed outcome on one side does not imply correlation: in the product state above the first qubit always reads \(0\), yet the two sides are completely independent. A constant is not a correlation.

Recap: entangled states concentrate measurement weight onto joint outcomes that agree or disagree together, while product states factor into independent per-qubit statistics. Bridge: with the correlation picture in place, the next section names the four states where the correlation is strongest.

8.1.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Do these correlations belong to the particles themselves, or only to the states of the system? A: That question reaches into philosophy, and the answer shifts with the view of quantum mechanics held by whoever answers. Whether the written description carries the correlations or the two systems truly possess them is exactly the puzzle that troubled Einstein, and it leads toward hidden-variable ideas. What stays safe is this: our information about the state comes from measurement, and the description we write down carries correlations that can stretch across space and time in ways classical pictures do not permit.

Q: Handed an arbitrary state that may or may not be entangled, can we decide the matter without measuring it? A: With the full mathematical description written out, a technique called the Schmidt decomposition identifies entanglement: every bipartite pure state can be rewritten as \(|\psi\rangle_{AB} = \sum_i \lambda_i |u_i\rangle_A \otimes |v_i\rangle_B\) with Schmidt coefficients \(\lambda_i \geq 0\), and more than one nonzero coefficient means entanglement. Handed an unknown two-qubit state in the laboratory, the decision is hard in general. Working practice uses entanglement witnesses, which are targeted measurements arranged to expose entanglement, or a Bell test (a Bell CHSH test) that quantifies correlations across a set of measurements and compares them against a threshold. Every such test needs many copies of the state, because one quantum measurement is a probabilistic event and only statistics reveal the pattern. Even on paper the task can be tough: for a general state with coefficients on \(00\), \(01\), \(10\), \(11\), inspection often fails, and larger systems are worse.

Q: Entanglement so far covers the pure states in use here. Do mixed states admit the same description? A: Mixed-state entanglement is a more delicate notion and returns later. The tensor-product definition does not carry over as directly as it does for pure states, so defining and quantifying entanglement for mixed states needs extra care.

Q: Once the parties measure, do the two entangled photons become disentangled, with measurement leaving an unentangled product? A: Yes. Measurement collapses the state, and the post-measurement state is no longer entangled. By the collapse rule, a polarization-entangled pair drops to "horizontal, horizontal" or "vertical, vertical", a direct product state. Until measurement happens the pair stays entangled, and while it stays entangled it can serve tasks such as teleportation.

8.2 Bell States

If one entangled pair can correlate two outcomes perfectly, how many fundamentally different ways can two qubits be perfectly correlated? The answer turns out to be exactly four, and those four states show up everywhere from laboratory photon sources to the circuits in the next section.

The previous section defined entanglement and showed its measurement signature. This section names the four states where that signature is strongest: the Bell states. They are the standard examples of maximal entanglement, the standard inputs to teleportation and Bell tests, and the states laboratories work hardest to produce.

8.2.1 The Four Bell States

Bell states are four two-qubit states that are maximally entangled, meaning each one carries the strongest possible quantum correlation between its two qubits. They also go by the name EPR states (EPR pairs). The compact form, with \(i, j \in \{0, 1\}\) and a bar marking a flipped bit (\(\bar{0} = 1\), \(\bar{1} = 0\)), reads

\[ |\psi_{ij}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|0\rangle|j\rangle + (-1)^{i}|1\rangle|\bar{j}\rangle\bigr). \]

In words: "psi i j equals ket zero ket j, plus minus one to the i times ket one ket j-bar, all over root two." Here \(i\) controls the sign (plus when \(i = 0\), minus when \(i = 1\)) and \(j\) controls whether the pair agrees (\(j = 0\) gives \(00\) and \(11\) terms) or disagrees (\(j = 1\) gives \(01\) and \(10\) terms). Written out one by one:

\[ |\psi_{00}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\bigr), \qquad |\psi_{01}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|01\rangle + |10\rangle\bigr), \]

\[ |\psi_{10}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|00\rangle - |11\rangle\bigr), \qquad |\psi_{11}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|01\rangle - |10\rangle\bigr). \]

Walk through \(|\psi_{00}\rangle\) in full. A two-qubit state has four basis strings, yet here \(01\) and \(10\) carry probability zero while \(00\) and \(11\) each carry amplitude \(1/\sqrt{2}\). Squaring gives outcome probabilities \(|1/\sqrt{2}|^2 = 1/2\) each, and the squared amplitudes sum to \(1/2 + 1/2 = 1\), as normalization requires. In \(|\psi_{01}\rangle\) the roles swap: \(01\) and \(10\) carry the weight while \(00\) and \(11\) sit at zero. The remaining two states repeat the pattern with minus signs, which leave the outcome probabilities unchanged but matter for interference, as the Bell-measurement derivation in the next section shows.

The state \(|\psi_{11}\rangle = (|01\rangle - |10\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) is often called the singlet and written \(\psi^-\), meaning the unique two-spin state with total spin zero. It is the state met when a spin-zero particle decays into two spin-half particles, because angular-momentum conservation pins the total spin at zero. It is also the standard state for testing Bell inequalities.

Each Bell state can be checked against the two-qubit determinant test from the previous section. For \(|\psi_{00}\rangle\) the amplitudes are \(\alpha = \delta = 1/\sqrt{2}\) with \(\beta = \gamma = 0\), so \(\alpha\delta - \beta\gamma = 1/2 \neq 0\), confirming entanglement. The same nonzero value appears for all four, and the largest possible size of that combination marks them as maximally entangled.

Recap: the four Bell states are the four maximally entangled two-qubit states, indexed by a sign bit and an agree-or-disagree bit. Bridge: the next step explains what "maximally" means precisely, through the state of either qubit viewed alone.

8.2.2 Maximal Entanglement and the Reduced State

The label maximally entangled earns a concrete meaning through the reduced state, meaning the description of one qubit after ignoring (tracing out) the other. Trace out either qubit of \(|\psi_{00}\rangle\) and the survivor is not a pure state but a mixed state of the largest possible mixedness. As a density matrix \(\rho\), with \(I\) the \(2 \times 2\) identity matrix,

\[ \rho = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{I}{2}. \]

In words: "the reduced density matrix is half the identity." To see where this comes from, form the projector \(|\psi_{00}\rangle\langle\psi_{00}|\) and sum over the second qubit's basis: the \(|00\rangle\langle 00|\) piece contributes \(|0\rangle\langle 0|/2\), the \(|11\rangle\langle 11|\) piece contributes \(|1\rangle\langle 1|/2\), and the cross terms vanish under the trace, leaving \(I/2\).

Think of a coin whose two faces have been separated so that neither side alone shows anything readable, yet bringing the halves together always reveals matching faces. Either qubit viewed alone is like one such half: completely blank, with outcome probabilities \(1/2\) and \(1/2\) in every basis. All information sits in the correlations, not in either qubit by itself. Where the picture breaks: coin halves carry pre-stamped faces, while qubits carry no pre-set outcome, which is exactly the point Bell tests later confirm.

The kept qubit, viewed alone, is as blank as a qubit can be, the maximally mixed single-qubit state. That is exactly why measuring one qubit fixes the other at once: the joint state is perfectly correlated, so one revealed outcome resolves the whole superposition.

The four states split into two measurement families worth keeping apart. States of the form \((|00\rangle \pm |11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) yield outcomes \(00\) and \(11\), which are perfectly correlated. States of the form \((|01\rangle \pm |10\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) yield \(01\) and \(10\), which are perfectly anti-correlated. All four are maximally entangled, each in its own pattern.

Scope: the reduced state \(I/2\) holds for every Bell state, but only for the two-qubit maximally entangled case. For larger systems, maximally entangled states still exist, yet their reduced states and the sense of "maximal" need more machinery. A common trap is reading "one qubit is blank" as "one qubit carries no information at all": the single-qubit statistics are blank, but the joint statistics carry everything.

Recap: Bell states are maximally entangled because each qubit alone is maximally mixed while the pair is perfectly correlated. Bridge: these states are not abstract curiosities, since laboratories build them routinely, which is where the discussion turns next.

8.2.3 Preparing Bell States in the Laboratory

These states are not abstract curiosities; laboratories build them. A preparation needs two qubit systems plus the ability to apply a Hadamard gate and a CNOT gate, and that combination at the output delivers one of the four Bell states, as the circuit section works out in full. One physical carrier is photon polarization, prepared through a process called parametric down-conversion, which emits pairs of photons whose polarizations are correlated: horizontal on the first forces horizontal on the second, vertical forces vertical.

A laboratory setup in an electrical engineering department builds pairs of entangled photons exactly this way.

One party can then keep one photon while sending the partner photon away, down a fiber or another quantum channel, so the two members of the pair finish far apart in space. Local measurements on the separated photons then show perfectly correlated polarizations. The correlations are generated once, at preparation, and they persist across the separation until measurement collapses them.

Worked picture: counting a laboratory run. A source emits one thousand photon pairs in the state \(|\psi_{00}\rangle\). Both sides measure polarization in the horizontal-or-vertical basis. About five hundred rounds read horizontal on both sides and about five hundred read vertical on both sides, with mixed rounds absent up to small experimental error. The two outcome lists look random individually, since each side sees about half horizontal and half vertical, yet the lists match entry by entry. Sense-check: per-side randomness with perfect cross-side agreement is exactly the maximally entangled signature, not independent randomness.

Recap: Bell pairs are made by entangling gates or by photon-pair sources, then shared across distance with correlations intact until measurement. Bridge: the names on these states carry their own history, which explains why the same states are called both Bell and EPR.

8.2.4 The EPR Paper and the Naming of Bell States

The name EPR stands for Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen, the three physicists who first identified and analyzed entanglement. That is why Bell states are also called EPR pairs. Their celebrated 1935 paper in Physical Review proposed that quantum mechanics permits states of exactly this kind, states that refuse factorization into products of local states. Notably, Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen wrote their entangled state not for two-level systems but for position and momentum, two infinite-dimensional spaces whose position and momentum degrees of freedom were entangled, and argued that such states carry properties at odds with the classical picture of systems.

For Einstein in particular this was a bug, not a feature. When state properties resist explanation from classical physics, he held, quantum mechanics must be incomplete, a position he kept to the end. He suspected hidden variables: hidden degrees of freedom, beyond our access, that dictate the apparently random outcomes. That incomplete-theory view is what motivated the hidden-variables idea pursued through the rest of this lecture.

The second name on the states belongs to John S. Bell, a particle physicist at CERN in its early days, the large collider laboratory at Geneva. In 1964 he published the inequality result that carries his name: he studied these entangled states for spin-half systems, and the two-level systems under discussion here are spin systems. Bell took up the story where EPR left off, pushed back against the incompleteness claim, and, most importantly, supplied a way to test for entanglement: he showed that these states carry correlations stronger than anything classical systems allow. That result is why entanglement today is described as quantum correlation.

Recap: EPR named the puzzle and Bell supplied the test, so the same states carry both names. Bridge: the circuits in the next section turn these named states into concrete gate sequences.

8.2.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: With the mathematical description in hand, can we say how entangled a state is, or whether its outcomes correlate or anti-correlate? A: For a two-qubit state the coefficients, call them \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), \(\gamma\), \(\delta\) on the four basis strings, carry the structure, and simple cases surrender to inspection, as with \(|00\rangle + |01\rangle\) factoring into \(|0\rangle \otimes |+\rangle\). Inspection fails for harder cases and beyond qubits, where the dimension growth multiplies the possibilities. Suggested practice includes working out whether a given state is entangled, and that exercise takes real time.

Q: A demonstration over thousands of kilometers must measure many times. Does each measurement consume the pair, so that a fresh pair is needed per round? A: Exactly, and a single pair proves nothing. One measurement collapses one pair into an unentangled product, so confirming correlations needs many identically prepared pairs and then statistics over the outcomes. A machine emitting only horizontally polarized photons would also agree on every round. Only repeated rounds, with horizontal tracking horizontal, vertical tracking vertical, and random choice per round, establish genuine entanglement.

Q: Does the distance between the particles change anything in practice? A: In practice, long travel exposes the photons to noise and decoherence, which disturb the state away from the ideal form, so across one hundred prepared pairs a small fraction may emerge uncorrelated, and that shows up as error. But travel alone does not collapse anything; collapse waits for measurement. In an ideal world free of disturbance, nothing forbids the pair from staying entangled over any distance.

8.3 Preparing and Measuring Bell States with Circuits

The Bell states look like mathematical ideals, but a real device builds them with just two gates. What is the shortest gate sequence that turns an ordinary basis input into a maximally entangled pair, and can the same gates run backward to read the pair out?

The previous section named the four Bell states. This section turns them into hardware: one short circuit prepares any Bell state from a basis input, and the same circuit run backward measures in the Bell basis. The teleportation protocol in the next section reuses both directions, so every algebraic step here pays off there.

8.3.1 The Bell-State Preparation Circuit

A compact two-qubit circuit manufactures any of the four Bell states. Feed it a basis input \(|ij\rangle\) with \(i, j \in \{0, 1\}\): apply a Hadamard gate to the first qubit, then apply a CNOT gate across the pair with the first qubit as control. The promised preparation map is

\[ |ij\rangle \;\xrightarrow{H \text{ on first}}\; \xrightarrow{\text{CNOT}} \; |\psi_{ij}\rangle. \]

In words: "input i j goes to the Bell state psi i j." The CNOT rule used throughout: when the control qubit is \(0\) the second qubit stays as it is; when the control is \(1\) the target flips its bit.

The Hadamard actions needed are \(H|0\rangle = |+\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\), in words "Hadamard on zero gives the plus state," and \(H|1\rangle = |-\rangle = (|0\rangle - |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\), in words "Hadamard on one gives the minus state." The Hadamard creates the superposition while the CNOT spreads it across the two qubits, which is why the order matters: superposition first, entangling second. Reversing the order would entangle before any superposition exists and produce no entanglement at all.

Think of the Hadamard as dealing a coin into the air and the CNOT as handcuffing a second coin to the first mid-flight. After the deal both coins spin together, so reading one fixes the other. Where the picture breaks: handcuffs are a physical link added afterward, while the CNOT creates a joint quantum description in which neither qubit has its own state at all.

Scope: this preparation circuit assumes clean basis inputs and ideal gates. On real hardware, gate errors and decoherence leave the output slightly short of a perfect Bell state, and the preparation must be followed by verification, such as the Bell test discussed later. Assumption: the CNOT control is always the first qubit. Swapping control and target builds a different (though equally entangled) family of states.

Recap: Hadamard on the first qubit plus CNOT across the pair maps each basis input to its matching Bell state. Bridge: the next step traces two of those maps branch by branch to show the mechanism at work.

8.3.2 Worked Preparation Examples

Example 1: input \(|00\rangle\) (that is, \(i = 0\), \(j = 0\)). After the Hadamard on the first qubit, the joint state is \(|+\rangle \otimes |0\rangle\), which expands to

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\bigr) \otimes |0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|00\rangle + |10\rangle\bigr). \]

In words: "root-two in the denominator, zero zero plus one zero." Writing the idle \(|0\rangle\) inside the parentheses distributes it across both terms. Next the CNOT acts branch by branch: in the \(|00\rangle\) branch the control is \(0\), so the target stays \(0\) and the branch is unchanged; in the \(|10\rangle\) branch the control is \(1\), so the target flips \(0 \to 1\), giving \(|11\rangle\). The output is

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\bigr) = |\psi_{00}\rangle. \]

Sense-check: two nonzero amplitudes of \(1/\sqrt{2}\) square to \(1/2 + 1/2 = 1\), and the surviving strings agree in both positions, matching the \(j = 0\) correlated family.

Example 2: input \(|10\rangle\) (that is, \(i = 1\), \(j = 0\)). The Hadamard on \(|1\rangle\) gives the minus state, so after the first stage the joint state is

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|0\rangle - |1\rangle\bigr) \otimes |0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|00\rangle - |10\rangle\bigr). \]

The CNOT then preserves the \(|00\rangle\) branch (control \(0\)) and flips the target in the \(|10\rangle\) branch (control \(1\)), turning \(|10\rangle\) into \(|11\rangle\), so the minus sign rides along onto the flipped term:

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|00\rangle - |11\rangle\bigr) = |\psi_{10}\rangle, \]

matching the table entry for input \(10\). Sense-check: the output strings are the same correlated pair as Example 1, but the relative minus sign marks \(i = 1\), exactly as the compact Bell formula predicts.

The remaining two inputs behave the same way and are set as suggested practice: each yields its matching Bell state through this circuit. Running input \(|01\rangle\) gives \((|01\rangle + |10\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) and input \(|11\rangle\) gives \((|01\rangle - |10\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\), completing the table.

The most common slip is applying the CNOT rule to the wrong qubit: the flip always hits the target (second qubit) conditioned on the control (first qubit). A second trap is dropping the minus sign when the input is \(|1\rangle\): the Hadamard minus state carries it, and the CNOT preserves it, so \(i = 1\) inputs always produce minus-sign Bell states.

8.3.3 The Bell Measurement Circuit

Measurement inverts preparation: given a Bell state \(|\psi_{ij}\rangle\), recover the bits \(i\) and \(j\). The circuit is the preparation circuit run in reverse, first the CNOT, then the Hadamard on the first qubit, followed by measurement of both qubits. A barrier symbol in the diagram marks that both measurements happen only after the Hadamard stage, never right after the CNOT. Classical wires then carry the two measured bits out. The claim: measuring the first qubit returns \(i\) and measuring the second returns \(j\).

Worked measurement of \(|\psi_{00}\rangle = (|00\rangle + |11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\). After the CNOT stage, the control logic flips the target wherever the first qubit is \(1\): the \(|00\rangle\) branch (control \(0\)) is unchanged, while the \(|11\rangle\) branch (control \(1\)) has its target flipped \(1 \to 0\), becoming \(|10\rangle\). The intermediate state is

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|00\rangle + |10\rangle\bigr). \]

For readability the shorthand \(|00\rangle\) here means \(|0\rangle \otimes |0\rangle\), and likewise for the other branch. Next the Hadamard hits the first qubit of each branch: \(|0\rangle \to (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) and \(|1\rangle \to (|0\rangle - |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\). Pulling the common \(1/\sqrt{2}\) factors out gives an overall \(1/2\) times

\[ |00\rangle + |10\rangle + |00\rangle - |10\rangle. \]

In words: "half times zero zero plus one zero plus zero zero minus one zero." The \(+|10\rangle\) and \(-|10\rangle\) terms cancel, leaving \(2|00\rangle\) times \(1/2\), that is, \(|00\rangle\) with certainty. The final state measures \(00\) with probability \(1\) (a two-qubit system in general could return \(00\), \(01\), \(10\), or \(11\), but here only \(00\) survives). Reading the first qubit gives \(0 = i\) and the second gives \(0 = j\). Each of the other three Bell inputs lands with certainty on its own entry in the same input-output table. Sense-check: a cancelling pair plus a doubling pair is the interference mechanism in miniature, previewing the larger algorithm story later.

To picture the reversal, imagine recording the preparation recipe and playing the tape backward: untying the handcuffs (CNOT) before landing the coin (Hadamard) returns both coins to their definite starting faces. The barrier in the diagram is the director's mark that says no measurement may roll before the final gate finishes.

Recap: CNOT then Hadamard then measurement maps each Bell state back onto its basis input with certainty, with the barrier enforcing the gate order. Bridge: both circuit directions reunite in teleportation, where the same CNOT-first ordering opens the protocol.

8.3.4 The Teleportation Circuit Preview

The same two gates return in teleportation in swapped order: a CNOT first, then a Hadamard, unlike preparation, which applies the Hadamard first and the CNOT second. The teleportation circuit then measures twice, producing classical bits \(m_1\) and \(m_2\), each \(0\) or \(1\), which travel to the receiver for corrections. The full protocol, with its three-qubit derivation, is worked in the next section.

Recap: preparation builds Bell states going forward while Bell measurement reads them going backward, and teleportation chains the backward direction into a larger protocol. Bridge: the next section runs that protocol end to end.

8.4 Quantum Teleportation

Suppose you must deliver a sealed letter whose contents even you are not allowed to read, and the only tools are a pre-shared secret code plus an ordinary phone call. How could the recipient reconstruct the letter exactly while your own copy is destroyed in the process?

That puzzle is teleportation. It moves an unknown qubit state across an arbitrarily long distance using prior entanglement plus ordinary communication, without moving any particle and without learning the state. The preparation and measurement circuits from the previous section supply the machinery; this section assembles them into the full three-qubit protocol.

8.4.1 The Setup

Teleportation here is not fiction: it moves an unknown qubit state across an arbitrarily long distance using prior entanglement plus ordinary communication. Party \(A\) and party \(B\) first take part in producing a Bell state \(|\psi_{ij}\rangle\) together. \(A\) keeps the first qubit of the pair and \(B\) keeps the second; \(B\) then moves far away. Although the shared state is sometimes tagged with subscripts \(A\) and \(B\) to mark ownership, those tags are dropped for convenience, while the split ownership stays understood. Because the pair is entangled, the separation does not break the entanglement by itself. Bell states are also called EPR pairs after Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen, the three scientists who first identified and analyzed entanglement.

Then \(A\) receives a new single qubit \(|\phi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\), where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are its probability amplitudes, meaning complex numbers with \(|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1\) whose squared sizes give the outcome probabilities. In words: "alpha zero plus beta one." \(A\) does not know \(\alpha\) or \(\beta\), since a third party may well have prepared the state, and one measurement cannot reveal them, since measuring the lone copy collapses it to \(0\) or \(1\) and destroys the superposition. \(A\) cannot travel to hand the qubit over physically. All \(A\) can use is the pre-shared entangled pair plus a classical channel, ordinary networking of the kind computers use daily, that carries two measured bits to \(B\). From those two bits plus his own half of the pair, \(B\) reconstructs \(|\phi\rangle\).

Think of the entangled pair as two blank fax pages manufactured together so that whatever is written on one during the protocol appears transformed on the other. The unknown qubit is the original page, the two classical bits are the fax transmission, and \(B\)'s correction gates are the printout step. Where the picture breaks: a fax leaves the original intact, while teleportation destroys \(A\)'s copy, which is exactly what the no-cloning theorem in the next section demands.

Recap: teleportation needs three ingredients: a pre-shared Bell pair, one unknown qubit, and a two-bit classical channel. Bridge: the next step lays out the gate sequence that combines them.

8.4.2 The Circuit

Three qubits take part: \(|\phi\rangle\) (the state to send), \(A\)'s half of the pair, and \(B\)'s half. Label them \(A_1\) (unknown state), \(A_2\) (\(A\)'s entangled half), and \(B\) (\(B\)'s half). \(A\) applies a CNOT between \(A_1\) (control) and \(A_2\) (target), follows with a Hadamard on \(A_1\), and measures both qubits she holds, producing bits \(m_1\) and \(m_2\). The two classical outcomes travel to \(B\); picture \(A\) calling out the two bit values over a telephone. \(B\) has done nothing until now. Depending on the received bits, \(B\) applies gates to his qubit: \(X^{m_2}\) (a bit-flip Pauli-\(X\) gate when \(m_2 = 1\), nothing when \(m_2 = 0\), since \(X^0\) is the identity) and \(Z^{m_1}\) (a phase-flip Pauli-\(Z\) gate when \(m_1 = 1\), nothing when \(m_1 = 0\)). After the corrections, \(B\)'s qubit sits in \(|\phi\rangle\). The state has vanished from \(A\)'s side, since her measurements collapsed it, and appeared at \(B\)'s side. Counting qubits confirms the picture: the run starts with two pair qubits plus one new arrival, and ends with \(A\)'s two measured and \(B\)'s one carrying \(|\phi\rangle\).

Scope: the protocol assumes the entangled pair is a known pure Bell state and the classical channel is reliable. If the pair has degraded through noise, the corrections land on a distorted state. A common trap is thinking the classical bits carry the amplitudes: they carry only two bits, far too little to describe \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), and the continuous information travels through the entanglement, unlocked by those bits.

Recap: CNOT, Hadamard, measure twice, phone the bits, correct with \(X^{m_2}\) and \(Z^{m_1}\). Bridge: the next step derives why those corrections work, tracking all eight three-qubit strings.

8.4.3 Worked Derivation with the Shared State \(|\psi_{00}\rangle\)

Take the shared pair to be \(|\psi_{00}\rangle = (|00\rangle + |11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\). The three-qubit input, call it \(|\chi_1\rangle\), is the tensor product of \(|\phi\rangle\) with the pair:

\[ |\chi_1\rangle = \bigl(\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\bigr) \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\bigr). \]

In words: "alpha zero plus beta one, tensored with zero zero plus one one over root two." Distributing gives \((1/\sqrt{2})\) times \(\alpha|0\rangle \otimes (|00\rangle + |11\rangle)\) plus \(\beta|1\rangle \otimes (|00\rangle + |11\rangle)\). This intermediate expansion carries \(A\)'s unknown amplitudes into a three-qubit register.

Next the CNOT acts on \(|\phi\rangle\) (control) and \(A\)'s pair member (target); \(B\)'s qubit rides along untouched. Arrange each term with the first qubit called out separately from the second-plus-third pair. Where the first qubit is \(0\), the second stays put (\(0 \to 0\), \(1 \to 1\)); where the first is \(1\), the second flips (\(0 \to 1\), \(1 \to 0\)). The result, \(|\chi_2\rangle\), reads

\[ |\chi_2\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigl[\alpha|0\rangle\otimes\bigl(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\bigr) + \beta|1\rangle\otimes\bigl(|10\rangle + |01\rangle\bigr)\Bigr]. \]

In words: "one over root two, alpha zero with zero zero plus one one, plus beta one with one zero plus zero one."

Then the Hadamard hits the first qubit only, producing \(|\chi_3\rangle\). The second and third qubits are untouched, so the bracketed pairs above persist while \(|0\rangle \to (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) (the plus state) and \(|1\rangle \to (|0\rangle - |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) (the minus state). Factoring the new \(1/\sqrt{2}\) against the existing one gives an overall \(1/2\). Expanding fully produces all eight three-qubit strings from \(000\) to \(111\):

\[ |\chi_3\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\alpha\bigl(|000\rangle + |100\rangle + |011\rangle + |111\rangle\bigr) + \beta\bigl(|010\rangle - |110\rangle + |001\rangle - |101\rangle\bigr)\Bigr]. \]

In words: "one half, alpha over the four strings with leading plus signs, beta over the four strings with mixed signs." As a concrete check, set \(\alpha = 3/5\) and \(\beta = 4/5\): each of the eight strings then carries probability \((1/4)(9/25) = 9/100\) or \((1/4)(16/25) = 16/100\), and the eight probabilities sum to \(4 \times 9/100 + 4 \times 16/100 = 1\), confirming normalization.

Regroup by \(A\)'s two qubits to expose what \(B\) holds for each of \(A\)'s four possible readings:

\[ |\chi_3\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl[|00\rangle \otimes \bigl(\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\bigr) + |01\rangle \otimes \bigl(\alpha|1\rangle + \beta|0\rangle\bigr) + |10\rangle \otimes \bigl(\alpha|0\rangle - \beta|1\rangle\bigr) + |11\rangle \otimes \bigl(\alpha|1\rangle - \beta|0\rangle\bigr)\Bigr]. \]

In words: "one half times zero zero with alpha zero plus beta one, zero one with the flipped pair, one zero with the phased pair, and one one with both changes." Call \(B\)'s current piece \(|\phi'\rangle\), meaning his state after all transformations but before \(A\)'s message arrives. The table linking \(A\)'s reading to \(|\phi'\rangle\) is: \(00 \to \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\); \(01 \to \alpha|1\rangle + \beta|0\rangle\); \(10 \to \alpha|0\rangle - \beta|1\rangle\); \(11 \to \alpha|1\rangle - \beta|0\rangle\). The original \(|\phi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\) is what \(B\) must finally infer, while \(|\phi'\rangle\) is only the raw material. Sense-check: each reading occurs with probability \(1/4\) regardless of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), so \(A\)'s measurement reveals nothing about the unknown amplitudes, exactly as required.

8.4.4 Bob Corrections and Other Shared States

Each row converts to \(|\phi\rangle\) with at most two single-qubit gates:

  • Reading \(00\): \(B\) already holds \(\alpha\vert 0\rangle + \beta\vert 1\rangle = \vert\phi\rangle\). No gate needed.
  • Reading \(01\): \(B\) holds \(\alpha\vert 1\rangle + \beta\vert 0\rangle\). A bit flip (\(1 \leftrightarrow 0\)), performed by a Pauli-\(X\) gate, returns \(\alpha\vert 0\rangle + \beta\vert 1\rangle\).
  • Reading \(10\): \(B\) holds \(\alpha\vert 0\rangle - \beta\vert 1\rangle\). A phase flip, performed by a Pauli-\(Z\) gate, returns \(\vert\phi\rangle\).
  • Reading \(11\): \(B\) holds \(\alpha\vert 1\rangle - \beta\vert 0\rangle\). Both a bit flip and a phase flip, an \(X\) gate plus a \(Z\) gate, return \(\vert\phi\rangle\).

Exam note: the correction pattern (identity for \(00\), \(X\) for \(01\), \(Z\) for \(10\), \(X\) plus \(Z\) for \(11\)) is core recall material: the second bit drives the bit flip and the first bit drives the phase flip.

Nothing forces the shared pair to be \(|\psi_{00}\rangle\): \(A\) and \(B\) may start from any of the four Bell states and still teleport, with identical algebra throughout, while only the correction table changes, assigning a different mix of \(X\) and \(Z\) gates per reading. Working one alternative pair through is set as suggested practice.

A frequent mix-up swaps which bit drives which gate: \(m_2\) drives \(X\) and \(m_1\) drives \(Z\). Another trap is applying the corrections in an order that drops a sign: \(X\) and \(Z\) anti-commute, so \(XZ = -ZX\), and while the overall phase is unobservable for a single qubit, keeping the lecture's stated order \(X^{m_2}\) then \(Z^{m_1}\) avoids confusion when the teleportation sits inside a larger circuit.

8.4.5 Entanglement Swapping, Experiments, and the Speed Limit

A twist called entanglement swapping reassigns who shares with whom. Suppose \(B\) and \(A\) share one pair \(|\psi_{ij}\rangle\) while \(A\) and a third party \(C\) share a different pair \(|\psi_{kl}\rangle\). Because \(A\) holds one member of each pair, she can teleport her \(B\)-linked member to \(C\) using the \(A\)–\(C\) pair as the channel. The endpoint: \(B\) and \(C\) finish sharing \(|\psi_{ij}\rangle\), the state once held between \(B\) and \(A\). The \(A\)–\(C\) link is consumed in the process.

In one experiment in China a qubit was teleported from the ground to a satellite 1400 kilometers overhead, using a pre-shared pair between the two endpoints. Swapping itself was shown between two of the Canary Islands in the Atlantic Ocean. A later satellite setup generated entangled photon pairs in orbit and beamed them to two ground stations thousands of kilometers apart, where polarization readings stayed perfectly correlated.

None of this outruns light. The readings \(m_1, m_2\) travel over a classical channel, regular networking that cannot exceed light speed, so the whole protocol stays capped by classical capacity even though the entanglement itself spans the distance. That resolution also answers the "spooky action" worry at the observer level: the particles carry hidden correlations, but no observer gains faster-than-light signaling, because nothing is usable until the classical message arrives. The state vanishes at the sender and appears at the receiver only via that classical message.

Recap: swapping chains teleportation to link strangers, experiments confirm it up to satellite scales, and the classical channel keeps everything within light speed. Bridge: teleportation destroys the original by necessity, and the next section proves that destruction is a law, not an engineering limit.

8.4.6 Student Questions and Answers

Q: The teleportation circuit was drawn but not yet analyzed. How do the three registers line up, and what exactly does \(B\) apply? A: The three registers are \(A_1\) (the unknown \(|\phi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\)), \(A_2\) (\(A\)'s half of the entangled pair), and \(B\) (\(B\)'s half). \(A\) runs CNOT then Hadamard on her two, measures them to bits \(m_1, m_2\), and phones the bits to \(B\). \(B\) applies \(X^{m_2}\) and \(Z^{m_1}\), so each gate fires only when its bit is \(1\), and his qubit lands in the unknown state.

8.5 The No-Cloning Theorem

A photocopier duplicates any page placed on its glass. Why should a quantum copier be impossible even in principle, when classical copying is the most routine operation in computing?

Teleportation moved a state by destroying the original. This section explains why that destruction is mandatory: no quantum circuit copies an arbitrary unknown qubit. The ban shapes everything from teleportation to Bell tests, since all of them work around it with many identically prepared copies instead of duplicated ones.

8.5.1 Statement and the Failed CNOT Copier

No quantum circuit copies an arbitrary unknown qubit. Formally, there is no unitary transformation \(U\) with \(U|\psi\rangle|s\rangle = |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle\) for every \(|\psi\rangle\), where \(|s\rangle\) is a fixed blank state the circuit always uses. In words: "no single unitary clones every psi." Classical computing copies freely, since moving bits between memory and storage is routine, but quantum copying of an unknown state is impossible. This is a genuine limitation of quantum computing, not a gap in engineering. Copying unknown states stays impossible, unlike classical memory moves.

Worked failure: the CNOT copier. A first candidate, the CNOT gate, fails on inspection. Suppose CNOT could copy a control qubit \(|q_1\rangle\) onto a target, so that both outputs equal \(|q_1\rangle\). The CNOT target output equals the XOR \(q_1 \oplus q_2\), so copying demands \(q_1 \oplus q_2 = q_1\), which forces a definite relation solvable only when the control is \(|0\rangle\) or \(|1\rangle\). Concretely, feeding \(|00\rangle\) gives \(|00\rangle\) and feeding \(|10\rangle\) gives \(|11\rangle\), so basis states copy fine. But an arbitrary superposition \(\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\) with blank target \(|0\rangle\) goes to \(\alpha|00\rangle + \beta|11\rangle\), which is entangled, not the product \(|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle = \alpha^2|00\rangle + \alpha\beta|01\rangle + \alpha\beta|10\rangle + \beta^2|11\rangle\). Sense-check: the CNOT output lacks the cross terms \(|01\rangle\) and \(|10\rangle\) entirely, so it cannot match the cloned product except when one amplitude vanishes.

Nor does teleportation refute the result: \(A\)'s measurement collapses her \(|\phi\rangle\) to some reading \(m\), destroying her copy, so only \(B\) ends with \(|\phi\rangle\). Teleportation is a transfer, not a copy.

Think of an unknown quantum state as a soap bubble and copying as grabbing it: any grip that reads its shape pops it. Classical bits are like printed pages that survive the copier; qubits pop on contact. Where the picture breaks: bubbles pop randomly, while quantum measurement follows precise probabilistic rules, and the no-cloning proof makes those rules do the work.

Recap: no single unitary clones every state, the CNOT copies only basis states, and teleportation transfers rather than copies. Bridge: the next step proves the ban for every possible unitary, not just the CNOT.

8.5.2 Proof from Inner Products

Assume, toward contradiction, that a cloning unitary \(U\) exists. It must clone any two states, so for states \(|\psi\rangle\) and \(|\varphi\rangle\):

\[ U|\psi\rangle|s\rangle = |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle, \qquad U|\varphi\rangle|s\rangle = |\varphi\rangle|\varphi\rangle. \]

In words: "U on psi and blank gives two psis; U on phi and blank gives two phis." Take inner products of both sides: bra on the left becomes conjugate transpose, so \(U\) becomes \(U^\dagger\), the ket \(|\psi\rangle\) becomes the bra \(\langle\psi|\), and \(|s\rangle\) becomes \(\langle s|\). Writing the bra equation as \(\langle\psi|\langle s|U^\dagger\) and overlapping it with the ket equation, the left side collapses because \(U^\dagger U\) is the identity for unitary matrices, leaving \(\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle\langle s|s\rangle = \langle\psi|\varphi\rangle\), since the blank state is normalized. The right side becomes \(\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle^2\), because each of the two cloned factors contributes one overlap. Hence

\[ \langle\psi|\varphi\rangle = \bigl(\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle\bigr)^2. \]

In words: "the overlap equals its own square," or with the conjugate transpose collapsing the unitary, the overlap equals the squared overlap. A number equal to its own square is \(0\) or \(1\), so either \(|\psi\rangle = |\varphi\rangle\) or the two are orthogonal. The assumed machine clones at most one pair of orthogonal states, never an arbitrary qubit with free amplitudes. That contradiction establishes the theorem. Filling the middle algebra line by line is set as suggested practice.

Scope: the proof bans cloning of unknown arbitrary states. Known states are a different matter: a circuit built for one specific state can prepare as many copies as wanted, and orthogonal basis states can be copied by the CNOT as shown above. The trap is over-reading the theorem as "nothing quantum can ever be duplicated": what cannot be duplicated is an unknown state handed to a fixed universal copier.

Recap: unitarity forces the overlap to equal its square, which only identical or orthogonal states satisfy, so no universal cloner exists. Bridge: the ban sounds severe, but practice routes around it with statistics over many preparations.

8.5.3 Why Copying Matters Less Than It Seems

Classical intuition treats copying as the most basic operation, so the ban feels severe. In quantum routines the workaround is statistical: rather than duplicating one precious copy, experimenters prepare many identical copies of a state and gather measurement statistics across them, the same many-copy logic that underpins entanglement verification and Bell tests below. One measurement of one copy yields one bit; a thousand measurements of a thousand fresh copies reveal the distribution.

Recap: the no-cloning rule forbids duplicating the unknown, but preparing many identical originals and counting outcomes recovers everything experiments need. Bridge: interference, the third quantum effect, is what those many-shot experiments amplify.

8.6 Interference and the Shape of Quantum Algorithms

Entanglement links qubits together, but linking alone does not compute anything. What mechanism steers a giant superposition toward right answers and away from wrong ones?

The lecture so far used two of the three quantum effects: superposition spreads the register across many strings and entanglement correlates them. This section adds the third effect, interference, and shows the skeleton every quantum algorithm hangs on. Later routines follow this skeleton with clever gate mixes that beat classical approaches.

8.6.1 Interference

Interference is the third quantum property, familiar from school physics. Any quantum object shows wave-particle duality, meaning treated as a wave, it interferes. Recall Young's double-slit experiment: a wave crossing two nearby slits paints light and dark fringes on a far screen. Aligned phases add amplitudes, which is constructive interference, the bright bands. Opposed phases cancel amplitudes, which is destructive interference, the dark bands.

Every superposition qubit carries a relative phase, meaning the angle between its amplitudes that decides whether two paths add or cancel, and well-chosen gates impose deliberate phase shifts on chosen components. The effect is selective: wanted states in the large superposition space grow (amplification) while unwanted ones shrink. Any general quantum algorithm hunts answers this way: it picks out the states of the \(2^n\) space that encode correct answers. One algorithm to keep in mind where interference does visible work is Grover's algorithm, a search routine met in later weeks that turns phase flips plus reflection steps into a quadratic speedup for unstructured search.

Picture interference as ripples from two stones dropped in a pond. Where crest meets crest the water rises higher; where crest meets trough the surface goes flat. Quantum gates place the stones so that right-answer ripples meet crest to crest while wrong-answer ripples cancel flat. Where the picture breaks: water waves spread continuously, while quantum amplitudes live on discrete basis strings and cancel exactly by arithmetic.

To picture it as a chart, draw amplitude on the vertical axis against basis strings on the horizontal axis. Before interference all bars stand equally tall. After the phase-and-mix sequence the bars over right answers tower while the bars over wrong answers shrink toward zero. The takeaway: interference redistributes fixed total probability toward the answers.

Worked miniature: two-path interference. Send one qubit through a Hadamard, a phase flip on \(|1\rangle\), and a second Hadamard. Start from \(|0\rangle\): the first Hadamard gives \((|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\); the phase flip signs the second term to \((|0\rangle - |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\); the second Hadamard maps \(|0\rangle \to (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) and \(|1\rangle \to (|0\rangle - |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\), so the \(|1\rangle\) pieces cancel and the \(|0\rangle\) pieces double, returning \(|0\rangle\) with certainty. Flip the phase pattern and the output lands on \(|1\rangle\) instead. Sense-check: the same gates with no phase flip return \(|0\rangle\) by direct Hadamard cancellation, so the phase is what steers the outcome, which is the whole trick in miniature.

Scope: interference needs coherence: the relative phases must survive until the amplifying gates act. Decoherence scrambles phases into noise, and then the cancellation pattern washes out. A common trap is crediting superposition alone for speedups: spreading across answers without interference leaves a uniform distribution that measures a random string, gaining nothing.

Recap: interference amplifies right answers and cancels wrong ones through controlled phase shifts, with the double slit as the prototype and Grover search as the flagship. Bridge: the next step fits interference into the full algorithm skeleton.

8.6.2 The Intuition Behind Any Quantum Algorithm

A fixed pattern underlies algorithm construction for an arbitrary problem. First fix the state space and inputs: choose the qubit count \(n\) and the input values, usually starting every input in \(|0\rangle\). Apply the Hadamard transform to all inputs at once; the joint gate is the \(n\)-fold tensor product \(H^{\otimes n}\), a \(2^n \times 2^n\) matrix, and it converts the all-zero input into an equal superposition of all \(2^n\) basis combinations, each with equal probability \(1/2^n\). Then a designed gate sequence transforms that superposition, using entanglement and interference to bias the correct answers upward and the wrong ones downward. Finally measure all \(n\) qubits, yielding one classical \(n\)-bit string per run. Because one measurement returns one definite string while the underlying superposition holds probabilities, many repetitions ("shots") are needed; the probability distribution, and hence the inferred superposition, emerges from the tallied outcomes.

Concretely, for \(n = 3\) the opening Hadamard layer maps \(|000\rangle\) to an equal superposition of all eight strings \(000\) through \(111\), each with amplitude \(1/\sqrt{8}\) and probability \(1/8\). The middle gate sequence then reshapes those eight amplitudes, and the final measurement samples from the reshaped distribution. Later routines follow this skeleton with clever gate mixes that beat classical approaches: the skeleton stays fixed while the middle sequence carries the ingenuity.

Recap: prepare equal superposition, entangle and interfere toward right answers, measure, and repeat for statistics. Bridge: the rest of the lecture replays how this quantum picture defeated its classical challengers, starting with the EPR paradox.

8.7 The EPR Paradox, Local Realism, and Hidden Variables

If measuring one particle instantly fixes the state of another faraway particle, has something travelled between them, or were both outcomes decided in advance? Einstein insisted the second answer must hold, and this section replays his challenge in full.

The circuits so far treated entanglement as a resource. This section rewinds to the argument over what entanglement means: the reality criterion, the split into locality plus realism, Bohm's spin recast, and the tilted-basis math that later feeds the quantitative tests.

8.7.1 Einstein Reality Criterion and the Momentum Example

Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen questioned the Copenhagen reading of quantum mechanics in their celebrated 1935 paper. Copenhagen holds that a physical quantity has no meaning apart from its measurement, so its value fixes at the instant of measuring. Einstein countered with an element of physical reality: "if, without in any way disturbing a system, we can predict with certainty (probability \(1\)) the value of a physical quantity, then there exists an element of physical reality corresponding to that quantity." In his view the measurement reveals a pre-encoded value rather than creating one.

A concrete thought experiment makes the clash sharp. A particle at rest (momentum zero) disintegrates into two equal-mass fragments flying apart along the \(x\) direction. Internal disintegration conserves momentum, so the pair's total momentum stays zero, and the fixed center of mass ties their positions together. Measure the momentum of fragment 2 and momentum conservation fixes fragment 1's momentum at the negative value; measure the position of fragment 1 and the center-of-mass condition fixes fragment 2's position. Both a position and a momentum for one particle become known at once, exactly what Heisenberg's uncertainty principle forbids for a single particle. The uncertainty rule restricts two measurements on the same particle, yet here each measurement touches a different particle while conservation laws bridge the gap.

Think of the fragment pair as two envelopes mailed from one office with a rule that their contents always sum to zero. Opening one envelope tells you the other without touching it. Einstein's claim: both envelopes held definite slips all along. Copenhagen's reply: neither slip existed until an envelope was opened. Where the picture breaks: envelopes can be steamed open and resealed without trace, while quantum measurement collapses the state for good.

Recap: conservation laws let distant measurements jointly pin down both position and momentum, challenging the Copenhagen view that values form only at measurement. Bridge: Einstein's demand splits into two named requirements, locality and realism.

8.7.2 Local Realism and Hidden Variables

Einstein's demand splits into two requirements jointly called local realism. Realism: objects carry properties independent of measurement; measuring only reveals what was already encoded, since the moon exists whether anyone looks. Locality: measuring one member of a far-apart pair cannot influence the other member's properties, in particular when the two measurements finish inside the light-travel time between them (a space-like separation), so no signal could have crossed. A theory satisfying both would be understandable in Einstein's sense; the suspected encoding goes by the name hidden variables, meaning system variables, hidden from view and understanding, that pre-ordain outcomes.

The moon line is the professor's shorthand for realism: the moon is there whether anyone looks, and spin values should be likewise definite before measurement. Locality adds the speed limit: with space-like separated measurements, even light cannot carry the news, so each side's outcome must have been fixed locally in advance.

Scope: local realism is a claim about all measurements at once, which is why the Bell test later needs a joint distribution over four observables. A theory can abandon either leg: standard quantum mechanics keeps locality at the signaling level but gives up realism about pre-set values, while non-local hidden-variable models keep realism and pay with faster-than-light machinery. A common trap is equating "local" with "no correlations": local theories allow correlations from shared past causes, and only ban correlations that need cross-talk at measurement time.

Recap: local realism joins pre-set values with no cross-talk, predicting hidden variables behind the randomness. Bridge: Bohm's spin version turns the abstract demand into a concrete singlet state.

8.7.3 Bohm Spin Version and Perfect Anti-Correlation

David Bohm recast the paradox in 1951 with spins, and the recast is routine physics: spin-zero systems decaying into pairs of spin-half particles abound in nuclear and particle physics. Conservation of angular momentum pins the pair in the singlet

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle - |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle\bigr) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(|01\rangle - |10\rangle\bigr) = |\psi^-\rangle, \]

where \(|\uparrow\rangle = |0\rangle\) and \(|\downarrow\rangle = |1\rangle\) mark spin up and down along \(z\). In words: "up down minus down up, over root two." Suppose \(A\) holds particle 1 and \(B\) holds particle 2, far apart. If \(A\) measures \(S_z\) and finds \(+\hbar/2\) (Pauli eigenvalue \(\sigma_z = +1\)), then \(B\), measuring along \(z\), is guaranteed \(-\hbar/2\) (\(\sigma_z = -1\)). Up on one side always pairs with down on the other: perfect anti-correlation.

Worked check: repeating the \(z\) measurement. Prepare one thousand singlet pairs and measure both sides along \(z\). About five hundred rounds read up on \(A\)'s side and down on \(B\)'s side, and about five hundred read the reverse, with agreeing rounds absent. Each side alone looks like fair coin tosses, yet the two lists are exact opposites entry by entry. Sense-check: the minus sign in the singlet never affects these probabilities, but it becomes load-bearing once tilted bases enter, as the next subsection shows.

Recap: the singlet pins opposite spins along any shared axis by conservation, giving perfect anti-correlation. Bridge: the paradox sharpens when the two sides measure along different axes.

8.7.4 Measurements Along Arbitrary Directions

Give \(B\) a general direction \(\mathbf{n} = (\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\theta)\), in words "sin theta cos phi, sin theta sin phi, cos theta," tilted by \(\theta\) from \(z\). The eigenstates of the Pauli component \(\sigma_{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) for eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\) are, up to an overall phase,

\[ |+\mathbf{n}\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle, \qquad |-\mathbf{n}\rangle = \sin\frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle - e^{i\phi}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle, \]

often written symmetrically by pulling out \(e^{\pm i\phi/2}\). Distinct eigenvalues demand orthogonal eigenstates, hence the minus sign; normalization is understood throughout, since \(\cos^2(\theta/2) + \sin^2(\theta/2) = 1\).

Suppose \(A\) found spin up along \(z\), so \(B\)'s \(z\)-down state \(|1\rangle\) is the live one. Expanding it in \(B\)'s tilted basis gives

\[ |1\rangle = \sin\frac{\theta}{2}|+\mathbf{n}\rangle - \cos\frac{\theta}{2}|-\mathbf{n}\rangle. \]

In words: "one equals sin theta-half times n-plus, minus cos theta-half times n-minus." To verify, substitute the tilted states and collect \(|0\rangle\) terms: \(\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2) - \cos(\theta/2)\sin(\theta/2) = 0\), and \(|1\rangle\) terms: \(\sin^2(\theta/2)e^{i\phi} + \cos^2(\theta/2)e^{i\phi} = e^{i\phi}\), which is \(|1\rangle\) up to the overall phase \(e^{i\phi}\). \(B\) records the \(|-\mathbf{n}\rangle\) branch with probability \(\cos^2(\theta/2)\). The anti-correlation is no longer perfect, but correlation persists at a calculable strength. At \(\theta = 0\) the probability is \(1\) (full anti-correlation); at \(\theta = \pi\) it is \(0\) (outcomes agree); at \(\theta = \pi/2\) it is \(1/2\) (no correlation).

The singlet also obeys a compact operator identity used heavily below: for each Pauli component \(i \in \{x, y, z\}\), with subscripts \(A\), \(B\) marking the particle,

\[ \bigl(\sigma_{iA} + \sigma_{iB}\bigr)|\psi^-\rangle = 0. \]

In words: "the sum of the i-th Pauli on A and on B annihilates psi-minus." Check \(i = x\): \(\sigma_x\) flips \(|0\rangle \leftrightarrow |1\rangle\), so \(\sigma_{xA}\) on \((|01\rangle - |10\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) gives \((|11\rangle - |00\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) while \(\sigma_{xB}\) gives \((|00\rangle - |11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\), and the sum vanishes. Check \(i = z\): \(\sigma_z\) leaves \(|0\rangle\) alone and signs \(|1\rangle\), so \(\sigma_{zA}\) yields \((|01\rangle + |10\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) and \(\sigma_{zB}\) yields \((-|01\rangle - |10\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\), again canceling. The \(i = y\) check works the same way with the phase rules \(\sigma_y|0\rangle = i|1\rangle\) and \(\sigma_y|1\rangle = -i|0\rangle\). The consequence: \(\sigma_{iA}\) acting on \(|\psi^-\rangle\) equals \(-\sigma_{iB}\) on it, so a \(B\)-side operator can be traded for an \(A\)-side one with a sign flip.

To picture the tilt, imagine two compasses that always point opposite when held flat together, but one compass is tipped by \(\theta\). The needles still favor opposition, weakening smoothly to randomness at a right angle and flipping to agreement when upside down. The takeaway: correlation strength follows \(\cos^2(\theta/2)\), not an on-or-off switch.

Two slips recur here. First, the tilted eigenstates carry a phase convention: different texts distribute \(e^{\pm i\phi/2}\) differently, but all choices describe the same physical directions, since overall phases are unobservable. Second, the trading identity \(\sigma_{iB}|\psi^-\rangle = -\sigma_{iA}|\psi^-\rangle\) holds only on the singlet, not as an operator identity in general.

Recap: tilted bases weaken anti-correlation smoothly to \(\cos^2(\theta/2)\), and the Pauli-sum identity lets later derivations trade sides with a sign flip. Bridge: the student questions below revisit the measurement-versus-separation distinction before the Bell test quantifies it.

8.7.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: After one side measures, is the pair disentangled, and until measurement, does separation alone break anything? A: The pair disentangles exactly at measurement: the collapsed "horizontal, horizontal" or "vertical, vertical" outcome is a product. Before that, distance alone breaks nothing in the ideal case; travel exposes the pair to noise and decoherence, which degrade correlations into errors, but the entanglement itself survives until someone measures.

Q: Reports of long-distance entanglement always involve many repeated rounds. Why are many pairs needed? A: Because one round proves nothing and measurement consumes the pair. A source emitting only fixed horizontal photons would also agree every round. Only many pairs plus outcome statistics, random per round yet always matching across sides, show the real thing.

8.8 The Bell-CHSH Inequality and Its Violation

Local realism sounds reasonable: pre-set values plus no cross-talk. But what if a tabletop experiment could force every such theory to predict one number while quantum mechanics predicts a larger one? This section builds that trap and springs it.

The EPR story so far is philosophy plus one paradox. Bell turned it into arithmetic: a ceiling every local-realistic theory must respect, a quantum setup that smashes through it, and an explanation of which assumption breaks. Clauser, Horne, Shimony, and Holt supplied the testable form in 1969, building on Bell's 1964 result.

8.8.1 The Classical Bound

Consider a general physical theory, with no quantum mechanics assumed, with four binary observables \(P, Q, R, S\), each returning \(+1\) or \(-1\) (spin values, two-level energies, light polarizations, and the like). Two are measured in laboratory \(A\), two in laboratory \(B\), on a shared two-particle state. Two assumptions frame the test. Realism: \(P, Q, R, S\) are objective properties and measurement merely reveals preset values, since the moon exists whether or not anyone looks. Locality: the laboratories are causally disconnected, so choices and outcomes on one side cannot affect the other; the two sides may be pictured as measuring simultaneously, and one joint probability distribution \(\Pr(p, q, r, s)\) over the little-letter outcomes \(p, q, r, s\) legitimately covers all four.

Form the combination

\[ M = PR + QR - PS + QS = (P + Q)R + (Q - P)S. \]

In words: "M is P R plus Q R minus P S plus Q S." Since \(P\) and \(Q\) are each \(\pm 1\), one of the brackets \((P + Q)\), \((Q - P)\) is always \(0\) while the other is \(\pm 2\). Check both cases: if \(P = Q\) then \(P + Q = \pm 2\) and \(Q - P = 0\), giving \(M = \pm 2R = \pm 2\); if \(P = -Q\) then \(P + Q = 0\) and \(Q - P = \pm 2\), giving \(M = \pm 2S = \pm 2\). Hence every run gives \(M = +2\) or \(M = -2\). Averaging over the joint distribution, the expectation obeys the Bell-CHSH inequality

\[ \bigl|\mathbb{E}[M]\bigr| = \bigl|\mathbb{E}[PR] + \mathbb{E}[QR] - \mathbb{E}[PS] + \mathbb{E}[QS]\bigr| \leq 2, \]

in words: "the modulus of the mean of M is at most two." The bound uses only probability arithmetic, with no quantum input, and any local-realistic description must respect it. The modern testable form is due to Clauser, Horne, Shimony, and Holt (1969); the broader program of such bounds traces to John S. Bell, the CERN particle physicist who pursued these foundations questions alongside collider work.

Think of \(M\) as a scorecard filled after each round with pre-printed answers. Whatever the four pre-printed values are, the bracket arithmetic forces the round score to \(\pm 2\), so the season average cannot escape \(\pm 2\) either. Where the picture breaks: the scorecard assumes all four answers are printed before the round, which is exactly the realism assumption quantum mechanics rejects.

Scope: the ceiling \(\pm 2\) rests entirely on the joint distribution \(\Pr(p, q, r, s)\) existing. Drop realism or locality and the joint law need not exist, so the bound evaporates. A frequent misread blames locality alone; the derivation needs both legs, and the violation forces giving up locality, realism, or both.

Recap: pre-set values plus a joint distribution force every round to \(\pm 2\) and the average inside \(\pm 2\). Bridge: a concrete classical story makes the bracket logic tangible before quantumness breaks it.

8.8.2 A Classical Illustration with T-Shirts

A concrete picture fixes the logic. A supplier ships T-shirts; \(A\) observes two properties, color (\(Q\): red \(= +1\), blue \(= -1\)) and size (\(R\): large \(= +1\), medium \(= -1\)), while \(B\) observes price (\(S\): high \(= +1\), low \(= -1\)) and fabric quality (\(T\): good \(= +1\), coarse \(= -1\)). Per round each side tosses a coin to pick which of its two properties to record, the two sides record simultaneously at space-like separation, and the results accumulate in a table. Grouping four rounds as \(QS + RS + RT - QT = (Q + R)S + (R - Q)T\) reproduces the bracket argument: one bracket vanishes and the combination is \(\pm 2\) per group, so the average stays within \(\pm 2\). These are classically fixed properties, and the observation only reads off what was already settled.

Recap: fixed shirt properties obey the same bracket arithmetic, so classical averages stay bounded. Bridge: quantum observables refuse to be pre-printed, which is where the bound falls.

8.8.3 Quantum Observables That Break the Bound

Quantum theory permits states and observables of a kind no classical picture allows: entangled states together with non-commuting local observables, and the pair breaks the bound. Take the shared state \(|\beta_{11}\rangle = (|01\rangle - |10\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) (repeating the calculation for \(|\beta_{00}\rangle\) is set as suggested practice; any Bell state serves). Choose Pauli spin observables

\[ P = \sigma_z, \qquad Q = \sigma_x, \qquad R = \frac{-\sigma_z - \sigma_x}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad S = \frac{\sigma_z - \sigma_x}{\sqrt{2}}, \]

each with eigenvalues \(\pm 1\), since each is a signed sum of anticommuting Paulis normalized by \(1/\sqrt{2}}\). In words: "P is Z, Q is X, R is minus Z minus X over root two, S is Z minus X over root two." \(P\) and \(Q\) do not commute, since \([\sigma_z, \sigma_x] = 2i\sigma_y \neq 0\), and the same holds for \(R\) and \(S\): writing \(R = -(Z + X)/\sqrt{2}\) and \(S = (Z - X)/\sqrt{2}\), direct computation gives \([R, S] = (-1/2)[Z + X, Z - X] = (-1/2)(-2[X, Z]) = [X, Z] = -2iY \neq 0\). A joint term such as \(PR\) means \(P_A \otimes R_B\):

\[ P_A \otimes R_B = \sigma_z \otimes \frac{-\sigma_z - \sigma_x}{\sqrt{2}}. \]

Evaluate the first expectation stepwise. Writing \(|\beta_{11}\rangle = (|01\rangle - |10\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) on both sides: the \(Z \otimes Z\) piece sends \(|01\rangle - |10\rangle\) to \(-|01\rangle + |10\rangle\), whose overlap with the bra gives \(-1\), times the overall \(-1/\sqrt{2}\) yields \(+1/\sqrt{2}\). The \(Z \otimes X\) piece flips the second qubit (\(|01\rangle \to |00\rangle\), \(|10\rangle \to |11\rangle\)), landing orthogonal to the bra, a Bell state against a different Bell state, so it contributes \(0\). The first term is \(1/\sqrt{2}\). In words: "Z-Z gives one over root two; Z-X vanishes by orthogonality."

The same pattern repeats: in the \(Q = \sigma_x\) rows the \(X \otimes Z\) piece maps one Bell state onto another (vanishing overlap) while \(X \otimes X\) preserves it (contributing \(1/\sqrt{2}\)). Each of the four expectations lands at \(1/\sqrt{2}\) in magnitude, with the single minus sign in \(M\) flipping the third term positive. Signs need care across the four terms, especially the third, since that minus sign is what turns all four contributions positive. The total is

\[ \mathbb{E}[M] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{2}} = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.828. \]

In words: "four over root two, which is two root two." The classical ceiling of \(2\) is broken. Sense-check: the total exceeds 2 by a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\), and each term's size \(1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707\) stays within the single-observable range \([-1, 1]\), so no individual expectation looks suspicious; only their combination exposes the violation.

A second, fully explicit route reaches the same number with \(Q = \sigma_z\), \(R = \sigma_x\), \(S = (-Z - X)/\sqrt{2}\), \(T = (Z - X)/\sqrt{2}\) as matrices

\[ S = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & +1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad T = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} +1 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \]

acting as \(S|0\rangle = (-|0\rangle - |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\), \(S|1\rangle = (-|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\), \(T|0\rangle = (|0\rangle - |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\), \(T|1\rangle = (-|0\rangle - |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\). Expanding \(\langle\beta_{11}|QS|\beta_{11}\rangle\) into its four bra-ket pieces, only the \(|01\rangle\)-against-\(|01\rangle\) overlap survives, giving \(1/\sqrt{2}\); the other three expectations evaluate the same way except the last, which yields \(-1/\sqrt{2}\), and the minus sign in \(QS + RS + RT - QT\) turns it positive for a total of \(2\sqrt{2}\). Sense-check: two independent routes landing on the same \(2\sqrt{2}\) confirm the arithmetic; evaluating individual terms this way is flagged as mid-term-style practice.

The observable choice is load-bearing: not every set of local observables breaks the bound, and commuting local choices reproduce classical-range statistics. The trap is thinking entanglement alone suffices. It does not; the violation needs the entangled state plus the non-commuting tilted local measurements together.

8.8.4 What the Violation Means

The number \(2\sqrt{2}\) is the largest violation attainable with qubits and two-outcome measurements.

Exam note: proving that ceiling is set as the fourth problem of the second assignment.

Bell states saturate it, which is one more sense in which they are maximally entangled; other entangled states score between \(2\) and \(2\sqrt{2}\), and the score itself quantifies the state's non-locality (a distinct gauge from entanglement entropy, though the two gauges agree closely for two qubits but can part ways in higher dimensions).

Two negatives sharpen the picture: product states never break the classical bound, so violation witnesses entanglement; and not every observable choice breaks it either, since the non-commuting local set above is load-bearing. In one device-independent check, vendors selling entanglement hardware are asked for exactly this test: measured statistics crossing \(2\) certify the goods, and the same statistics feed quantum-cryptographic protocols. The first laboratory violation came in 1982 from Alain Aspect's group with photons, the easiest carrier to control; recent demonstrations push the effect into further systems such as nuclear spins.

The violated assumption deserves a final precise word. Quantum expectations here are computed term by term with the Born rule, since each local pair gets its probability from trace of projector times state, and non-commuting observables sit safely in different subsystems per term. What does not exist is one joint distribution over all four observables whose marginals reproduce each observable's statistics: such a joint law would imply simultaneous measurability of \(X\) and \(Z\) on one system, contradicting the uncertainty relation (an \(X\) reading followed by \(Z\) scrambles the \(X\) information). Classical intuition assumes that joint law for free; quantum theory withholds it. So the result forces giving up locality, realism (objective values before measurement), or both, and hidden-variable models that reproduce the \(2\sqrt{2}\) value with non-local machinery remain unfinished research.

Recap: \(2\sqrt{2}\) is the qubit ceiling, Bell states hit it, product states never exceed 2, and the missing joint distribution is the precise casualty. Bridge: the next section turns the same ideas into projector arithmetic with counted probabilities.

8.8.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Classically the expectation used one joint distribution, but quantum talk says no joint distribution exists for incompatible observables. How do both statements stand? A: Quantum mechanics still defines each term's expectation through the Born rule: trace of projector times state, giving numbers per local pair, with the non-commuting members living in separate subsystems per term. The missing object is the single four-observable joint law whose marginals would serve \(\sigma_z\), \(\sigma_x\), and the tilted \(B\)-side observables all at once. That law would amount to simultaneous \(X\)-\(Z\) statistics on one system, which is impossible, since a \(Z\) measurement after \(X\) scrambles the \(X\) record. Constraining \(M\) needed the joint sum; computing each quantum term does not.

Q: The decomposition used for entanglement detection was said to be unique up to local unitaries. Do the decomposition weights stay fixed while bases rotate? A: Yes. The weights (the Schmidt coefficients \(\lambda_i\)) come from a singular-value decomposition of the coefficient matrix \(C_{ij}\) of the bipartite state \(|\psi_{AB}\rangle\), so local unitaries on \(A\) and \(B\) cannot change them, since they carry the entanglement content. The local bases may rotate; Bell states show this by keeping the same Schmidt form in the plus-minus basis too. That freedom never alters the \(\lambda_i\).

8.9 Quantitative Tests: Projectors and the Three-Direction Experiment

The CHSH violation used clever observable choices to break a classical ceiling. Can the same verdict be reached by counting raw outcome probabilities along a few well-chosen axes, with no operator ingenuity at all?

The previous section broke the classical bound with tilted Pauli observables. This section rebuilds the verdict from projectors and counted probabilities: a general correlator formula, the projectors behind it, and a three-axis experiment where quantum mechanics and hidden variables predict cleanly separated numbers.

8.9.1 From Anti-Correlation to a General Expectation

The identity \((\sigma_{iA} + \sigma_{iB})|\psi^-\rangle = 0\) turns \(B\)-side operators into \(A\)-side ones: \(\sigma_{iB}|\psi^-\rangle = -\sigma_{iA}|\psi^-\rangle\). Let \(A\) measure spin along unit direction \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(B\) along \(\mathbf{b}\), and form the correlator \(\langle\psi^-|(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_A\cdot\mathbf{a})(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_B\cdot\mathbf{b})|\psi^-\rangle\), meaning the expected product of their \(\pm 1\) outcomes. Trading the \(B\) operator for \(-A\) gives

\[ -\sum_{i,j} a_i b_j \langle\psi^-|\sigma_{iA}\sigma_{jA}|\psi^-\rangle. \]

In words: "minus the sum over i and j of a-i b-j times the psi-minus expectation of sigma-i-A sigma-j-A." Nine terms appear, one per pair of Pauli components.

Nine terms appear. The six off-diagonal (\(i \neq j\)) terms vanish one by one. Concretely, \(\sigma_{xA}\sigma_{yA} = i\sigma_{zA}\) acts on \(|01\rangle - |10\rangle\) to collect a phase \(i\) on one branch and \(-i\) on the other (since \(\sigma_y|0\rangle = i|1\rangle\) and \(\sigma_y|1\rangle = -i|0\rangle\), with \(\sigma_x\) flipping back), and orthogonality against the bra kills the sum. Next \(\sigma_{xA}\sigma_{zA} = -i\sigma_{yA}\) produces a combination proportional to \(|11\rangle + |00\rangle\)-type terms, orthogonal to the bra; the \(\sigma_y\sigma_z\) combination likewise lands orthogonally (up to phases); and the three reversed-order partners behave identically. Only the three diagonal terms (\(i = j\)) survive, and \(\sigma_{iA}^2 = I\) reduces each to the norm, leaving

\[ \langle\psi^-|(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_A\cdot\mathbf{a})(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_B\cdot\mathbf{b})|\psi^-\rangle = -\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = -\cos\theta, \]

with \(\theta\) the angle between the axes. In words: "minus a dot b, which is minus cos theta." The earlier \(\cos^2(\theta/2)\) partial correlation is now confirmed by direct algebra: at \(\theta = 0\) the correlator is \(-1\) (perfect anti-correlation), at \(\theta = \pi/2\) it is \(0\), and at \(\theta = \pi\) it is \(+1\). Sense-check: tracing off-diagonal cancellations term by term is set as suggested practice, and every cancellation follows the same orthogonality pattern.

Picture the correlator as a dial: aligned axes read \(-1\), perpendicular axes read \(0\), and opposed axes read \(+1\), sweeping smoothly through \(-\cos\theta\). The takeaway: the singlet interpolates between perfect anti-agreement and perfect agreement with no jumps.

Recap: operator trading plus off-diagonal cancellation reduces nine terms to three, giving the clean law \(-\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}\). Bridge: projectors turn the same law into counted outcome probabilities.

8.9.2 Projection Operators and Joint Probabilities

Measuring along \(\mathbf{n}\) and finding \(+1\) projects onto \(|+\mathbf{n}\rangle\). The projector is

\[ P_{\mathbf{n}+} = \frac{I + \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{2}, \qquad P_{\mathbf{n}-} = \frac{I - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{2}. \]

In words: "half of identity plus or minus n dot sigma." Acting \((I + \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})/2\) on \(|0\rangle\): the identity returns \(|0\rangle/2\); \(\sigma_x\) flips to \(|1\rangle\) weighted \(n_x = \sin\theta\cos\phi\); \(\sigma_y\) flips with phase \(i\), weighted \(n_y = \sin\theta\sin\phi\); \(\sigma_z\) holds \(|0\rangle\), weighted \(n_z = \cos\theta\). Assembling, the top entry is \((1 + \cos\theta)/2 = \cos^2(\theta/2)\) and the bottom is \(e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2)\); pulling out \(\cos(\theta/2)e^{i\phi/2}\) leaves exactly \(|+\mathbf{n}\rangle\). So the operator projects \(|0\rangle\) onto \(|+\mathbf{n}\rangle\) with amplitude \(\cos(\theta/2)\) (up to phase); on \(|1\rangle\) it yields \(|+\mathbf{n}\rangle\) with amplitude \(\sin(\theta/2)\); the minus projector analogously targets \(|-\mathbf{n}\rangle\).

Now let \(A\) measure along \(\mathbf{n}\) and \(B\) along \(\mathbf{m}\), with \(\theta\) the angle between them. Sandwiching the pair projectors around \(|\psi^-\rangle = (|01\rangle - |10\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) (each projector squares to itself, so one copy acts on the ket and one on the bra) gives amplitudes \((1/\sqrt{2})\sin(\theta/2)\) for the like pairs and \((1/\sqrt{2})\cos(\theta/2)\) for the unlike pairs. As a concrete check with \(\theta = 60^\circ\): each like outcome carries \((1/2)\sin^2(30^\circ) = 1/8\) and each unlike outcome carries \((1/2)\cos^2(30^\circ) = 3/8\), summing to \(2 \times 1/8 + 2 \times 3/8 = 1\). Squaring in general:

\[ \Pr(\text{same}) = \frac{1}{2}\sin^2\frac{\theta}{2} \quad \text{per pair}, \qquad \Pr(\text{different}) = \frac{1}{2}\cos^2\frac{\theta}{2} \quad \text{per pair}. \]

In words: "same outcomes carry half sin-squared theta-half each; different outcomes carry half cos-squared theta-half each." At \(\theta = 0\) the like probability vanishes (perfect anti-correlation); at \(\theta = \pi\) the unlike probability vanishes.

Scope: these probabilities assume the singlet and the stated axis pair. Change the shared state and the angle law changes with it. A common slip reads \(\Pr(\text{same})\) as the total over both like outcomes: it is the probability per pair (\(++\) alone or \(--\) alone), so the total same-outcome probability is twice that, namely \(\sin^2(\theta/2)\).

Recap: projectors convert directions into outcome probabilities with clean half-angle laws. Bridge: three well-chosen axes turn those laws into a decisive hidden-variable test.

8.9.3 The Three-Direction Hidden-Variable Test

Choose three axes: \(\mathbf{n}_1\) along \(z\); \(\mathbf{n}_2\) tilted \(30^\circ\) from the \(x\) axis; \(\mathbf{n}_3\) tilted \(30^\circ\) from \(z\) in the second quadrant. \(B\) measures along \(-\mathbf{n}_1\) (the sign absorbs the singlet minus so \(A\) reads results directly) and reports to \(A\); \(A\), measuring along her own axes, thereby holds joint results for the pairs \((\mathbf{n}_1, \mathbf{n}_2)\), \((\mathbf{n}_2, \mathbf{n}_3)\), \((\mathbf{n}_1, \mathbf{n}_3)\), pairs she could never co-measure alone. Counting only "same" results (\(++\) or \(--\), two cases per pair), each pair contributes \(2 \times \frac{1}{2}\sin^2(\theta/2) = \sin^2(\theta/2)\); with the chosen \(30^\circ\) geometry each pair gives \(1/4\). Summing over the three pairs, quantum mechanics predicts

\[ P_{\text{same}}(\mathbf{n}_1,\mathbf{n}_2) + P_{\text{same}}(\mathbf{n}_2,\mathbf{n}_3) + P_{\text{same}}(\mathbf{n}_1,\mathbf{n}_3) = \frac{3}{4} < 1. \]

Under local hidden variables there is no collapse to worry about, so \(A\) can contemplate definite outcomes along all three axes at once: each axis reads \(+\) or \(-\), giving patterns such as \(++-\). With three binary slots, at least one of the three pairs always matches (check all eight patterns: \(+++\), \(++-\), \(+-+\), \(+--\), \(-++\), \(-+-\), \(--+\), \(---\)), so some pair's "same" event has probability \(1\) and the summed same-probability obeys

\[ P_{\text{same}}(\mathbf{n}_1,\mathbf{n}_2) + P_{\text{same}}(\mathbf{n}_2,\mathbf{n}_3) + P_{\text{same}}(\mathbf{n}_1,\mathbf{n}_3) \geq 1. \]

The two predictions part ways cleanly: below \(1\) favors quantum mechanics, at or above \(1\) favors hidden variables. Running the experiment decides. Sense-check: the gap \(3/4\) versus \(\geq 1\) is wide enough that finite-sample statistics separate the two after enough rounds, which is why the protocol counts pairs rather than trusting any single round.
