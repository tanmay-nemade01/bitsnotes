# Quantum Teleportation, the No-Cloning Theorem, and Superdense Coding

This set of notes builds three connected ideas in quantum information. First, quantum teleportation: how one unknown quantum state can be moved from one lab to another using shared entanglement plus two classical bits. Second, the no-cloning theorem: a fundamental no-go result that forbids copying an arbitrary unknown quantum state, and which explains why teleportation has to be clever. Third, superdense coding: the reverse trick, where one qubit carries two classical bits. Throughout, the emphasis is on tracking states through a circuit, honesty about what is and is not possible, and the resource bookkeeping that makes quantum communication different from classical communication.

Teleportation consumes one shared entangled pair and two classical bits to deliver one qubit of quantum communication. No-cloning explains why the original must vanish. Superdense coding runs the resource trade in the opposite direction. Mixed states and the coherent-versus-incoherent distinction explain the boundary of what teleportation can do exactly.

9.1 Quantum Teleportation Protocol

Quantum teleportation moves one unknown qubit state from Alice to Bob using one shared entangled pair plus two classical bits. Measurement alone would collapse the state, and copying alone is forbidden by no-cloning, so the protocol must do something subtler: it consumes the entanglement and the input, and rebuilds the state on Bob's side.

9.1.1 The Setup and the Cartoon Picture

Why try to move a state you are not allowed to look at? Alice holds exactly one copy of an unknown qubit state. If she measures it, the superposition collapses. If she copies it, no-cloning stops her. Teleportation is the third option: destroy the state here, rebuild it there.

Think of a fax machine that shreds the original as it sends. A classical fax scans the page, sends the scan, and the original still sits in the tray. The quantum fax is stricter: the only way Bob gets the state is for Alice's copy to be consumed. That is the cartoon to keep in mind. A state \(|\psi\rangle\) enters Alice's lab; after two classical bits travel to Bob, the same \(|\psi\rangle\) sits in Bob's lab, and Alice's qubits no longer carry it.

Concretely, Alice holds two qubits and Bob holds one. Label them \(a_1\), \(a_2\), and \(b\):

  • \(a_1\): Alice's message qubit, the unknown state to be sent.
  • \(a_2\): Alice's half of the shared entangled pair.
  • \(b\): Bob's half of the same pair.

The message is an arbitrary single-qubit pure state (one definite qubit state, unknown to Alice, with complex amplitudes), written

\[ |\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle \]

where \(\alpha\) (alpha) and \(\beta\) (beta) are complex amplitudes (numbers whose squared magnitudes give outcome probabilities), \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) are the two computational basis states (the outcomes of a \(Z\)-basis measurement), and normalization \(|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1\) guarantees probabilities sum to one. A number helps: \(\alpha = 3/5\) and \(\beta = 4i/5\) gives \(|3/5|^2 + |4i/5|^2 = 9/25 + 16/25 = 1\).

The shared pair is the Bell state (one of four maximally entangled two-qubit states)

\[ |\beta_{00}\rangle = \frac{|00\rangle + |11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \]

where the first ket label belongs to \(a_2\) and the second to \(b\). This is the same \(\Phi^+\) state used for entanglement throughout: measuring the two halves in the \(Z\) basis always gives matching outcomes.

The goal is fixed: \(|\psi\rangle\) vanishes from Alice's lab and appears in Bob's lab. The shared entanglement is spent in the process. In class this was put bluntly: at the start the two sides share entanglement that will help them, and at the end there is no \(|\psi\rangle\) on Alice's side but there is \(|\psi\rangle\) on Bob's side.

Teleportation setup. Qubits \(a_1\) (message, Alice), \(a_2\) (Alice's entangled half), \(b\) (Bob's entangled half). Input \(|\psi\rangle_{a_1} = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\) with \(|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1\), plus shared \(|\beta_{00}\rangle_{a_2b} = (|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\). Output: \(|\psi\rangle\) on \(b\), entanglement consumed, \(a_1a_2\) collapsed.

Picture the three qubits left to right as message, Alice-half, Bob-half. Before the circuit runs, all the entanglement sits between the right two positions. After the CNOT and Hadamard below, the correlation spreads across all three, and the final measurement concentrates the message on the far right. The one-sentence takeaway: entanglement starts local to the pair and ends up linking the measurement result to Bob's correction.

9.1.2 Mathematical Formulation: Tracking the State Through the Circuit

The circuit has four stages: prepare \(|\psi_0\rangle\), apply CNOT with \(a_1\) controlling \(a_2\), apply Hadamard to \(a_1\), measure \(a_1a_2\). Write the full three-qubit state after each gate, keeping the order \(a_1a_2b\).

State chain. With \(|\psi_0\rangle = |\psi\rangle_{a_1}\otimes|\beta_{00}\rangle_{a_2b}\), CNOT \(a_1\to a_2\), then \(H\) on \(a_1\), the states are

\[ |\psi_0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\alpha|000\rangle + \alpha|011\rangle + \beta|100\rangle + \beta|111\rangle\big) \]

\[ |\psi_1\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\alpha|000\rangle + \alpha|011\rangle + \beta|110\rangle + \beta|101\rangle\big) \]

\[ |\psi_2\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\big(\alpha(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)(|00\rangle+|11\rangle) + \beta(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)(|10\rangle+|01\rangle)\big) \]

regrouped as

\[ |\psi_2\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\big(|00\rangle(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle) + |01\rangle(\alpha|1\rangle+\beta|0\rangle) + |10\rangle(\alpha|0\rangle-\beta|1\rangle) + |11\rangle(\alpha|1\rangle-\beta|0\rangle)\big). \]

Symbols: \(|\psi_k\rangle\) is the joint state after stage \(k\); CNOT flips \(a_2\) when \(a_1=1\); \(H|0\rangle = (|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) and \(H|1\rangle = (|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\).

Derivation in words before the algebra: the initial state is a product across the cut \(a_1\mid a_2b\), meaning \(a_1\) factorizes out and carries no entanglement with the pair. The CNOT correlates \(a_1\) with \(a_2\), so the state no longer factorizes that way. The Hadamard rotates the \(a_1\) basis so that measuring \(a_1a_2\) in the \(Z\) basis acts like a Bell-basis measurement, splitting Bob's qubit into four correctable branches.

Step-by-step algebra with every substitution:

\[ \begin{aligned} |\psi_0\rangle &= (\alpha|0\rangle_{a_1}+\beta|1\rangle_{a_1})\otimes\frac{|0\rangle_{a_2}|0\rangle_b+|1\rangle_{a_2}|1\rangle_b}{\sqrt{2}} &\text{(insert definitions)}\\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\alpha|000\rangle+\alpha|011\rangle+\beta|100\rangle+\beta|111\rangle\big) &\text{(distribute the tensor product)}\\ |\psi_1\rangle &= \mathrm{CNOT}_{a_1\to a_2}|\psi_0\rangle &\text{(flip }a_2\text{ where }a_1=1\text{)}\\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\alpha|000\rangle+\alpha|011\rangle+\beta|110\rangle+\beta|101\rangle\big) &\text{(only the two }\beta\text{ terms change)}\\ |\psi_2\rangle &= (H_{a_1}\otimes I_{a_2}\otimes I_b)|\psi_1\rangle &\text{(rotate }a_1\text{ only)}\\ &= \frac{1}{2}\big(\alpha(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)+\beta(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)(|10\rangle+|01\rangle)\big) &\text{(collect the two }1/\sqrt{2}\text{ factors into }1/2\text{).} \end{aligned} \]

Expanding the last line and collecting by \(a_1a_2\) gives the regrouped form above. Each of the four \(a_1a_2\) strings multiplies a different single-qubit state on \(b\), which is exactly what makes the measurement outcomes manifest.

Scope: this chain assumes a pure message \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\), the exact Bell state \(|\beta_{00}\rangle\), and ideal gates with no noise. Assumption: Alice and Bob already share the pair before the protocol starts; distributing that pair needs a quantum link at some earlier time.

A common slip is losing the Hadamard minus signs when regrouping. The safe habit from class: expand to all eight terms first, then group by \(a_1a_2\). The minus signs survive only on branches where \(a_1=1\) came from \(|1\rangle\to|0\rangle-|1\rangle\), namely the \(|10\rangle\) and \(|11\rangle\) groups.

9.1.3 Worked Example: Full State Evolution and the Entanglement Reregistration

Trace the three-qubit state end to end. Start from

\[ |\psi_0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\alpha|000\rangle + \alpha|011\rangle + \beta|100\rangle + \beta|111\rangle\big). \]

CNOT (\(a_1\to a_2\)): \(\alpha|000\rangle\to\alpha|000\rangle\); \(\alpha|011\rangle\to\alpha|011\rangle\); \(\beta|100\rangle\to\beta|110\rangle\) (flip \(a_2\): \(0\to1\)); \(\beta|111\rangle\to\beta|101\rangle\) (flip \(a_2\): \(1\to0\)). So

\[ |\psi_1\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\alpha|000\rangle + \alpha|011\rangle + \beta|110\rangle + \beta|101\rangle\big). \]

Hadamard on \(a_1\): replace \(|0\rangle_{a_1}\) by \((|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) and \(|1\rangle_{a_1}\) by \((|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\):

\[ |\psi_2\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\big(\alpha(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)(|00\rangle+|11\rangle) + \beta(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)(|10\rangle+|01\rangle)\big). \]

Expand to eight terms: \(\alpha|000\rangle,\alpha|011\rangle,\alpha|100\rangle,\alpha|111\rangle\) and \(\beta|010\rangle,\beta|001\rangle,-\beta|110\rangle,-\beta|101\rangle\), all over \(2\). Group by \(a_1a_2\): \(|00\rangle(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle)\), \(|01\rangle(\alpha|1\rangle+\beta|0\rangle)\), \(|10\rangle(\alpha|0\rangle-\beta|1\rangle)\), \(|11\rangle(\alpha|1\rangle-\beta|0\rangle)\), all over \(2\). Each branch holds Bob's qubit in a known rotation of \(\mathbf{|\psi\rangle}\).

Sense-check with numbers: take \(\alpha=3/5\), \(\beta=4/5\). Then branch \(|00\rangle\) leaves Bob with \((3|0\rangle+4|1\rangle)/5\), branch \(|01\rangle\) leaves \((3|1\rangle+4|0\rangle)/5\), and the squared norm of each branch is \((1/4)(9/25+16/25)=1/4\). Four branches at probability \(1/4\) each sum to one, so the bookkeeping closes.

What happened to entanglement? Initially the state factorized across \(a_1\mid a_2b\). After the CNOT it does not: the correlation now links the pair \(a_1a_2\) jointly to \(b\). The four states \(|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle\) form an orthonormal basis for the two-qubit space, and \(b\) sits in one of four states depending on which basis element \(a_1a_2\) occupies. The measurement then breaks that three-way entanglement and collapses both sides at once.

9.1.4 Measurement Outcomes, Classical Bits, and Conditional Gates

Measure \(a_1\) (giving bit \(m_1\)) and \(a_2\) (giving bit \(m_2\)) in the \(Z\) basis. Each of the four outcomes occurs with probability \(1/4\). Read Bob's state from the grouped \(|\psi_2\rangle\):

\(a_1a_2\) \((m_1,m_2)\) State of qubit \(b\) Gate Bob applies Final state of \(b\)
\(|00\rangle\) \((0,0)\) \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) \(I\) \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\)
\(|01\rangle\) \((0,1)\) \(\alpha|1\rangle+\beta|0\rangle\) \(X\) \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\)
\(|10\rangle\) \((1,0)\) \(\alpha|0\rangle-\beta|1\rangle\) \(Z\) \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\)
\(|11\rangle\) \((1,1)\) \(\alpha|1\rangle-\beta|0\rangle\) \(X\) then \(Z\) \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\)

Case \(00\): Bob already holds \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\); identity does nothing. Case \(01\): the bit-flip gate \(X\) (swaps \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\)) maps \(\alpha|1\rangle+\beta|0\rangle\) back to \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\). Case \(10\): the phase-flip gate \(Z\) (leaves \(|0\rangle\), sends \(|1\rangle\to-|1\rangle\)) removes the relative minus. Case \(11\): apply \(X\) first, giving \(\alpha|0\rangle-\beta|1\rangle\), then \(Z\), giving \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\). Compactly, Bob applies

\[ X^{m_2}Z^{m_1} \]

to his qubit, where \(X^0=Z^0=I\) (identity when the controlling bit is zero) and \(X^1=X\), \(Z^1=Z\). Note the order: \(Z^{m_1}\) acts first on the state, then \(X^{m_2}\), matching the \(11\) case worked above.

Without the two bits the protocol succeeds only one time in four, because only the \(00\) branch needs no correction. Alice must send \((m_1,m_2)\) for Bob to know which correction to apply.

Pitfalls: (1) treating the drawn \(X\) and \(Z\) as unconditional — they are \(X^{m_2}\) and \(Z^{m_1}\) and both are identity on outcome \(00\); (2) swapping which bit controls which gate — \(m_2\) (from \(a_2\)) controls \(X\), \(m_1\) (from \(a_1\)) controls \(Z\); (3) thinking Bob can guess — without \((m_1,m_2)\) he holds one of four states and does not know which.

Visual: draw time left to right. Three horizontal wires \(a_1,a_2,b\). A CNOT dot on \(a_1\) wired to a cross on \(a_2\), then a Hadamard box on \(a_1\), then meter boxes on \(a_1,a_2\) with double lines (classical wires) running forward to conditional \(X\) and \(Z\) boxes on \(b\). The double lines are the visual cue that classical information, not quantum wires, drives the final gates. Takeaway: everything left of the meters creates the four branches; everything right of the double lines selects the correction.

9.1.5 The Resource Statement: Ebits and Classical Bits

Resource inequality. Teleportation is summarized as

\[ 1\ \text{ebit} + 2\ \text{cbits} \;\longrightarrow\; 1\ \text{qubit} \]

where an ebit (one shared Bell pair, here \(|\beta_{00}\rangle\)), a cbit (one classical bit, here \(m_1\) or \(m_2\)), and an output qubit (one qubit's worth of quantum communication, here the delivered \(|\psi\rangle\)) are the units of account.

The surprise: Alice and Bob need no live quantum channel during the protocol. Pre-shared entanglement plus ordinary classical communication achieves quantum communication. The loop to remember: the entanglement itself had to be distributed earlier over a quantum link, so teleportation reschedules the quantum transmission rather than eliminating physics. That is why sharing high-quality entanglement without corruption is a central engineering problem for quantum networks.

9.1.6 No Faster-Than-Light Communication

Teleportation looks like magic — what Einstein called "spooky action at a distance": a state disappears in one lab and reappears in another that could be thousands of kilometers away. It is not, and there is no faster-than-light communication. The reason is the classical communication.

Before \((m_1,m_2)\) arrive, Bob holds one of four states and does not know which correction to apply. Only after the ordinary classical message reaches him — bounded by the speed of light — can he recover \(|\psi\rangle\). The total transfer speed is limited by classical communication.

Two facts reinforce this. First, Alice's measurement consumes the input: her qubits collapse to definite values, so no surviving copy of \(|\psi\rangle\) remains with her. Second, Bob's recovery needs the classical information: the measurement statistics on his side alone reveal nothing about \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). In class this was settled as a direct question: the delay comes from the classical information that must be sent, and that communication is bounded by the speed of light, so nothing here is instantaneous.

Recap: teleportation = Bell pair + CNOT + Hadamard + Bell-basis measurement + \(X^{m_2}Z^{m_1}\). Input consumed, entanglement consumed, state rebuilt. Bridge: the reason the input must be consumed is next — the no-cloning theorem forbids keeping a copy.

Teleportation appears in modular quantum computing proposals (move a state between modules), long-distance quantum networks with entanglement swapping, and any setting where a direct quantum channel is unavailable but pre-shared entanglement plus classical links exist.

9.1.7 Student Questions and Answers

Q: The \(X\) and \(Z\) are drawn after the measurement. Do we always apply both gates?

A: No. The double lines after a meter mark a conditional operation. The gates are \(X^{m_2}\) and \(Z^{m_1}\): when \(m_1=0\), \(Z^0=I\); when \(m_2=0\), \(X^0=I\). On outcome \(00\) nothing is applied. The double lines carry classical bits into the gates.

The next question probes the same diagram convention from the communication angle, so read the two as a pair.

Q: Do the double lines mean classical communication with conditionality?

A: Yes — this is classical conditioning. Contrast it with quantum conditioning in the CNOT, where the target flips based on the control qubit's state with no measurement involved. Here \(M_1,M_2\) collapse the state, produce bits \(m_1,m_2\), and those bits decide the \(X\) and \(Z\). Several students asked variants of this; the confusion point is single: single lines are quantum wires, double lines are classical wires feeding measurement outcomes forward.

A related worry: does handing Bob the state amount to cloning? No. Alice's side collapses to a definite outcome, so \(|\psi\rangle\) no longer exists there. Bob's qubit is reconstituted with classical help. Nothing is duplicated.

9.1.8 History and Experimental Implementations

The protocol is due to Bennett, Brassard, Crépeau, Jozsa, Peres, and Wootters, published in Physical Review Letters in 1993. The paper is short and readable. The volume is 70, pages 1895–1899 (not 17): the in-class volume number was a slip, corrected here against the published reference.

Demonstrations followed on several platforms. Photon-polarization teleportation was reported in 1997 by Bouwmeester, Pan, Mattle, Eibl, Weinfurter, and Zeilinger in Nature — the Zeilinger-group experiment from Innsbruck, recalled in class as the Bouwmeester-led photon experiment. Continuous-variable teleportation with squeezed states of light was reported in 1998 by Furusawa, Sorensen, Braunstein, Fuchs, Kimble, and Polzik in Science — this confirms the in-class attribution to a Furusawa-led team and fixes the spelling. Complete teleportation with nuclear magnetic resonance, where the qubit is a nuclear spin, was reported in 1998 by Nielsen, Knill, and Laflamme in Nature.

These were table-top demonstrations. Later work pushed teleportation to hundreds of kilometers over fiber and free space, and satellite experiments shared entangled photons over thousands of kilometers. The reachable distance remains bounded by how far good entanglement can be shared, which returns to the resource moral: entanglement distribution is the hard part.

9.2 The No-Cloning Theorem

Teleportation moves a state instead of copying it. That choice is forced: copying an arbitrary unknown quantum state is impossible. This impossibility is the no-cloning theorem (a no-go result saying no single machine copies every unknown input), and it punctures any idea that quantum information is all-powerful. Some easy classical tasks are impossible quantumly, and copying is the classic example.

9.2.1 Why Copying Is Forbidden

If Alice holds one copy of an unknown \(|\psi\rangle\), why not photocopy it and keep the original? Because any process that outputs two systems in state \(|\psi\rangle\) while destroying the input is not a copy — it is measure-and-reprepare, which collapses the superposition and needs the answer in advance.

The temptation is concrete. Alice cannot measure the state (that collapses it) and cannot send the physical qubit, so copying looks like the escape. It fails on the definition of copy: a genuine copy leaves the original intact in the same unknown superposition. Destroying the original to print two fresh systems in some guessed state breaks the phase relationships that made the state quantum. Teleportation respects this boundary by transporting the state without breaking its superposition: it never learns \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), yet Bob ends with them.

Recap: no measurement, no copying — so teleportation moves rather than duplicates. Bridge: the photocopier picture next makes the universality demand precise.

9.2.2 The Universal Copying Machine and the Xerox Analogy

A campus photocopier takes lecture notes plus blank paper and returns notes plus copy, original unharmed, for any input page. The quantum question: does a machine exist that does this for every unknown qubit state? Like the copier analogy, the demand is universality — one machine, all inputs — and that demand is what fails.

A classical copier is universal over pages. The quantum version needs two registers plus workspace: one register holding \(|\psi\rangle\) (the original), one blank register starting in a standard state such as \(|0\rangle\) (the blank sheet), and an ancilla workspace \(|A\rangle\) (the machine's internals). The ideal action would be

\[ |\psi\rangle|0\rangle|A\rangle \;\xrightarrow{\;U\;}\; |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle|A_\psi\rangle \]

where \(U\) (a unitary operation, the only allowed evolution for closed quantum gates and circuits) is fixed once, \(|0\rangle\) is the blank target, \(|A\rangle\) is the machine's initial workspace state, and \(|A_\psi\rangle\) is whatever the workspace becomes. The machine must leave the original untouched — nobody lends notes to a copier that eats them — and must work for any \(|\psi\rangle\), not one pre-chosen state.

Where the analogy breaks: classical pages are distinguishable configurations that can be read without disturbance, while unknown quantum states cannot be read without collapse. The copier reads by reflecting light; the quantum copier has no equivalent non-disturbing read for non-orthogonal inputs.

9.2.3 Formal Statement

No-cloning orthogonality condition. Let a fixed unitary \(U\) copy two pure states:

\[ U|\psi\rangle|0\rangle = |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle, \qquad U|\phi\rangle|0\rangle = |\phi\rangle|\phi\rangle \]

where \(|\psi\rangle,|\phi\rangle\) are the two candidate inputs and \(|0\rangle\) is the shared blank target. Then

\[ \langle\psi|\phi\rangle = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \langle\psi|\phi\rangle = 1. \]

Here \(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\) (the inner product, or overlap: \(0\) means orthogonal/perfectly distinguishable, \(1\) means identical up to phase) forces the conclusion: one machine copies perfectly only the same state twice or a mutually orthogonal pair. An unknown state drawn from non-orthogonal alternatives cannot be copied.

Full derivation, end to end, filling the step deferred in class. Take the inner product of the two copying equations. Unitaries preserve inner products, so the overlap of the inputs (left) equals the overlap of the outputs (right):

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle\psi|\langle 0|U^\dagger U|\phi\rangle|0\rangle &= \big(\langle\psi|\langle\psi|\big)\big(|\phi\rangle|\phi\rangle\big) &\text{(insert the two copying actions)}\\ \langle\psi|\phi\rangle\langle 0|0\rangle &= \langle\psi|\phi\rangle\langle\psi|\phi\rangle &\text{(factor each register; }U^\dagger U=I\text{)}\\ x &= x^2 &\text{(write }x=\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\text{; }\langle 0|0\rangle=1\text{).} \end{aligned} \]

The equation \(x=x^2\) has only \(x=0\) and \(x=1\). Inner products are bounded by one in magnitude, so no other complex value survives. That is the whole mechanism: two outputs carry the overlap twice (\(x^2\)), one input carries it once (\(x\)), and unitarity equates them.

Numerical spot-check: \(|\psi\rangle=|0\rangle\) and \(|\phi\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) have overlap \(x=1/\sqrt{2}\approx 0.707\), but \(x^2=1/2\neq x\), so no unitary copies both. Orthogonal inputs such as \(|0\rangle,|1\rangle\) pass (\(x=0=x^2\)), and identical inputs pass (\(x=1=1^2\)). This matches the classical case: distinct classical bit values are orthogonal states, so classical copying is allowed.

Scope: the statement covers exact copying of pure states by a fixed unitary. Assumption: the same \(U\) and the same blank must serve both inputs — building a different machine per known input is not cloning an unknown state. Approximate copying with finite fidelity loss, and probabilistic schemes, are separate topics outside this bound.

The result is due to Wootters and Zurek, published in Nature in 1982 (received 1981, printed 1982 — the in-class year 1983 was a slip, corrected here). It belongs with the other no-go results that bound quantum information: no exact copy, no perfect discrimination of non-orthogonal states without disturbance, no faster-than-light signaling.

Pitfalls: (1) "copy then destroy the original counts" — it does not, that is repreparation; (2) "orthogonal states are covered" — they are explicitly allowed, which is why classical information copies fine; (3) "unitarity is a technicality" — it is the entire proof, since \(U^\dagger U=I\) equates the two overlaps.

9.2.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Copying must be unitary — does that just mean a unitary matrix?

A: Yes. Every quantum gate and circuit is a unitary matrix (a square matrix \(U\) with \(U^\dagger U=I\), so it preserves lengths and overlaps). The copying machine, being a physical quantum evolution, must be some such \(U\). Imposing \(U^\dagger U=I\) is what equates the input overlap with the output overlap and forces \(x=x^2\).

The second question attacks the same impossibility from the destruction angle.

Q: Can I copy if I accept destroying the original?

A: That is not a copy. Destroying the original means measuring it (or otherwise collapsing it), which yields one classical outcome and breaks the superposition. Reprinting two systems from that outcome reproduces the outcome, not the unknown \(|\psi\rangle\). A genuine copy keeps \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) intact on both systems, and that is what the no-cloning theorem forbids for arbitrary unknown states.

Exam note: state the condition \(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\in\{0,1\}\), derive \(x=x^2\) from inner-product preservation in three lines, and name unitarity as the forcing mechanism. Bridge: teleportation obeys this by consuming the input; superdense coding next runs entanglement the other way.

9.3 Superdense Coding

Superdense coding is the inverse of teleportation. Teleportation spends one entangled pair plus two classical bits to deliver one qubit. Superdense coding spends one entangled pair plus one transmitted qubit to deliver two classical bits. Alice touches only her half, sends it, and Bob extracts twice the classical information one qubit normally carries.

9.3.1 Motivation: Two Classical Bits from One Qubit

How can one qubit deliver two bits? Alone it cannot — measuring a single qubit yields one bit. But Alice's qubit arrives pre-correlated with a qubit Bob already holds. She steers the joint pair into one of four distinguishable states, and Bob reads which one.

The setup: Alice and Bob pre-share a Bell pair, one qubit each. Alice wants to send one of four messages — \(00,01,10,11\). She may act only on her qubit, then sends that single qubit to Bob through a quantum channel. Bob then holds both qubits and measures them to recover the two bits.

The teleportation contrast is the memory hook. Teleportation: classical bits travel, quantum state is rebuilt. Superdense coding: a qubit travels, classical bits are extracted. The resource arrows point opposite ways, and confusing them is the number-one exam slip.

A circuit-reading note: the dashed red box on the left of the textbook circuit only builds the shared pair (Hadarmard on the first qubit from \(|00\rangle\), then CNOT). The protocol proper starts once each side holds its half.

9.3.2 The Encoding Operations

Alice and Bob share

\[ |\beta_{00}\rangle = \frac{|00\rangle + |11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \]

where the first qubit is Alice's and the second is Bob's. Alice applies one of four single-qubit unitaries to her qubit only, written as \(U\otimes I\) (operator on hers, identity on Bob's):

  • Send \(00\): apply \(I\) (do nothing). Result: \(\dfrac{|00\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\).
  • Send \(01\): apply \(X\otimes I\). The bit-flip \(X\) swaps \(|0\rangle\leftrightarrow|1\rangle\), giving \(\dfrac{|10\rangle+|01\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\).
  • Send \(10\): apply \(i\sigma_y\otimes I\). With the Pauli-Y matrix (one of the three Pauli matrices \(X,Y,Z\))

\[ \sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad i\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \]

this sends \(|0\rangle\to-|1\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\to|0\rangle\). Applied to the pair it gives \(\dfrac{-|10\rangle+|01\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\).

  • Send \(11\): apply \(Z\otimes I\). The phase-flip \(Z\) leaves \(|0\rangle\) and sends \(|1\rangle\to-|1\rangle\), giving \(\dfrac{|00\rangle-|11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\).

Check the \(i\sigma_y\) arithmetic explicitly, since this is where signs get lost. Act on each term's first qubit: \(|00\rangle\to(-|1\rangle)|0\rangle=-|10\rangle\) and \(|11\rangle\to(|0\rangle)|1\rangle=|01\rangle\). So \((i\sigma_y\otimes I)(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}=(-|10\rangle+|01\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\). A number-free sanity check: the two outputs differ from the \(01\) case only by a relative minus, so they are orthogonal to it.

Encoding map. The four local operations send the shared \(|\beta_{00}\rangle\) to four mutually orthogonal Bell states, one per message. Orthogonal states are perfectly distinguishable by measurement, so Bob can tell them apart. A global (overall) minus sign carries no physics and is ignored.

Notation note for readers of textbooks: many texts assign \(10\to Z\) (giving \(\Phi^-\)) and \(11\to ZX\) (giving \(\Psi^-\) up to phase). The lecture's assignment here is \(10\to i\sigma_y\) and \(11\to Z\). Both produce the same set of four Bell states, only the message labels on \(\Phi^-\) and \(\Psi^-\) are permuted. Keep the lecture's table for the exam; recognize the textbook table as the same physics with relabeled rows.

Visual: picture four arrows from one starting point, each labeled by Alice's local gate, landing on four perpendicular directions. The takeaway: local action steers the global state into four distinguishable corners, and no action on Bob's side was needed.

9.3.3 Worked Example: Encoding, CNOT, and Measurement

Decode all four messages. Bob holds one of the four states above, applies CNOT (first qubit controls, second is target), then Hadamard on the first qubit, measuring to recover the message.

CNOT step — flip the second qubit wherever the first is \(1\):

  • Message \(00\), state \(\dfrac{|00\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\): \(|11\rangle\to|10\rangle\). Result \(\dfrac{|00\rangle+|10\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\). Second qubit uniformly \(0\).
  • Message \(01\), state \(\dfrac{|10\rangle+|01\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\): \(|10\rangle\to|11\rangle\), \(|01\rangle\) unchanged. Result \(\dfrac{|11\rangle+|01\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\). Second qubit uniformly \(1\).
  • Message \(10\), state \(\dfrac{-|10\rangle+|01\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\): \(|10\rangle\to|11\rangle\). Result \(\dfrac{-|11\rangle+|01\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\). Second qubit uniformly \(1\).
  • Message \(11\), state \(\dfrac{|00\rangle-|11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\): \(|11\rangle\to|10\rangle\). Result \(\dfrac{|00\rangle-|10\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\). Second qubit uniformly \(0\).

Measure the second qubit: \(0\) narrows to \(\{00,11\}\); \(1\) narrows to \(\{01,10\}\). The surviving first qubit is \((|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) for messages \(00,01\) and \((|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) for messages \(11,10\). Hadamard sends the plus combination to \(|0\rangle\) and the minus combination to \(|1\rangle\). Measure the first qubit and look up:

Second qubit First qubit Message
\(0\) \(0\) \(00\)
\(0\) \(1\) \(11\)
\(1\) \(0\) \(01\)
\(1\) \(1\) \(10\)

Every message is recovered exactly. For example second \(=0\), first \(=1\) gives candidate sets \(\{00,11\}\) and \(\{10,11\}\), whose intersection is \(11\).

Sense-check: each CNOT output above factors as (first-qubit superposition) \(\otimes\) (definite second qubit), so measuring the second qubit truly does not disturb the first. The Hadamard then converts a phase difference (plus versus minus) into a computational-basis difference (\(0\) versus \(1\)). Two measurements, two bits, no ambiguity.

Classroom order versus textbook order: the lecture measures the second qubit mid-way, then applies Hadamard to the first, then measures it. Textbooks often apply CNOT, then Hadamard, then measure both at once. The statistics are identical by deferred measurement (a control followed by measurement gives the same distribution as measurement followed by classical control). Use either order; the lookup table is the same.

Pitfalls: (1) letting Alice touch Bob's qubit — she acts as \(U\otimes I\) only; (2) dropping the relative minus in the \(10\) case — it is what separates \(01\) from \(10\) at the final Hadamard; (3) reading the table backwards — the second-qubit outcome selects the row pair, the first-qubit outcome selects within it.

9.3.4 Generalization Beyond the Bell State and the GHZ Example

Superdense coding needs only a structure, not the specific Bell state. Suppose there is a set of \(2^n\) orthonormal states \(S=\{|S_0\rangle,\dots,|S_{2^n-1}\rangle\}\) and, for every pair \(i,j\), a unitary \(U_{ji}\) acting on Alice's side alone with

\[ U_{ji}|S_i\rangle = |S_j\rangle. \]

In words: from the shared starting state, Alice reaches any member of the set without touching Bob's qubits. Then she sends her qubits, and Bob distinguishes the \(2^n\) orthogonal outcomes to recover \(n\) classical bits. The Bell case is \(n=2\) with single-qubit Paulis steering among four states.

The GHZ state (three-qubit maximally entangled state) shows the pattern scaling up:

\[ |\mathrm{GHZ}\rangle = \frac{|000\rangle + |111\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \]

with Alice holding two qubits and Bob one. Alice sends three classical bits (one of eight strings \(000\) through \(111\)) using her two qubits: for each target string she applies a two-qubit operation to her pair, identity on Bob's. Example: to send \(010\) she applies \(X\otimes I\) on her two qubits (identity on Bob's), flipping her first qubit so the joint state moves to a different orthogonal branch. Bob then applies suitable decoding operations and measurements to infer the three bits.

This was left as homework: enumerate all eight local operations and Bob's exact decoding measurement. The design constraint is unchanged — Alice touches only her qubits, then ships them — and orthogonality of the eight outcomes is what makes decoding possible.

Recap: one transmitted qubit plus pre-shared entanglement delivers two classical bits because Alice's local gates steer among four orthogonal Bell states. Exam note: memorize the lecture's four-operation table, the CNOT-plus-Hadamard decode, and the intersection logic of the two measurements. Bridge: the states discussed so far were pure; the next section asks what survives when coherence is gone.

Superdense coding sits beside teleportation as a standard quantum-network primitive: entanglement plus a small transmission buys a larger communication payoff. It was proposed by Bennett and Wiesner and shown optically in the 1990s.

9.4 Mixed States, Coherent versus Incoherent Superposition

Teleportation as written assumes a pure input. Two questions test that boundary: can it teleport a mixed state, and what exactly distinguishes a coherent superposition from an incoherent mixture? The answers explain why pure-state teleportation is exact and why mixed-state teleportation cannot be.

9.4.1 Why Mixed States Cannot Be Teleported Perfectly

A pure state is one definite ket such as \(|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\). A mixed state is a probabilistic mixture of pure states, described by a density operator (a matrix \(\rho\) encoding classical probabilities over kets) rather than a single ket. The teleportation circuit assumes the input is pure.

Mixed-state limitation. A mixed state is a convex sum (probability-weighted mixture) \(\rho=\sum_k p_k|\psi_k\rangle\langle\psi_k|\) of pure states. To teleport it you must first be given its pure-state decomposition (the list of \(|\psi_k\rangle\) and weights \(p_k\)); then each pure member can be teleported individually. Teleporting an unknown mixed state perfectly, with no decomposition given, cannot be done. About the best available is closeness up to some fidelity (overlap between intended and actual output, \(0\) to \(1\)).

Why the wall exists: a mixture has no relative phase between its members to preserve — only classical probabilities \(p_k\). There is no coherent superposition for the circuit to carry across; what must cross is a probability distribution, and the teleportation circuit moves amplitudes, not distributions. A concrete case: one half of an entangled pair, viewed alone, is mixed (its reduced state), so the same reasoning applies — decompose first, then teleport members.

Scope: exact teleportation here means ideal gates, ideal Bell pair, pure input — unit fidelity. Assumption: the decomposition, when used, is known to the sender. Without it the protocol has no well-defined single ket to track through the CNOT–Hadamard chain.

9.4.2 Coherent versus Incoherent Superposition

Toss a coin and prepare \(|0\rangle\) on heads, \(|1\rangle\) on tails: that is a mixture — the two outcomes never interact. Now imagine the two possibilities combined with a definite wavelike phase between them: that is a coherent superposition, and the phase is real, measurable data.

A coherent superposition keeps a relative phase:

\[ |\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle \]

where \(\alpha,\beta\) are complex amplitudes and their relative phase (the argument of \(\alpha^*\beta\)) determines interference. In the lab, making one on a spin qubit takes a coherent pulse (a timed drive of a set width) during which phase coherence is maintained — the pulse lets \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) interact coherently rather than being chosen independently.

An incoherent mixture has only probabilities:

\[ \rho = p_1|0\rangle\langle 0| + p_2|1\rangle\langle 1| \]

where \(p_1,p_2\) are classical probabilities (\(p_1+p_2=1\)) and each \(|k\rangle\langle k|\) is a projector onto that basis state. Heads: prepare \(|0\rangle\). Tails: prepare \(|1\rangle\). No cross terms, no phase.

Numbers make the difference visible. Take \(|\psi\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) versus \(\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}|0\rangle\langle 0|+\tfrac{1}{2}|1\rangle\langle 1|\). Measure either in \(Z\): fifty-fifty both ways. Measure in \(X\): the coherent state gives \(+\) with certainty (interference), the mixture still gives fifty-fifty. The phase shows up exactly where mixtures cannot mimic it — and teleportation carries that phase across untouched.

Pitfalls: (1) "mixture with fifty-fifty weights equals plus state" — \(Z\) statistics match but \(X\) statistics expose the difference; (2) "teleportation copies the probabilities" — for pure inputs it moves amplitudes including phase; for mixtures there is no phase to move; (3) "unknown half of an entangled pair teleports like a pure qubit" — its local state is mixed, so decomposition comes first.

9.4.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Must \(|\psi\rangle\) be a single-qubit pure state, or can it be half of an entangled pair — any arbitrary state?

A: The circuit assumes a pure input. A mixed state is a convex sum of pure states, so you must be given its pure-state decomposition and teleport each member. An unknown mixed state cannot be teleported perfectly. Half of an entangled pair is locally mixed, so the same rule applies: decompose, then teleport the members. This was the single confusion point behind the "arbitrary state" wording — arbitrary here means arbitrary pure single-qubit state.

The follow-up moves from teleportation's input requirements to the underlying phase distinction itself.

Q: What is coherent summation — what differs between coherent and incoherent?

A: Coherent: \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) with complex amplitudes and a real relative phase; the branches interact (spin-qubit coherent pulse of a set width, phase preserved). Incoherent: \(\rho=p_1|0\rangle\langle 0|+p_2|1\rangle\langle 1|\); each basis state realized independently like a coin flip, only \(p_1,p_2\) matter. Teleportation moves the coherent combination with its phase; for a mixture the phase question does not arise. Repeated asks of this were merged here — one canonical answer covers them.

Recap: pure states carry phase and teleport exactly; mixtures carry probabilities and need decomposition first. Exam note: be ready to write both forms from memory and to explain the \(X\)-basis experiment that separates them.

These notes reward reproducing derivations, not recalling final answers. Memorize sequences and tables as working tools.

  • The teleportation state chain as a sequence: initial product \(|\psi_0\rangle\), post-CNOT \(|\psi_1\rangle\), post-Hadamard \(|\psi_2\rangle\), regrouped four-term form. Practice expanding the three-qubit state and regrouping by \(a_1a_2\) without losing Hadamard minus signs.
  • The measurement table: for each of the four outcomes, Bob's qubit state and the correction \(X^{m_2}Z^{m_1}\). Drill which bit controls which gate (\(m_2\to X\), \(m_1\to Z\)).
  • The resource statement \(1\ \text{ebit} + 2\ \text{cbits} \to 1\ \text{qubit}\): know what an ebit is and why classical communication plus pre-shared entanglement achieves quantum communication.

Exam note: State the no-cloning orthogonality condition \(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle=0\) or \(1\) and derive it from unitarity (\(x=x^2\)) in three lines. Examiners target the forcing mechanism, not just the statement.

For superdense coding the drill is the encode table plus the decode order.

Exam note: For superdense coding, know the four encoding unitaries, the CNOT-plus-Hadamard decode, and how the two measurements intersect to identify the message. The GHZ three-qubit extension is a strong check of understanding.

Finally, the highest-frequency slip across both protocols is directional.

Key Industry Applications

  • Quantum teleportation runs in real hardware. Early table-top demonstrations now extend to hundreds of kilometers over fiber and free space, with satellite experiments sharing entangled photons over thousands of kilometers. Reachable distance tracks the distance over which high-quality entanglement can be shared.
  • Squeezed states of light provided one early teleportation platform — continuous-variable entanglement rather than polarization entanglement — showing the protocol is not tied to one degree of freedom.
  • Nuclear magnetic resonance systems (qubits as nuclear spins) and polarization-entangled photons provided other early platforms. Together the three show teleportation across optics, spin ensembles, and photonic hardware.
  • Sharing entanglement reliably without corruption is the central engineering bottleneck for quantum networks under construction today. Teleportation and superdense coding are the core communication primitives those networks are built to support.
  • Superdense coding is the canonical conversion of one shared pair plus one transmitted qubit into two classical bits — the companion primitive to teleportation, and a standard building block in quantum communication protocol stacks.
