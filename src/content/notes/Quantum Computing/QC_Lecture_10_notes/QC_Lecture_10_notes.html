# Classical and Quantum Information Theory

10.1 What Is Information?

10.1.1 The Everyday Meaning and Its Limits

Why start a quantum communication lecture with a dictionary? Because the everyday word information feels clear until you try to measure it.

The Concise Oxford Dictionary defines information as facts provided or learned about something or someone. In daily talk we all use the word without trouble, yet we never attach a number to it. Nobody says "that message carried 3.2 units of information." Science needs numbers, so before any quantum protocol can be discussed, the qualitative word must become a quantity, measured the way length or mass is measured.

The lecture opens with three motivating questions. Does every piece of information convey something? What decides that one message is richer than another? And how do we put a number on that richness? These questions are the hook for the whole lecture: everything from Shannon entropy to Von Neumann entropy is an answer to them.

An uncertain situation, in the sense used here, is a situation with several possible outcomes where the actual one is unknown. Information is what removes that uncertainty. That single sentence is the seed of the subject, and each later section grows it one step further.

10.1.2 Making the Statement Precise

Take the statement "it is cold in Mumbai today." Does it inform you of anything? At first it seems to. The trouble starts when you ask what cold means. Cold compared with Alaska? Cold relative to yesterday? A lifelong resident with a feel for local weather gets a hint from it. A stranger gets almost nothing, because the word cold has no fixed meaning.

So the first move is to define the term. Looking at Indian climates broadly, suppose we agree to call a day cold when the temperature lies between 17 and 22 degrees Celsius. Now the sentence carries real content: the temperature sits somewhere in that band. But it still does not say whether the reading is 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, or 22. Six options remain, and by assumption each is equally likely, so each carries probability one in six. Six equally likely outcomes is the same uncertainty as one throw of a fair die.

Worked illustration: sharpening the forecast. Start with six equally likely temperatures, 17 through 22, each with probability \(1/6\). Now add one fact: yesterday's reading was 19 degrees, and today is colder. Suppose only two options survive the new fact, say 17 and 18, each now with probability \(1/2\). The uncertainty has shrunk from a six-way choice to a coin toss. When the true value is finally revealed — today was 21 degrees — the uncertainty drops to zero. Each added fact cut the number of surviving possibilities, and the uncertainty fell with it.

The pattern across those steps is the whole subject in miniature. As the statement grew more precise, the count of remaining possibilities fell from six to two to one. Information is a measure of the uncertainty attached to a message. Learn the definite value and the uncertainty is gone: guessing by die throw succeeds with probability one in six, while being told the figure removes all doubt. Quantify the uncertainty first, and the information content follows second.

Here the analogy bank picture helps: entropy is the average surprise in tomorrow's weather. A vague forecast ("cold") leaves you unsurprised by almost any reading, so it told you little. A sharp forecast leaves room for real surprise, so it told you a lot.

10.1.3 The Light on the Hill

A second picture makes the same point with no arithmetic at all.

Q: Does a steadily burning light on a hilltop tell you anything? A: Not really. A lamp that never changes carries no news, because every glance shows the same thing. Now let that light blink on and off in a fixed pattern. At once you suspect that someone is signalling, perhaps in code. The steady light gave nothing away. The variation is what could carry a message. Information lives in change, not in constancy.

The mapping is direct. The steady lamp is a situation with one possible observation, so there is nothing to resolve. The blinking lamp is a situation with several possible patterns, and identifying the actual pattern removes uncertainty. Both pictures in this section — the shrinking temperature list and the blinking lamp — point the same way: information is tied to uncertainty, and uncertainty ends when a definite outcome is revealed.

Picture the uncertainty as a bar. Six candidate temperatures fill the bar high. Each new fact shortens it. The announced value empties it. The rest of the lecture is the project of putting a calibrated scale on that bar.

Scope: This section fixes the meaning of information as uncertainty removed. It does not yet fix a unit for it. The unit arrives with Shannon's question-counting in the next sections, where one yes/no answer earns the name of one bit.

Two traps sit right at the start. First, longer messages need not carry more information: a rambling vague paragraph can say less than one sharp number. Length and content are different things. Second, a precise-sounding word can still be empty: "cold" felt informative until its meaning was pinned down. Always ask what possibilities a statement rules out. A message that rules out nothing says nothing.

Information measures the uncertainty a message removes. Fewer surviving possibilities means less uncertainty, and a fully revealed outcome means none. The next step is to count that uncertainty, which is where Shannon's yes/no questions come in.

A weather office, a compression program, and a quantum cryptography link all trade in the same goods: possible outcomes, probabilities, and the questions that settle them. The lecture now turns to the first counting scheme.

10.2 Two Ways to Quantify Information

10.2.1 Shannon's Approach: Bits and Questions

How do you count uncertainty? Shannon gave a concrete answer: information is the number of bits that must be sent to pick the right answer from a list of choices agreed in advance. To pin down one answer, you ask a run of yes/no questions. Each answer cuts the list down. The count of questions you needed is your measure of the information.

A bit (short for binary digit), written as 0 or 1, is one yes/no answer. If a choice among \(M\) equally likely options takes \(q\) yes/no questions, it carries \(q\) bits of information. The question-and-answer run forms a decision tree: each branch point is a question, each branch is an answer, and each leaf is a final pick.

A tiny case shows the idea. Two options need one question. Four options need two questions: the first halves the field to two, the second picks the winner. Eight options need three questions. Each extra question doubles the field you can handle, so the question count grows as the logarithm of the field size. That doubling pattern is the reason logarithms rule this subject.

Picture a guessing game. A friend picks one card from eight face down. You may only ask yes/no questions. "Is it red?" halves the field. "Is it a face card?" halves it again. One more question names it. Three questions, three bits. The decision tree you just walked is Shannon's measure made visible.

Scope: Shannon's count fits a fixed menu of known options with known odds. It says nothing about the meaning of the message, only about how many questions settle it. A deep poem and a random string with the same option count score the same here. Meaning belongs to readers; counting belongs to Shannon.

The visual is a tree on its side. The root sits at the left edge. Questions branch rightward, one fork per question, leaves at the right edge. For eight options the tree has three layers of forks and eight leaves. The takeaway at a glance: balanced forks mean every leaf sits at the same depth, so every pick costs the same number of questions.

Beginners often mix up the message with its length. A 200-bit string need not carry 200 bits of Shannon information: if the receiver already knows most of it, few questions remain. Count the surviving options, not the characters on the page.

Shannon counts information as yes/no questions needed to settle a choice, and each question earns one bit. The decision-tree picture from this section returns in every later section, so keep the eight-cards-three-questions image ready.

Fib re-optic links, barcode scanners, and quiz games all run on this count: a fixed menu, a run of yes/no tests, and a shortest path to the answer.

10.2.2 Kolmogorov and Chaitin: Algorithmic Complexity

Shannon counts questions. Kolmogorov and Chaitin count description length instead. They ask: how short is the shortest program that prints the message? That length is the algorithmic complexity (also called Kolmogorov complexity) of the string.

Worked contrast: patterned versus random strings. Take a 200-bit message. First version: the pair 10 written over and over, 10, 10, 10, all the way to 200 bits. You never write all of it out. In any computer language you say "print 10 one hundred times," a do loop of a few words. Its complexity is tiny. Second version: 200 flips of a fair coin with no pattern at all. No loop helps, no shortcut exists, and the shortest description is nearly the string itself. Same length, wildly different complexity. The patterned string is simple; the random string is complex.

The mapping to daily life: a knitting pattern ("repeat these six stitches forty times") against a shopping list of forty unrelated items. The pattern compresses; the list does not. Complexity measures how much shortcut a message allows.

Where the picture breaks: complexity has no agreed yardstick. Change the computer language and the shortest program changes a little. Shannon's count avoids that wobble by fixing the menu and the odds first. That is why computer science studies the Kolmogorov and Chaitin route while the rest of this lecture follows Shannon, with Kolmogorov named once more at the end as the road not taken.

Exam note: Know the two routes by name and by test. Shannon: questions to settle a choice. Kolmogorov and Chaitin: length of the shortest generating program, small for the repeated 10-string, large for a random string. The lecture builds only on Shannon from here on.

Zip programs live on the second route: repetitive files shrink far, already-random files barely shrink at all.

10.3 The Decision Tree: Selecting a Celebrity

10.3.1 The Setup

Imagine inviting a celebrity to an event. Beforehand everyone agrees on eight names, so there are eight possible answers. The names carry structure that smart questions can exploit. Four of the eight are Indian and four are not. The group spans several professions: sports people, film people, people in public service, and two authors. The task is to name the one chosen person with as few yes/no questions as possible.

Why does structure matter? A good question splits the field evenly. "Is the person Indian?" cuts eight names into four and four. A bad question, like "Is it the Prime Minister?", cuts eight into one and seven and wastes the answer in most cases. Even splits find the answer fast. Lopsided splits drag the game out.

Think of the eight names as eight marbles in a bag, four painted red (Indian) and four painted white (not Indian). Each question shakes the bag through a sieve. The art is picking sieves that leave half the marbles each time.

10.3.2 Walking the Tree

Question one: is the person Indian? Yes leaves four names, no leaves the other four. Record yes as 0 for the first bit.

Question two: is the person male? Yes adds a 0, no adds a 1. Each group of four now splits into two groups of two.

Follow the Indian-then-male branch. Question three: is the person a public figure? Yes narrows the pair to one, the Prime Minister, Modi. No leaves the author of that pair, Vikram Seth.

Take the sibling branch: Indian but not male. Question three becomes: is the person a sports personality? Yes names Sania Mirza. No names the actress.

A decision tree is the full map of those forks: each internal node is a yes/no question, each edge is an answer bit (0 for yes, 1 for no in this lecture's convention), and each leaf is a final name. The bit string on the path from root to leaf is the codeword for that name. Depth of the leaf equals questions asked.

The same walk covers the non-Indian half, with its own third questions. Every leaf sits three forks deep, so every name costs three questions.

A notation fix is needed here. Labelling the leaves with four-bit strings such as 0000 for the Prime Minister and 0001 for Vikram Seth conflicts with the tree itself, which asks only three questions. Three yes/no answers give three bits, and three bits name exactly \(2^3 = 8\) leaves:

\[ 000,\; 001,\; 010,\; 011,\; 100,\; 101,\; 110,\; 111. \]

So the standard three-bit labels are 000 for the first leaf, 001 for the second, and so on. The extra leading bit in the draft's four-bit rendering is a bookkeeping prefix, not a fourth question. From here on this section uses the three-bit form, which matches the three-question walk: the Prime Minister is 000 and Vikram Seth is 001 on the branch traced above.

Worked trace: finding Vikram Seth. Start with eight names. Q1: Indian? Yes (bit 0), four names left. Q2: male? Yes (bit 0), two names left: the Prime Minister and Vikram Seth. Q3: public figure? No (bit 1), one name left: Vikram Seth, codeword 001. Three questions, three bits, one answer. Sense-check: each question halved the field, \(8 \to 4 \to 2 \to 1\), so three halvings had to suffice.

A second trace keeps the pattern firm. Sania Mirza sits on the Indian, not-male, sports-personality path: yes (0), no (1), yes (0), codeword 010. Three questions again. Every one of the eight leaves works the same way.

10.3.3 What the Tree Tells Us

Count the questions. Every path needs three, so the decision tree assigns three bits to the choice among eight. That count is Shannon information in action: the number of bits of communication needed to arrive at one answer from a previously agreed set.

The tree picture also previews the logarithm. Eight options needed three questions because \(2^3 = 8\). In general, \(M\) equally likely options need \(\log_2 M\) questions when \(M\) is a power of two. The celebrity game is the case \(M = 8\) with \(\log_2 8 = 3\).

Picture the tree lying on its side. The root is a dot at the left. Three layers of forks spread rightward. Eight leaves line the right edge, each tagged with its three-bit codeword. The takeaway from the shape: balanced forks keep every leaf at the same depth, which is why every name costs the same three questions here. Lopsided trees, met later, give ragged depths.

Scope: This even-split tree is the best case: eight options, equal odds, three questions each. When odds differ, some leaves deserve shorter paths than others, and the average question count drops below \(\log_2 M\). That uneven case is where entropy earns its keep.

Two beginner traps hide in this game. First, reading the bit labels as head counts: 000 does not mean "zero of anything," it is just the path yes-yes-yes. Second, assuming any three questions work: only even-split questions achieve three for all eight. Three lopsided questions can leave the answer still open.

Eight equally likely names, settled by three even-split yes/no questions, carry three bits of information. A decision tree turns Shannon's abstract count into a visible map of questions, answers, and codewords. The next section converts that map into a function with testable properties.

Television quiz games, medical triage charts, and computer file menus all use the same device: halve the field with each question until one leaf remains.

10.4 Properties of the Uncertainty Function

10.4.1 The Random Variable and the Function H

Uncertainty needs a symbol before it can be calculated. Start with the standard statistics notation. Let \(X\) be a random variable (the physical process itself, written in capitals), taking particular values \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m\) (written in lowercase). Outcome \(x_i\) occurs with probability \(P_i\). There are \(m\) outcomes in all, and the probabilities sum to one:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{m} P_i = 1. \]

Now define the uncertainty function \(H\) of the outcome probabilities:

\[ H(P_1, P_2, \dots, P_m). \]

\(H\) is the average uncertainty attached to the event. Read it two ways that mean the same thing: it is the uncertainty hanging over the experiment before the outcome is known, and it is the amount of uncertainty removed when the result is learned. It depends on all the probabilities together, never on the labels \(x_i\) themselves. Rename heads as 0 and tails as 1 and \(H\) does not move.

Why must labels drop out? Because the questions that settle the outcome care only about odds. "Heads versus tails at 50-50" and "0 versus 1 at 50-50" need the same single yes/no question. The values ride along; the probabilities drive.

10.4.2 The Equal-Probability Function f(M)

The general \(H\) is hard to pin down directly, so corner it through the easy case first. Take \(M\) events, each with probability \(1/M\), and define:

\[ f(M) = H\!\left(\frac{1}{M}, \frac{1}{M}, \dots, \frac{1}{M}\right), \]

with \(M\) arguments on the right, each equal to \(1/M\). The familiar case is a fair coin toss, with \(P_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(P_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\), so that:

\[ f(2) = H\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right). \]

Think of \(f\) as the evenly divided share: total uncertainty split across \(M\) equal slices. A fair die (one of six faces, each chance \(1/6\)) gives \(f(6) = H(\tfrac16, \dots, \tfrac16)\). The function \(f\) inherits its shape from the situation it describes, and four properties of that shape will fix its formula outright. Where the everyday picture breaks: real dice wear down and coins bend, so perfect equality is an ideal. The math keeps the ideal and lets the physics approximate it.

10.4.3 Monotonicity, Additivity, and Certainty

Monotonic increase. More equal options means more uncertainty:

\[ M > M' \implies f(M) > f(M'). \]

A coin offers two alternatives, a die offers six, and the die leaves you more in the dark. The function climbs with \(M\). No math beyond comparison shopping is needed: choosing among six Suspects is harder than choosing between two.

Additivity for a joint experiment. Run two independent experiments side by side and their uncertainties add. Let \(X\) be a coin toss with two alternatives and \(Y\) a die throw with six alternatives. Do both together: head-with-1 through head-with-6, tail-with-1 through tail-with-6, twelve combined outcomes in all. The rule is:

\[ f(MN) = f(M) + f(N). \]

Worked picture: the joint coin and die experiment shows the two uncertainties simply add. Toss a fair coin (\(M = 2\)) and throw a fair die (\(N = 6\)) together. Learn the die result, say 4: the coin is still 50-50, so its \(f(2)\) of uncertainty survives untouched. Learn the coin result, say heads: the die still hides among six faces, so its \(f(6)\) survives untouched. Neither answer dents the other experiment, giving \(f(12) = f(2) + f(6)\). Sense-check: twelve equal combined outcomes must carry more uncertainty than either part alone, and the sum says exactly how much more.

Independence does the work here. The coin cannot whisper the die's face, so resolving one leaves the other fully open.

Certainty has no uncertainty. One outcome, no choice, no doubt:

\[ f(1) = 0. \]

A lamp with a single state asks no questions.

Picture \(f\) as a rising staircase: flat zero at \(M = 1\), one step up at \(M = 2\), higher at \(M = 6\), higher still at \(M = 12\), with the joint-experiment rule saying each landing stacks exactly. The takeaway from the shape: equal options pile uncertainty in a regular, composable way.

Scope: Additivity needs independent experiments. Linked outcomes, like today's temperature and today's ice-cream sales, share information, so settling one partly settles the other and the sum overcounts. The lecture's coin and die are independent by construction; correlated pairs wait for mutual information in later chapters.

10.4.4 The Grouping Theorem

The fourth property is the engine of everything after. It is called the grouping theorem, and its full derivation sits in any classical information theory text. The idea is what matters here.

Take a random variable \(X\) with \(M\) outcomes carrying probabilities \(P_1, \dots, P_M\). Split them into two groups. Group A holds the first \(r\) outcomes with probabilities \(P_1, \dots, P_r\). Group B holds the rest. Let the group totals be:

\[ P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{r} P_i \]

and

\[ P(B) = \sum_{i=r+1}^{M} P_i = 1 - P(A). \]

The grouping theorem says the uncertainty of the whole set equals the uncertainty between the two groups plus the average of the uncertainties within each group:

\[ H(P_1, \dots, P_M) = H\!\left(P(A), P(B)\right) + P(A)\, H\!\left(\frac{P_1}{P(A)}, \dots, \frac{P_r}{P(A)}\right) + P(B)\, H\!\left(\frac{P_{r+1}}{P(B)}, \dots, \frac{P_M}{P(B)}\right). \]

Inside group A, the conditional probability of outcome \(x_i\) given that the result landed in A is \(P_i / P(A)\). Those conditionals sum to one within the group, so each within-group \(H\) is a proper uncertainty over a proper distribution. Same for group B. The relation follows from Bayes' theorem, the detective that updates odds as clues (here, group membership) arrive.

Read it as a two-stage quiz. Stage one: which group? That costs \(H(P(A), P(B))\). Stage two: which member inside the winning group? That costs the within-group \(H\), but you pay it only with that group's probability as weight. Total bill: between-group charge plus weighted within-group charges.

Three traps recur here. First, feeding raw \(P_i\) into the within-group slot instead of the rescaled \(P_i / P(A)\): unscaled numbers do not sum to one, so they are not probabilities at all. Second, forgetting the weights \(P(A)\) and \(P(B)\): rare groups contribute rarely. Third, believing grouping changes the total: it only re-slices it. The whole stays the whole.

Exam note: State and justify all four properties cold: \(f(1) = 0\) (certainty), monotonic increase in \(M\) (more options, more doubt), the joint-experiment rule \(f(MN) = f(M) + f(N)\) (independent uncertainties add, coin-plus-die as witness), and the grouping theorem expansion above (whole equals between-group plus weighted within-group). The next section runs the grouping theorem on live numbers.

Lottery designers, pollsters, and compression programs all lean on this split: divide outcomes into blocks, price the block choice, then price the within-block choice.

10.5 The Grouping Theorem in Action

10.5.1 The Two-Group Setup

Abstract formulas stick best with numbers inside them. Take four outcomes with probabilities \(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18\), which sum to one. Group A holds the first two events, with probabilities one half and one quarter, so its total is:

\[ P(A) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac34. \]

Group B holds the remaining two events, each with probability one eighth:

\[ P_3 = P_4 = \tfrac18, \qquad P(B) = \tfrac18 + \tfrac18 = \tfrac14. \]

Why these lopsided numbers? Because equal groups would hide the weighting. Here group A carries three times the weight of group B, so the weighted average in the grouping theorem visibly matters. The numbers are rigged to teach, not to describe any real source.

Picture two jars. Jar A holds slips marked with chances 1/2 and 1/4 of the total draw. Jar B holds two slips of 1/8 each. First you learn which jar the draw came from, then which slip inside it.

10.5.2 The Worked Numerical Example

Write the total uncertainty of the whole set as \(H(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18)\). The grouping theorem splits it into three priced pieces.

The between-group piece uses the two group totals:

\[ H\!\left(\tfrac34, \tfrac14\right). \]

The within-group piece for group A needs conditional probabilities, rescaled to sum to one inside the group. Given the draw landed in group A:

\[ \frac{P_1}{P(A)} = \frac{\tfrac12}{\tfrac34} = \frac{2}{3}, \]

and

\[ \frac{P_2}{P(A)} = \frac{\tfrac14}{\tfrac34} = \frac{1}{3}. \]

Those conditionals, \(2/3\) and \(1/3\), sum to one inside group A, giving the within-group term \(H(\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\). Group B's two members are equal halves of their group, giving:

\[ H\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac12\right). \]

Assembled, the grouping theorem on these numbers reads:

\[ H\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18\right) = H\!\left(\tfrac34, \tfrac14\right) + \tfrac34\, H\!\left(\tfrac23, \tfrac13\right) + \tfrac14\, H\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac12\right). \]

Each within-group term carries its group's total as weight: \(\tfrac34\) for A, \(\tfrac14\) for B. Between-group choice first, weighted within-group choices after.

Full trace with the arithmetic on show. Between-group odds are 3-to-1, since \(P(A) = 3/4\) against \(P(B) = 1/4\). Inside A the odds run 2-to-1 (\(2/3\) versus \(1/3\)), because the original \(1/2\) is twice the original \(1/4\) and rescaling by the shared \(3/4\) keeps that ratio. Inside B the odds are even. Weighting follows frequency: the A-interior question gets asked three times in four draws, the B-interior question once in four. Sense-check: the weights \(\tfrac34 + \tfrac14 = 1\) and the conditionals \(\tfrac23 + \tfrac13 = 1\), \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1\) all sum correctly, so every \(H\) on the right sits over a proper distribution.

Run the detective analogy once more. Learning "group A" is the first clue; it leaves a 2-to-1 interior suspect list. Learning "group B" is the rarer clue; it leaves a coin toss. Bayes' rule prices each clue exactly this way.

Scope: This example applies the grouping theorem; it does not prove it. The numbers confirm the shape of the split but the theorem's truth rests on the general Bayes-rule argument, not on one lucky arithmetic case.

The classic slip is writing \(H(\tfrac12, \tfrac14)\) for group A's interior instead of \(H(\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\). Raw probabilities leak group weight into the interior; only rescaled conditionals belong there. A second slip drops the \(\tfrac34\) and \(\tfrac14\) weights. Both mistakes price the interior questions as though they were asked on every draw.

The four-outcome set splits into a between-group question plus two weighted within-group questions, with conditionals \(2/3, 1/3\) and \(1/2, 1/2\). The open question now is sharp: which function satisfies certainty, monotonicity, additivity, and this grouping split all at once? The logarithm is the answer, and the next section proves it.

Medical testing uses the same two-stage bill: first the disease family, then the exact strain within it, weighted by family frequency.

10.6 Establishing the Logarithmic Form

10.6.1 Testing the Candidate f(M) = c log M

Four properties, one suspect. The claim is that a constant times the logarithm,

\[ f(M) = c \log M, \]

with \(c\) a positive constant and the log base left open for now, satisfies every property at once.

First, the power rule. By definition \(f(M^2) = f(M \times M)\), and additivity gives \(f(M) + f(M)\), so \(f(M^2) = 2f(M)\). Iterating:

\[ f(M^k) = k\, f(M) \]

for integer \(k\). For roots, write \(M\) as \((M^{1/n})^n\) and apply the same rule:

\[ f(M) = f\!\left(\left(M^{1/n}\right)^{n}\right) = n\, f\!\left(M^{1/n}\right), \]

so that:

\[ f\!\left(M^{1/n}\right) = \frac{1}{n} f(M). \]

Together, integer and fractional powers give, for any real exponent \(a\):

\[ f(M^a) = a\, f(M). \]

The logarithm passes this test because it carries the identical power rule: \(\log(x^a) = a \log x\). The left side gives \(c \log(M^a) = c\, a \log M\), which is \(a\) times \(c \log M\). Think of the logarithm as counting folds: "how many times must paper be folded to reach \(M\) layers" scales exactly the way \(f\) does when options multiply.

Second, the certainty property. The candidate gives \(f(1) = c \log 1 = 0\), since the logarithm of one is zero in every base. That matches \(f(1) = 0\) exactly. Two properties down, and the candidate also visibly climbs with \(M\) for positive \(c\), matching monotonicity. The hard part is showing no other shape works.

10.6.2 The Sandwich Argument Fixes the Form

Now the squeeze that leaves the logarithm as the only survivor. Fix \(M > 1\) and a positive integer \(r\). Powers of \(M\) march upward without bound while powers of 2 do the same, so some power of 2 must land between consecutive powers of \(M\). There is a unique integer \(k\) with:

\[ M^{k} \le 2^{r} \le M^{k+1}. \]

Concrete sandwich. Take \(M = 4\) and \(r = 2\), so \(2^r = 4\). The inequality becomes \(4^{k} \le 4 \le 4^{k+1}\). Try \(k = 0\): \(1 \le 4 \le 4\) holds on the left but the right edge needs checking; try \(k = 1\): \(4 \le 4 \le 16\) fits snugly. So \(k = 1\) is the unique solution. The power \(2^2 = 4\) is sandwiched between \(4^1\) and \(4^2\). Sense-check: raising \(r\) refines the sandwich, the way more decimal places pin a number tighter.

Because \(f\) is monotonic, applying it across the sandwich keeps the order:

\[ f\!\left(M^{k}\right) \le f\!\left(2^{r}\right) \le f\!\left(M^{k+1}\right). \]

The power rule turns each term into a multiple: \(f(M^k) = k\,f(M)\), \(f(M^{k+1}) = (k+1) f(M)\), \(f(2^r) = r\, f(2)\). So:

\[ k\, f(M) \le r\, f(2) \le (k+1)\, f(M). \]

Divide through by \(r\, f(M)\) (positive, since \(M > 1\) makes \(f(M) > f(1) = 0\)):

\[ \frac{k}{r} \le \frac{f(2)}{f(M)} \le \frac{k+1}{r}. \]

Do the identical steps for the logarithm, which is also monotonic with the same power rule. From the same sandwich \(M^{k} \le 2^{r} \le M^{k+1}\), taking logs gives:

\[ \log\!\left(M^{k}\right) \le \log\!\left(2^{r}\right) \le \log\!\left(M^{k+1}\right). \]

Since \(\log(M^k) = k \log M\), this reads:

\[ k \log M \le r \log 2 \le (k+1) \log M, \]

and dividing by \(r \log M\):

\[ \frac{k}{r} \le \frac{\log 2}{\log M} \le \frac{k+1}{r}. \]

10.6.3 Why the Base Does Not Matter

Both ratios sit in the same vise. The quantity \(f(2)/f(M)\) and the quantity \(\log 2 / \log M\) are squeezed between the same fences, \(k/r\) and \((k+1)/r\). Nothing assumed \(r\) small, so let \(r\) grow. The fences close together: their gap is \((k+1)/r - k/r = 1/r\), which shrinks to zero. Two numbers trapped in a shrinking vise must be equal:

\[ \frac{f(2)}{f(M)} = \frac{\log 2}{\log M}. \]

Rearranged, this is the whole proof in one line:

\[ f(M) = \frac{f(2)}{\log 2}\, \log M = c \log M, \]

with \(c = f(2)/\log 2\) independent of \(M\). The base of the logarithm never entered the argument except through ratios in which it cancels. Pick base 2, base 10, or base \(e\): the fences, the squeeze, and the constant's definition move together and leave \(f\) unchanged.

Picture two runners fenced into the same narrowing corridor. However they jostled early, at the far end they exit shoulder to shoulder. The takeaway: monotonicity plus the power rule forces the logarithmic shape, and the base is a free choice of ruler, like inches versus centimetres.

10.6.4 Choosing the Constant and the Base

Convention now fixes the free choices. Set \(c = 1\) and take base 2, the natural unit for yes/no questions:

\[ f(M) = \log_2 M. \]

Eight options give \(\log_2 8 = 3\), the three celebrity questions. Two options give \(\log_2 2 = 1\), the single coin-toss bit. The circle closes.

Scope: This derivation covers equal probabilities only. Unequal odds need the full \(H\), built next from this \(f\) through the grouping theorem. Do not quote \(\log_2 M\) for lopsided distributions.

A persistent trap is reading "base does not matter" as "value does not matter." Changing base rescales every value by one shared factor, like converting currencies: ratios stay put, absolute readings shift. Fix one base per calculation and keep it.

Exam note: This sandwich argument is a favourite reproduction question. Memorize its skeleton: sandwich \(M^k \le 2^r \le M^{k+1}\), apply monotonic \(f\), insert the power rule, divide to the fenced ratio, repeat for the log, let \(r\) grow, read off \(f(M) = c \log M\). Then set \(c = 1\), base 2.

Map scales, decibel meters, and earthquake magnitudes all exploit the same trick: multiplicative growth in the world becomes additive growth on the logarithmic ruler.

10.7 Deriving Shannon Entropy from the Grouping Theorem

10.7.1 The Two-Event Case H(P, 1-P)

With \(f(M) = \log M\) secured, the general \(H\) is one grouping argument away. Start with two events and the unknown:

\[ H(P, 1-P). \]

Return to the grouping theorem with \(s\) equally likely events. Split them into group A with \(r\) members and group B with the remaining \(s - r\). Group totals are \(r/s\) and \((s-r)/s\). The grouping relation reads:

\[ f(s) = H\!\left(\frac{r}{s}, \frac{s-r}{s}\right) + \frac{r}{s} f(r) + \frac{s-r}{s} f(s-r). \]

Why is the left side \(f(s)\)? Because \(s\) equally likely events carry exactly \(H(\tfrac1s, \dots, \tfrac1s)\), which is the definition of \(f(s)\). The interiors are \(f(r)\) and \(f(s-r)\) for the same reason: inside each group the members are again equally likely. The detective's first clue (which group) plus the rescaled interior lists (which member) reconstruct the whole roster.

Substitute \(f(M) = \log M\) and write \(P = r/s\), so the between-group pair is \((P, 1-P)\):

\[ \log s = H(P, 1-P) + P \log r + (1-P) \log(s-r). \]

Isolate the unknown:

\[ H(P, 1-P) = \log s - P \log r - (1-P) \log(s-r). \]

Now the reshuffle, line by line. The goal is to fold each \(\log s\) fragment into its partner term so that ratios \(r/s\) and \((s-r)/s\) appear. Add and subtract \(P \log s\) and \((1-P) \log s\) together, which jointly add and subtract exactly \(\log s\) since \(P + (1-P) = 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} H(P, 1-P) &= \log s - P \log s - (1-P) \log s - P \log r + P \log s - (1-P) \log(s-r) + (1-P) \log s \\ &\text{(split log s into P and 1-P shares, then regroup)} \\ &= \big[P \log s - P \log r\big] + \big[(1-P) \log s - (1-P) \log(s-r)\big] \\ &\text{(collect the P terms and the 1-P terms; the leading log s cancels)} \\ &= -P \big[\log r - \log s\big] - (1-P) \big[\log(s-r) - \log s\big] \\ &\text{(factor each bracket with a minus sign)} \\ &= -P \log\!\left(\frac{r}{s}\right) - (1-P) \log\!\left(\frac{s-r}{s}\right) \\ &\text{(quotient rule for logs applied to each bracket)} \\ &= -P \log P - (1-P) \log(1-P). \end{aligned} \]

The two-event entropy, fully derived with no skipped algebra:

\[ H(P, 1-P) = -P \log P - (1-P) \log(1-P). \]

Each term pairs a probability with its own logarithm, negated so the sum stays positive. The earlier verbal add-and-subtract of \(P \log s\) is now visible as the regrouping in the first two lines above.

Dimensional check: \(P\) is dimensionless, logs of dimensionless ratios are dimensionless, so \(H\) is a pure number counted in bits (base 2). Boundary check: \(P = 0\) or \(P = 1\) gives zero, matching \(f(1) = 0\).

10.7.2 The General Many-Event Expression

The two-event result extends by repeated grouping to any outcome list \(P_1, P_2, \dots\):

\[ H = -\sum_i P_i \log P_i. \]

This sum is the Shannon entropy: each outcome contributes its probability times the log of that probability, negated because probabilities below one have negative logs. Rare outcomes carry large \(-\log P_i\) (big surprise) but small weight \(P_i\); common outcomes carry small surprise but large weight. The sum prices the average surprise per draw.

The bag-of-marbles picture fits: each colour's share of the bag is \(P_i\), and \(-\log P_i\) scores how startling that colour is when drawn. Entropy averages the startlement. Where the picture breaks: marbles are drawn with replacement here, so odds never shift between draws.

10.7.3 The Coin Toss: One Bit of Uncertainty

Test the formula on a fair coin, \(P_1 = P_2 = \tfrac12\):

\[ H\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac12\right) = -\tfrac12 \log_2 \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 \log_2 \tfrac12. \]

Since \(\log_2 \tfrac12 = -1\), each minus sign cancels it, each term gives \(\tfrac12 \times 1 = \tfrac12\), and:

\[ H\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac12\right) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1. \]

One bit, confirmed two ways. Formula route: the sum above gives exactly 1 bit. Question route: one yes/no question ("heads?") settles the toss. Both routes agree, as they must: calling heads 0 and tails 1 turns the coin into a single binary digit. Sense-check: a biased coin, say \(P = 0.9\), gives \(H \approx 0.47\) bits, less than 1, because the answer is mostly predictable. Maximum doubt needs maximum evenness.

Plot \(H(P, 1-P)\) against \(P\) and the curve rises from zero at \(P = 0\) to a single peak of 1 at \(P = \tfrac12\), then falls symmetrically back to zero at \(P = 1\). The symmetry reflects the relabelling freedom: probability \(P\) on the first event is \(1 - P\) on the second. Horizontal axis probability, vertical axis bits; one hump, peak dead centre; takeaway: even odds maximize uncertainty.

Scope: This \(H\) prices single draws from a fixed distribution. Runs of draws, correlated draws, and message meaning all need heavier tools. Do not apply \(-\sum P_i \log P_i\) to odds that drift mid-experiment.

Three slips stalk this formula. First, dropping the minus sign and reporting negative entropy: probabilities sit below one, their logs are negative, and only the leading minus restores positivity. Second, feeding percentages (50) instead of probabilities (0.5) into the log. Third, mixing log bases mid-sum: pick base 2 for bits and hold it across every term.

Exam note: Derive \(H(P, 1-P) = -P \log P - (1-P) \log(1-P)\) from the grouping split \(f(s) = H(r/s, (s-r)/s) + (r/s) f(r) + ((s-r)/s) f(s-r)\) with \(f = \log\), then generalize to \(H = -\sum_i P_i \log P_i\). The coin toss \(H(\tfrac12, \tfrac12) = 1\) bit is the calibration point for the entire subject.

Insurance tables, weather odds, and quiz-show wagers all price doubt this way: weight each surprise by its chance and add.

10.8 Entropy versus the Decision Tree: A Worked Comparison

10.8.1 The Five-Event Distribution

Theory earns trust on numbers. Let a random variable have five outcomes \(x_1\) through \(x_5\) with:

\[ P_1 = 0.3,\quad P_2 = 0.2,\quad P_3 = 0.2,\quad P_4 = 0.15,\quad P_5 = 0.15. \]

These sum to \(0.3 + 0.2 + 0.2 + 0.15 + 0.15 = 1\), a proper distribution. Unlike the celebrity game, the odds here are uneven: \(x_1\) leads, \(x_4\) and \(x_5\) trail. That unevenness is the whole point, because uneven odds let smart questioning beat the flat \(\log_2 5 \approx 2.32\).

10.8.2 Shannon Entropy of the Five Events

Apply \(H = -\sum_i P_i \log_2 P_i\) term by term:

\[ H = -0.3 \log_2 0.3 - 0.2 \log_2 0.2 - 0.2 \log_2 0.2 - 0.15 \log_2 0.15 - 0.15 \log_2 0.15. \]

Term-by-term evaluation. Convert with \(\log_2 y = \ln y / \ln 2\). Then \(-0.3 \log_2 0.3 = -0.3 \times (-1.737) = 0.521\); each \(-0.2 \log_2 0.2 = -0.2 \times (-2.322) = 0.464\), and there are two, giving \(0.929\); each \(-0.15 \log_2 0.15 = -0.15 \times (-2.737) = 0.411\), and there are two, giving \(0.821\). Adding up:

\[ H = 0.521 + 0.929 + 0.821 = 2.27 \text{ bits}. \]

Sense-check: the answer sits below \(\log_2 5 \approx 2.32\) (uneven odds carry less doubt than even odds) and above 2 (five options need more than two flat bits). 2.27 lands exactly in that window.

So the average uncertainty is 2.27 bits per draw.

10.8.3 The Decision Tree for the Same Distribution

Now settle the same distribution with questions. First question: is the outcome in \(\{x_1, x_2\}\)? Yes (total chance \(0.5\)) leaves two suspects; ask "is it \(x_1\)?" and finish in two questions for \(x_1\) or \(x_2\). No (total chance \(0.5\)) leaves \(\{x_3, x_4, x_5\}\); ask "is it \(x_3\)?" and finish in two questions for \(x_3\). Otherwise one suspect pair remains; ask "is it \(x_4\)?" and finish in three questions for \(x_4\) or \(x_5\).

Average question count. Weight each depth by its probability:

\[ \bar{Q} = 0.3 \times 2 + 0.2 \times 2 + 0.2 \times 2 + 0.15 \times 3 + 0.15 \times 3 = 2.3. \]

Line by line: \(0.3 \times 2 = 0.6\), \(0.2 \times 2 = 0.4\) twice, \(0.15 \times 3 = 0.45\) twice, totalling \(0.6 + 0.4 + 0.4 + 0.45 + 0.45 = 2.3\) questions. Note the correction built in here: \(x_4\) and \(x_5\) each need three questions down their longer branch, so their factors are 3, not 2. Sense-check: the average 2.3 sits just above the entropy 2.27, exactly where a good-but-imperfect tree should land.

Picture the tree: a short bushy left side (two-question leaves for the likely trio \(x_1, x_2, x_3\)) and a taller thin right side (three-question leaves for the rare pair). Frequent outcomes got short paths; rare ones got long paths. That is the design lesson in one glance.

10.8.4 Why the Two Answers Differ

The tree averages 2.3 questions; the entropy says 2.27 bits. The gap is real, not rounding.

Shannon entropy is the minimum average question count over all possible trees. Any particular tree, like the one just built, sits on or above it. A cleverer set of questions can shave the average toward 2.27, but no questioning scheme, and no uniquely decipherable code built from it, can go below. The tree we built is good but not optimal, and the 0.03 gap is the price of that imperfection.

Think of entropy as the sea level and every tree as a boat floating on or above it. Better trees draw less water; none sail underground.

Scope: The floor holds for uniquely decipherable codes, where every bit stream parses one way. Loose codes that sometimes misparse can cheat below the floor on paper, but their answers cannot be trusted. The guarantee prices reliable communication, not lucky guesses.

Do not read 2.27 as "each draw needs 2.27 questions." Fractional bits price averages over many draws, not single draws. One draw always costs a whole number of questions; a thousand draws average out near 2270 questions total.

Exam note: Reproduce this comparison cold: entropy \(H = 2.27\) bits from the five-term sum, tree average \(\bar{Q} = 2.3\) from the weighted depths, and the moral that Shannon entropy is the minimum average length, so the tree count sits above it. This gap is the doorway into the coding theorem.

Parcel sorters and emergency-room triage run the same trade: common cases get the short path, rare cases absorb the longer walk, and the average wait hugs its theoretical floor.

10.9 Why the Word "Entropy": Boltzmann and Statistical Entropy

10.9.1 Macro states and Micro states

Why does information theory borrow a word from steam engines? Because the formulas match, and the match is not chance. The word entropy was introduced by Boltzmann for the statistical physics of gases, and Shannon's \(H\) turned out to be its twin.

A macroscopic state (macrostate) is what you see from the gross picture of the whole ensemble: how many particles sit in each region, without naming names. A microscopic state (microstate) names every particle: particle 1 here, particle 2 there, particle 3 elsewhere. Many microstates can share one macrostate, the way many seating charts fill the same headcount per table. Boltzmann's result is that the entropy of the system is:

\[ S = k \log W, \]

where \(W\) is the count of microstates behind the macrostate and \(k\) is Boltzmann's constant linking the count to heat and temperature.

The mapping to information: a macrostate is the question ("how many per cell?"), a microstate is the full answer ("which particle where?"). More microstates per macrostate means more missing detail, which is more uncertainty, which is more entropy. Same shape of doubt, two fields of study.

10.9.2 Counting Configurations

Make it concrete. Take \(N\) particles in a volume split into \(L\) identical cells. To fix a microstate, number the particles 1 to \(N\) and record each one's cell. To fix a macrostate, record only headcounts:

\[ n_1 \text{ in cell 1},\quad n_2 \text{ in cell 2},\quad \dots,\quad n_L \text{ in cell } L, \]

with

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{L} n_i = N. \]

Two particles swapping cells leave every headcount unchanged, so the macrostate survives while the microstate changes. The count of microstates behind one macrostate is the multinomial count from permutations and combinations:

\[ W = \frac{N!}{n_1!\, n_2!\, \cdots\, n_L!}. \]

Small-numbers illustration. Put \(N = 4\) labelled balls into \(L = 2\) boxes with headcounts \(n_1 = 3\), \(n_2 = 1\). The formula gives \(W = 4!/(3!\,1!) = 4\): pick which of the four balls sits alone in box 2. Four microstates, one macrostate. Even headcounts \(n_1 = n_2 = 2\) give \(W = 4!/(2!\,2!) = 6\), more microstates behind a more balanced macrostate. Sense-check: balanced splits always admit more seatings than lopsided ones, previewing why systems drift toward balance.

10.9.3 The Boltzmann Entropy

With that \(W\), Boltzmann's entropy is \(S = k \log W\). The constant \(k\) ties the counting to thermometers: Boltzmann proposed that the multiplier on \(\log W\) be identified with a universal constant, now carrying his name, so the statistical entropy reads \(k \log W\) in physics units.

Strip the physics units and the shape remains: entropy grows as the log of the configuration count. Double the configurations and entropy rises by \(k \log 2\); square them and it doubles. The logarithm turns multiplicative plenty into additive score, exactly as \(f(M) = \log M\) did for equally likely options. That shared log-of-count shape is why Shannon's quantity earned Boltzmann's name.

Picture the volume as an ice-cube tray with \(L\) wells and \(N\) marbles. Each headcount pattern is one tray silhouette. \(W\) counts how many marble-by-marble fillings cast that silhouette. Crowded-together silhouettes admit few fillings; spread-out silhouettes admit armies of them. The takeaway: entropy scores silhouette roominess.

Scope: The physics constant \(k\) and the temperature machinery around it play no role in information theory. Communication theory sets \(k = 1\) (and base 2 for bits) and keeps only the counting shape. Do not carry joules and kelvins into the coding theorem.

A common mix-up is calling \(W\) itself the entropy. It is not: \(W\) is a raw count, and entropy is its logarithm. Counts multiply when systems combine; entropies add. The log is what makes entropy composable.

Boltzmann entropy \(S = k \log W\) prices a macrostate by the log of its microstate count \(W = N!/(n_1! \cdots n_L!)\), where the macrostate lists headcounts and the microstate names every particle. The next section grinds that count through Stirling's approximation and out pops Shannon's sum.

Warehouse managers live this count daily: one headcount report, thousands of box-by-box arrangements behind it, and the roomiest report the likeliest to match reality.

10.10 From Configuration Count to Shannon Entropy

10.10.1 Applying Stirling's Approximation

Start from the configuration count and take logs:

\[ \log W = \log N! - \sum_i \log n_i!. \]

The numbers are molecular-assembly large, so Stirling's approximation applies. For large \(n\) it replaces the log of a factorial by:

\[ \log n! \approx n \log n - n. \]

Apply it to every factorial:

\[ \log W \approx \big(N \log N - N\big) - \sum_i \big(n_i \log n_i - n_i\big). \]

The linear leftovers cancel. The \(-N\) from the first term meets \(+\sum_i n_i\) from the distributed minus sign, and \(\sum_i n_i = N\), so they annihilate. What survives is:

\[ \log W = N \log N - \sum_i n_i \log n_i. \]

No approximation survives in the cancellation itself: it is exact bookkeeping once Stirling's form is accepted.

Check the move with tiny numbers to trust it. Take \(N = 4\), \(n_1 = 3\), \(n_2 = 1\). Stirling is crude here, but the cancellation pattern is exact: \(-4 + (3 + 1) = 0\) regardless of the log terms. The structure holds at every scale.

10.10.2 Rewriting with Probabilities

Now trade headcounts for probabilities. The chance that one picked particle sits in cell \(i\) is:

\[ p_i = \frac{n_i}{N}, \qquad n_i = N p_i. \]

Substitute \(n_i = N p_i\) into the log count:

\[ \log W = N \log N - \sum_i N p_i \log(N p_i). \]

Expand the product inside the log, \(\log(N p_i) = \log N + \log p_i\):

\[ \log W = N \log N - \sum_i N p_i \big(\log N + \log p_i\big). \]

Pull the \(i\)-free \(\log N\) out of the sum:

\[ \log W = N \log N - N \log N \sum_i p_i - N \sum_i p_i \log p_i. \]

Since \(\sum_i p_i = 1\), the first two terms cancel exactly, leaving:

\[ \log W = -N \sum_i p_i \log p_i. \]

Divide by \(N\) for the per-particle average:

\[ \frac{1}{N} \log W = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i. \]

That is Shannon's sum, derived from gas counting. The statistical expression from physics and the information expression from communication are the same function of the probabilities. The \(0 \log 0\) terms that appear for empty cells are read as limits and equal zero, matching the convention that impossible events contribute nothing.

The full chain in one breath: multinomial count, log, Stirling, cancel linear terms, insert \(p_i = n_i/N\), expand, cancel \(\log N\) terms, divide by \(N\). Each step is reversible algebra except Stirling, whose error shrinks as \(N\) grows.

10.10.3 All in One Cell versus Spread in Two

Numbers make the meaning match too. Split the volume into about \(10^6\) identical cells, \(L = 10^6\).

First macrostate: every particle crammed into a single cell. Then \(p_i = 1\) for that cell and \(0\) elsewhere. The Shannon sum gives \(-1 \log 1 = 0\) plus zeros, so:

\[ S = 0. \]

One cell, no choice, no entropy. This is \(f(1) = 0\) wearing a physics coat.

Second macrostate: particles spread evenly across two cells. Each particle sits in either cell with probability one half, so the per-particle entropy is:

\[ -\tfrac12 \log_2 \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 \log_2 \tfrac12 = 1 \text{ bit} = \log 2. \]

How much likelier is spread than crammed? The draft counts the two-cell macrostate by choosing which two cells out of \(10^6\) host the particles:

\[ W = {}^{10^6}\!C_2 = \frac{10^6!}{2!\,(10^6 - 2)!} = \frac{10^6 (10^6 - 1)}{2} \approx 5 \times 10^{11}. \]

The last step keeps the dominant term: \(10^6 \times 10^6 = 10^{12}\), halved to \(5 \times 10^{11}\), with the \(-10^6\) beside \(10^{12}\) negligible. Against the single-cell macrostate (one choice of cell, up to ordering), the relative weight of the spread state is:

\[ \frac{5 \times 10^{11}}{1 + 5 \times 10^{11}} \approx 1 - 10^{-5}. \]

A probability extremely close to one: the spread arrangement wins by a landslide. Note the reading used here: \(W\) counts choices of cells, while the per-particle odds within the chosen pair stay one half each. Sense-check: \(5 \times 10^{11}\) towers over 1, so drifting from one cell to two is drift toward overwhelming likelihood.

Systems drift toward the state of maximum entropy. That is why entropy reads as disorder in physics: more spread means more microstates, more microstates mean higher entropy, and the system lands where the counting is richest. Shannon entropy reads the same way for uncertainty: more even odds mean higher entropy. The two notions line up in formula and in meaning.

Picture a drop of ink in water. Crammed in one corner: one macrostate, few microstates, zero entropy. Diffused evenly: the headcounts every eye sees, backed by astronomical microstate counts. The takeaway: maximum entropy is simply maximum roominess, and nature fills rooms.

Scope: Stirling needs large \(n_i\). For nearly empty cells the approximation frays, and exact factorials must return. The lecture's \(N\) is molecular, so the error is dust, but do not quote Stirling for dice with six faces.

Do not confuse the combination count of cells with the particle distribution count: \({}^{10^6}C_2\) picks the host cells, while the \(50\)-50 split divides the particles within them. Both counts favour spreading, but they count different choices.

Stirling turns \(\log W\) into \(N \log N - \sum n_i \log n_i\), probabilities turn that into \(-N \sum p_i \log p_i\), and dividing by \(N\) lands on Shannon's sum. The one-cell state scores zero entropy; the two-cell spread scores \(\log 2\) per particle and wins with probability near one. Physics disorder and information uncertainty are one quantity.

Perfume crossing a room, heat evening out in a bar, and shuffling a deck all march the same road: from cramped order toward roomy disorder.

10.11 The Communication System and Coding

10.11.1 The Block Diagram of a Communication System

Entropy defined, attention turns to sending messages. A communication system has five blocks in a row. A source generates the message. An encoder turns it into binary digits. A channel carries them: the old telegraph wire, a fibre-optic line, a microwave link, whatever the situation provides. A receiver takes the signal in. A decoder turns the code back into the original message.

The channel is never clean. The environment superimposes random disturbances, called noise, on whatever travels. A microwave link catches weather static; a fibre line catches thermal jitter. This is the same problem met in quantum communication, where stray interactions corrupt qubits. The whole job of the system is to push messages through that dirt with as few errors as possible.

Picture the chain left to right: mouth, envelope, road, ear, letter-opener. Source speaks, encoder seals bits into an envelope, channel roads it through a storm, receiver catches it, decoder opens it. The takeaway: every block except the channel exists to fight the channel.

10.11.2 Information Capacity

How fast can the road carry goods? The information capacity of a system is the rate of information, usually quoted in kilobits per second, that the channel can carry with the least error. The advertised figure is a raw capacity measured in a clean lab. Real capacity runs lower, because noise eats into it.

A fibre line rated at 100 kilobits per second raw may deliver far less once interference, retransmissions, and error correction take their cut. Raw is the brochure; real is the road in rain. The coding theorem ahead prices exactly how much of the raw rate survives.

Scope: This lecture prices noiseless coding only: short codes for frequent symbols, with no corruption in flight. Noisy channels, where bits flip mid-road and redundancy must repair them, belong to the noisy coding theorem, a later story.

10.11.3 A DNA Coding Example

Codes are the tool that fights the loss. Suppose DNA letters A, C, G, T must cross a binary channel. The natural first code spends two bits per letter:

\[ A = 00,\quad C = 01,\quad G = 10,\quad T = 11. \]

Four letters, two bits each, no thought needed.

Now the odds: A appears with probability 0.4, C with 0.3, G with 0.15, T with 0.15. In this flat code every letter still costs two bits, so the average length is:

\[ \bar{n} = 2 \text{ bits per letter}. \]

Average-length arithmetic. Weight each code length by its frequency: \(\bar{n} = 0.4 \times 2 + 0.3 \times 2 + 0.15 \times 2 + 0.15 \times 2 = 2\). Every term pays the same 2-bit fare, so the odds do not matter yet. Common A costs the same as rare T. Sense-check: with four letters and fixed two-bit slots, 2 bits per letter is both the average and the only possible value.

The waste is visible: frequent A burns as much channel as rare T. Morse code never made that mistake: E, the commonest English letter, gets a single dot, while rare Q gets dash-dash-dot-dash. The next section applies the Morse lesson to DNA.

Fixed-length codes are simple to build and simple to parse, which is why hardware loves them. Their price is paid in channel time: every frequent symbol overpays. Use them when simplicity beats throughput; drop them when the bill matters.

A communication line runs source, encoder, channel, receiver, decoder, with noise attacking mid-road and capacity measuring the surviving rate. The flat DNA code at 2 bits per letter ignores the odds and overpays on common letters. Uneven codes that favour frequent symbols come next.

Postal codes, phone keyboards, and barcode tables all face the same design choice: flat slots for simplicity or short slots for frequent items.

10.12 Shannon's Noiseless Coding Theorem

10.12.1 A Shorter DNA Code and Its Gain

The flat code spends two bits on every DNA letter though A and C dwarf G and T. Common letters deserve short codewords and rare letters longer ones. Here is a purpose-built alternative under the same odds (A 0.4, C 0.3, G 0.15, T 0.15):

\[ A = 0,\quad C = 10,\quad G = 110,\quad T = 111. \]

A costs one bit, C two bits, G and T three bits each.

Average length of the short code. Weight each length by its frequency:

\[ \bar{n} = 0.4 \times 1 + 0.3 \times 2 + 0.15 \times 3 + 0.15 \times 3 = 0.4 + 0.6 + 0.45 + 0.45 = 1.9 \text{ bits per letter}. \]

Note the corrected arithmetic: the fourth letter's codeword 111 has length three, so its factor is 3, giving \(0.15 \times 3 = 0.45\) and the total 1.9. Sense-check: 1.9 beats the flat 2.0 because the commonest letter A dropped from two bits to one while only the rare pair paid an extra bit.

Saving a tenth of a bit per letter sounds small until millions of letters cross the channel. Small per-unit savings scale into real throughput.

10.12.2 The Entropy Lower Bound for the DNA Source

Can any code do better than 1.9? The answer depends only on the source odds, not on the code's cleverness. Compute the Shannon entropy of the DNA source:

\[ H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i = -0.4 \log_2 0.4 - 0.3 \log_2 0.3 - 0.3 \log_2 0.15. \]

Entropy arithmetic. The two 0.15 terms merge into one \(-0.3 \log_2 0.15\), which is why the coefficient reads 0.3 rather than 0.15. Term by term with \(\log_2 y = \ln y / \ln 2\): \(-0.4 \log_2 0.4 = -0.4 \times (-1.322) = 0.529\); \(-0.3 \log_2 0.3 = -0.3 \times (-1.737) = 0.521\); \(-0.3 \log_2 0.15 = -0.3 \times (-2.737) = 0.821\). Total:

\[ H = 0.529 + 0.521 + 0.821 = 1.871 \text{ bits}. \]

Most calculators give natural or base-10 logs, so convert before trusting the display. Sense-check: 1.871 sits below the flat 2.0 and just under the clever 1.9, exactly where a true floor should sit.

The 1.9-bit code hugs the 1.871-bit floor. No code, however clever, averages below it. That floor is Shannon's noiseless coding theorem in action.

10.12.3 Prefix and Non-Prefix Codes

The theorem covers codes the receiver can actually parse. A code is a prefix code (also called prefix-free) when no codeword forms the start of another codeword. The failure mode is immediate: with one letter coded 0 and another coded 01, the codeword 0 prefixes 01, so a stream opening with 0 leaves the receiver guessing whether the letter ended or continues. Likewise 01 prefixing 011 breaks parsing.

Two clean prefix-free examples. Fixed-length:

\[ A = 00,\quad B = 01,\quad C = 10,\quad D = 11, \]

where equal lengths make prefixing impossible. Variable-length:

\[ A = 0,\quad B = 10,\quad C = 110,\quad D = 111, \]

where no codeword begins with any other. The decoding rule this enforces: once 0 is taken by A, no other codeword may start with 0; once 10 is taken by B, nothing else may start with 10, so 101 or 100 are forbidden while 110 and 111 stay legal. The draft's label "non-prefix codes" for this condition is reconciled here against the standard name: the condition "no codeword is a prefix of another" defines a prefix (prefix-free) code, and codes violating it are non-prefix codes.

Decoding a prefix-free stream needs no lookahead and no backtracking: read bits until a codeword completes, emit the letter, start fresh. That instant decodability is why real channels insist on the condition.

10.12.4 Statement of the Coding Theorem

Shannon's noiseless coding theorem: for a uniquely decipherable code where letter \(x_i\) occurs with probability \(P_i\) and carries code length \(n_i\), the average length obeys:

\[ \sum_i n_i P_i \ge H = -\sum_i P_i \log P_i. \]

The average code length cannot sink below the entropy. Entropy is the compression floor; uniquely decipherable prefix codes are the construction crew building down toward it.

Compare the two DNA results against the floor: flat code 2.0, clever code 1.9, floor 1.871. Each smarter design crept closer; none crosses. The earlier five-event tree told the same story at 2.3 versus 2.27.

Scope: This floor assumes long messages and clean channels. Short messages pay rounding overhead (a one-letter message still needs whole bits), and noisy channels pay repair overhead. The theorem prices the best average over long, clean runs.

Two traps end this chapter. First, quoting entropy with mixed log bases: bits need base 2 throughout. Second, calling any short code optimal: only codes approaching the entropy floor earn that name, and the prefix-free condition is the ticket of admission.

Exam note: State the inequality \(\sum_i n_i P_i \ge H\) with each symbol named, distinguish prefix-free codes (no codeword starts another; instantly decodable) from non-prefix codes (ambiguous streams), and reproduce the DNA numbers: flat 2.0, short 1.9, floor 1.871. The quantum analogue of this entropy story starts next.

Zip files, image formats, and genome databases all press against this floor daily: frequent symbols short, rare symbols long, average length hovering just above entropy.

10.13 Von Neumann Entropy

10.13.1 From Classical to Quantum Ensembles

Take stock. A random variable \(X\) tied to a physical process takes outcomes \(x_1, \dots, x_M\) with probabilities \(P_1, \dots, P_M\) summing to one. Its average uncertainty is the Shannon entropy:

\[ H = -\sum_i P_i \log P_i, \]

conventionally base 2. Two facts matter going forward: it depends only on the probabilities, never on the outcome values; and it floors compression, so uniquely decipherable prefix codes average at least \(H\).

Von Neumann generalized this to quantum systems, by an argument not reproduced here. Where Shannon entropy prices the uncertainty of a classical event, Von Neumann entropy prices our ignorance of a quantum system. The classical object was a probability distribution; the quantum object is an ensemble described by a density matrix \(\rho\). A distribution lists chances of distinct outcomes; a density matrix packs chances plus quantum coherence into one operator.

The bridge is close: diagonal density matrices behave like classical distributions, with eigenvalues playing the role of probabilities. Off-diagonal entries add the quantum spice.

10.13.2 The Definition

Recall the density-matrix toolkit. For an observable \(A\) with operator form, the expectation value is:

\[ \langle A \rangle = \operatorname{Tr}(\rho A), \]

where \(\rho\) is the density matrix (\(2 \times 2\) for one qubit, \(2^n \times 2^n\) for \(n\) qubits) and \(\operatorname{Tr}\) sums diagonal entries. The density matrix carries unit trace:

\[ \operatorname{Tr} \rho = 1. \]

For a pure system it is idempotent:

\[ \rho^2 = \rho. \]

The Von Neumann entropy measures the mixedness of the ensemble, defined by analogy with \(-\sum_i P_i \log P_i\) as:

\[ S(\rho) = -\operatorname{Tr}\!\big(\rho \log \rho\big). \]

The log of a matrix acts on its eigenvalues: diagonalize \(\rho\), take the log of each eigenvalue, transform back. As before the log base is free, with base 2 traditional for bits. A sharp ensemble (one dominant eigenvalue) scores low; a flat ensemble scores high.

Shape check: if \(\rho\) is diagonal with entries \(P_i\), the trace reduces to \(-\sum P_i \log P_i\), Shannon's form exactly. The quantum definition swallows the classical one whole.

10.13.3 Vanishing for Pure States

Entropy must not depend on the basis used to compute it, and the trace guarantees exactly that: traces cycle, so rotating the basis leaves \(S\) fixed. Switch to the basis where \(\rho\) is diagonal. The trace becomes a sum over eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\):

\[ S(\rho) = -\sum_i \lambda_i \log \lambda_i. \]

Pure state scores zero. A pure system has one eigenvalue 1 and the rest 0. Substituting:

\[ S(\rho) = -1 \log 1 - 0 \log 0 = 0, \]

with \(0 \log 0\) read as a limit equal to 0, the same convention as Shannon theory: impossible outcomes contribute nothing. This matches the classical demand that a perfectly certain state carries no uncertainty. Sense-check: \(\rho^2 = \rho\) forces eigenvalues to 0 or 1, and unit trace allows exactly one 1, so zero entropy is forced, not assumed.

10.13.4 The Maximum Value

What scores highest? Let the system live in \(D\) dimensions. Eigenvalues must sum to one (unit trace) and stay nonnegative. The most even spread puts weight \(1/D\) on each of \(D\) eigenvalues:

\[ S(\rho)_{\max} = -\sum_{i=1}^{D} \frac{1}{D} \log \frac{1}{D} = \log D. \]

Maximum Von Neumann entropy equals \(\log D\), achieved by the completely mixed state \(\rho = I/D\). This echoes the classical \(f(M) = \log M\) for \(M\) equal options: \(D\) evenly weighted eigenvalues are \(D\) evenly likely alternatives. The lecture sketches the intuition (even spreading is maximally mixed, and entropy measures mixedness) rather than the full proof that nothing beats it.

Calibrate with a qubit, \(D = 2\): pure states score 0, the completely mixed state scores \(\log_2 2 = 1\) bit. The whole qubit range fits between those rails.

Scope: \(D\) counts Hilbert-space dimensions (2 for one qubit, 4 for two qubits), not particle counts or outcome labels. A two-qubit system maxes at \(\log_2 4 = 2\) bits, not 1.

Do not confuse a superposition with a mixture. A balanced superposition is pure (one eigenvalue 1, entropy 0) while the fifty-fifty mixture is maximally mixed (two eigenvalues \(1/2\), entropy 1). Same fifty-fifty appearance on one measurement axis, opposite entropy. The worked examples next drive this home.

Exam note: Write \(S(\rho) = -\operatorname{Tr}(\rho \log \rho)\), diagonalize to \(-\sum_i \lambda_i \log \lambda_i\), and quote the rails: 0 for pure states, \(\log D\) maximum for the completely mixed state. Basis independence (via the trace) is the one-line justification for diagonalizing.

Quantum tomography labs and random-number testers use this scale daily: near-zero entropy means a clean pure preparation, near-maximum means noise has flattened it.

10.14 Properties and Worked Examples of Von Neumann Entropy

10.14.1 Subadditivity for Bipartite Systems

Composite systems, like multi-qubit registers, obey one more rule. For a joint state \(\rho_{AB}\) split into parts A and B:

\[ S(\rho_{AB}) \le S(\rho_A) + S(\rho_B). \]

Subadditivity: the entropy of the whole is at most the sum of the entropies of the parts. Equality holds exactly when the composite factorizes:

\[ \rho_{AB} = \rho_A \otimes \rho_B, \]

meaning the parts are uncorrelated, each carrying its own independent uncertainty. Correlated parts share uncertainty, so the whole counts less than the sum. The lecture illustrates rather than fully proves this; the examples below are the evidence.

The classical echo is \(H(X,Y) \le H(X) + H(Y)\) with equality for independent variables. Quantum correlation can push the gap much wider, even to negative conditional entropy, as the entangled example shows.

10.14.2 Worked Example: The Pure Superposition State

Start from any pure state \(\rho\): eigenvalues 1 and 0, entropy 0. Take the balanced superposition:

\[ |\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\big). \]

Density matrix and eigenvalues. The density matrix is the outer product \(\rho = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\). With \(|0\rangle = (1, 0)\) and \(|1\rangle = (0, 1)\) as columns and the bras as rows, the outer product gives:

\[ \rho = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

The \(1/2\) comes from \(1/\sqrt{2}\) appearing twice. Trace is \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1\); determinant is \(\tfrac14 - \tfrac14 = 0\). Eigenvalues of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix with trace 1 and determinant 0 are 1 and 0. So:

\[ S(\rho) = -1 \log 1 - 0 \log 0 = 0, \]

as every pure state must. Sense-check: \(\rho^2 = \rho\) holds here (multiply it out: the factor \(1/4\) times the all-ones square gives back \(\rho\)), confirming purity directly.

Superposition is not mixture: this state gives fifty-fifty results on a computational-basis measurement yet carries zero entropy, because a different measurement axis reveals it perfectly.

10.14.3 Worked Example: The Maximally Mixed State

Contrast with the maximally mixed qubit:

\[ \rho = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & 0 \\ 0 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Entropy at the ceiling. Already diagonal, eigenvalues \(\tfrac12\) and \(\tfrac12\):

\[ S(\rho) = -\tfrac12 \log_2 \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 \log_2 \tfrac12 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1 = \log_2 2. \]

For a qubit \(D = 2\), the ceiling \(\log D = \log_2 2 = 1\). Pure gives 0, maximally mixed gives the full 1 bit. Sense-check: every measurement axis returns fifty-fifty here, so no axis reveals anything, which is exactly what maximum entropy means.

Side by side the lesson is stark. Balanced superposition: fifty-fifty on one axis, perfectly known on another, entropy 0. Maximal mixture: fifty-fifty on every axis, entropy 1. Same one-axis statistics, opposite knowledge.

10.14.4 Worked Example: Entanglement and Partial Trace

The finale shows subadditivity with teeth. Take the entangled two-qubit pure state:

\[ |\psi\rangle = \cos\theta\, |00\rangle + \sin\theta\, |11\rangle. \]

Jointly pure, so joint entropy vanishes:

\[ S(\rho_{AB}) = 0. \]

The parts tell another story. Trace out qubit B to get qubit A's reduced state. The joint density matrix is:

\[ \rho_{AB} = \big(\cos\theta\, |00\rangle + \sin\theta\, |11\rangle\big)\big(\cos\theta\, \langle 00| + \sin\theta\, \langle 11|\big). \]

Tracing B contracts the B-side bras against kets. Matching pairs survive: \(\langle 0|0\rangle = 1\) keeps the \(\cos^2\theta\) term, \(\langle 1|1\rangle = 1\) keeps the \(\sin^2\theta\) term. Cross terms die because \(\langle 0|1\rangle = \langle 1|0\rangle = 0\). The reduced state is:

\[ \rho_A = \cos^2\theta\, |0\rangle\langle 0| + \sin^2\theta\, |1\rangle\langle 1|. \]

Already diagonal in the computational basis, with eigenvalues \(\cos^2\theta\) and \(\sin^2\theta\) summing to one: a proper density matrix. The same holds for \(\rho_B\) by symmetry.

Its entropy reads:

\[ S_A = -\cos^2\theta \log(\cos^2\theta) - \sin^2\theta \log(\sin^2\theta) = -2\cos^2\theta \log(\cos\theta) - 2\sin^2\theta \log(\sin\theta). \]

Subadditivity on display. The two logarithm forms match because \(\log(\cos^2\theta) = 2\log(\cos\theta)\), and likewise for sine. By symmetry \(S_B = S_A\), so:

\[ S_A + S_B = -4\Big[\cos^2\theta \log(\cos\theta) + \sin^2\theta \log(\sin\theta)\Big]. \]

Each of \(\cos\theta\), \(\sin\theta\) is at most 1, so each log is negative or zero, and the leading minus makes the sum positive. With \(S(\rho_{AB}) = 0\),

\[ S(\rho_{AB}) = 0 \le S_A + S_B, \]

exactly subadditivity. At \(\theta = \pi/4\) (the Bell state), \(\cos^2 = \sin^2 = \tfrac12\), each marginal hits the full 1 bit while the joint sits at 0: maximally uncertain parts, perfectly known whole. At \(\theta = 0\) the state un-entangles to \(|00\rangle\) and both marginals drop to 0. Sense-check: tuning \(\theta\) from 0 to \(\pi/4\) dials entanglement from none to maximal, and the marginal entropy climbs with it.

A pure entangled state has zero joint entropy but positive marginal entropies. Classically that sentence is nonsense: knowing the whole exactly must bound each part's doubt. Quantumly the whole can be perfectly known while each part looks random, because the knowledge lives in the correlations between parts, visible only to joint measurements.

A theorem much like Shannon's noiseless coding theorem governs qubit compression, with Von Neumann entropy as the floor. Parallel quantum theorems exist but lie beyond this lecture. The coming lectures build the quantum communication protocols on this foundation.

Scope: The partial-trace recipe used here (contract the traced qubit's bras against kets, keep matches, drop overlaps) works for any bipartite pure state. Mixed joint states need the full operator trace; do not hand-wave cross terms there.

Do not equate "reduced state looks mixed" with "the qubit decohered." Here each marginal looks mixed only because its partner was ignored. The pair together stays pure. Ignorance of the partner is not damage to the qubit.

Exam note: State subadditivity \(S(\rho_{AB}) \le S(\rho_A) + S(\rho_B)\) with equality for product states, then illustrate with \(|\psi\rangle = \cos\theta|00\rangle + \sin\theta|11\rangle\): joint entropy 0, reduced state \(\rho_A = \cos^2\theta|0\rangle\langle0| + \sin^2\theta|1\rangle\langle1|\), marginal entropy \(-2\cos^2\theta\log\cos\theta - 2\sin^2\theta\log\sin\theta\), sum above the joint. Maximum gap at \(\theta = \pi/4\).

Entanglement-verification labs and quantum-key-distribution links test exactly this signature: joint purity with marginal randomness means entanglement is present.

Key Industry Applications

Real-world: data compression. Shannon entropy sets the exact floor on the average number of bits needed to encode a source, so it is the theoretical limit against which any compressor for text, images, or DNA sequence data is measured. The DNA example shows the practical move in numbers: the flat code spends 2 bits per letter, the short code with brief words for common letters reaches 1.9, and the 1.871-bit entropy floor says no further saving is possible. Every modern codec, from text archives to genome stores, presses against that floor with frequent symbols kept short.

Real-world: communication channels. The communication-system chain of source, encoder, channel, receiver, and decoder is the everyday architecture of telegraph wires, fibre-optic lines, and microwave links. Each channel corrupts the signal with external noise, and the design goal is to carry the information rate with the least error. Raw capacity quoted in kilobits per second is always optimistic beside real capacity once noise takes its cut, and the coding theorem prices how much survives.

Real-world: error-free parsing in codes. The prefix-free condition is the everyday requirement that a receiver decode a bit stream unambiguously as it arrives, with no lookahead or backtracking. Codes that break it, like pairing 0 with 01, stall the receiver at the first ambiguous bit. Barcodes, Morse timing, and network packet headers all obey some version of this instant-decodability rule.

Real-world: quantum error correction and quantum communication. The same uncertainty-measurement ideas carry over to qubits. Von Neumann entropy is the quantum analogue of Shannon entropy: it governs the compression limit for quantum states and makes entanglement visible, since a pure two-qubit state has zero total entropy while each qubit alone can look maximally uncertain. That joint-pure, marginal-mixed signature is the laboratory test for entanglement and the foundation for the quantum communication protocols that come next.
