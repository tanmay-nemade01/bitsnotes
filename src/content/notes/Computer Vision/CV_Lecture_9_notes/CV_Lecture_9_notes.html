Image Classification: From Nearest Neighbours to Linear Classifiers

Published: 2026-10-03
Level: undergraduate
Audience: Undergraduate students in Computer Vision

9.1 What Image Classification Asks For

9.1.1 Core Idea and Task Definition

An image classifier answers one question: what does this picture show? Later stages answer where it sits and how it moves.

An image classifier (a function that takes an image and returns a label for what it shows) sits at the start of most vision work. We give it a picture. It answers what the picture shows. A later stage answers where the object sits in the picture, with detection or segmentation. A third stage answers how it moves over time, with tracking. This session stays on the first question: what is this image.

Take a photo of a cat. The set of discrete labels (the fixed list of allowed answers) might hold cat, dog, plane, truck, and a few more. The classifier must pick one label for the image. In binary classification (a yes-or-no choice between two labels), the question is cat or not cat. In multi-class classification (a choice among many labels), the question is cat, dog, truck, or plane. In multi-label classification (a two-stage choice with a coarse label and then a fine label), the question has two steps. First we say dog. Then we say which breed: Labrador, Dalmatian, Husky, and so on. First we say cat. Then we say house cat, tabby cat, and so on.

A classifier maps a grid of numbers to a label plus a score per class. A typical output reads 82 percent cat, 15 percent dog, 2 percent hat, 1 percent mug. The winning label is the one with the top score.

The human view and the machine view differ. We see a cat curled on its back. The machine sees a sampled and quantized image (a grid of rows and columns where each cell stores a discrete intensity value). The classifier must map that grid of numbers to a label, plus a score for each class. That vector of scores is a softmax output (a list of probabilities that sum to one). We say the image is a cat because cat holds the top score. The full task statement is then: cat, dog, plane, truck labels with softmax scores, pick the top.

A concrete cloud case makes this real. Upload a photo of a flute to a managed cloud vision service. The service returns woodwind instrument or wind instrument with a photo score such as 0.91. Under the service sits an image classification model trained on thousands of tagged photos. The flute case shows the same pattern as the cat case: pixels in, ranked labels out.

9.1.2 Why Recognition Is Hard

Your eyes solve vision with shape, context, and memory at once. A pixel-only model has none of that help, so small changes in pose or light can flip its answer.

Recognition looks easy to us and hard to machines. The same cat can sleep stretched out or curled up. That is deformation (change in pose and shape). Light can shift from bright to dim or from warm to cold. That is illumination variation (change in light level and colour). Parts of the subject can hide behind furniture or leaves. That is occlusion (part of the object is hidden). Scenes can hold many items at once. That is clutter (extra objects that distract the model).

Members of one class also differ. Not all dogs share one shape. Breeds vary in size, fur, and face. That is intra-class variation (real differences inside one label). Viewpoint shifts add more change. A cat shot from far behind in low light looks small and dark. Scale shifts add more change. A small frog and a large frog share a label but fill a different number of pixels. Work on scale invariance with SIFT had faced this same point head on: one object can span 30 pixels or 300 pixels and still be the same thing.

Colour and shape can mislead. Green grass and a green grasshopper share colour but belong apart. A green tank against green plants shares colour with the background but stays distinct. A cliff edge seen in water can read as the back of an animal, yet shape and context tell them apart. Real size can mislead too. A person posing so a finger seems to pinch the tip of a large monument is not the same height as the monument. Perspective shrinks far things.

Picture meeting old friends in a crowd. You use face, walk, voice, and context at once. A model that uses raw pixel values alone lacks that mix. So we need many training views and strong features. One front photo of each class will not hold up. Dozens of poses, lights, and backgrounds per class give the model a fair shot.

Scope: the what-question assumes one main label per image. When a photo holds a dog and a cat and a ball, single-label scoring breaks down. That case needs detection or multi-tag output instead. Assumption: the label list is fixed and known at train time. Open-ended naming of new objects needs a different setup.

Draw the mental chart here. Put light level on the x-axis from dark to bright. Put model score for the true label on the y-axis from 0 to 1. The curve starts high in good light, sags at both dark and glare ends, and drops in steps where occlusion removes key parts. The takeaway in one line: scores fall as views drift from the trained mix.

Two traps catch beginners. First, trusting colour alone: green-on-green pairs show that colour match does not mean class match. Second, trusting size in pixels: a near toy can fill more pixels than a far truck, so pixel size does not fix real size.

9.1.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: What is multi-label classification, and how does dog versus wolf fit in? A: Multi-label classification means two stages. First pick the coarse group. Then pick the fine type inside that group. One image can carry more than one tag. A dog photo gets dog in stage one and Labrador or Husky in stage two. A cat photo gets cat in stage one and tabby cat in stage two. The wolf example is close but misses the point. Wolf versus dog is a choice between two coarse labels. Multi-label coarse plus fine for the same image is not two close coarse guesses. Dog versus wolf tests coarse separation. Dog then Labrador tests coarse plus fine tagging.

Several students asked about staged pipelines, so one merged answer covers the group.

Q: In that two-stage pipeline, must stage one always be binary and stage two always multi-class, or can we reuse multi-class twice? A: Either form works in stage one. Use binary or multi-class based on the task. In visual quality inspection the first question is defect or no defect. That is binary. The second question is what kind of defect: scratch, dent, stain. That is multi-class. In animal sorting the first question may already need multi-class: dog, cat, horse. The second question is again multi-class: which breed of dog. So multi-class can appear in both stages. Binary then multi-class and multi-class then multi-class are both valid. Match the stage to the task, and reuse the task stages as needed.

A last confusion point mixed two edge methods, so it gets its own canonical answer.

Q: The exam asked us to sort responses into strong edge, weak edge, and no edge with a threshold, and called it Harris corner detection. Is that right? A: No. Harris marks each point as flat, edge, or corner. That is the full Harris output. Strong edge, weak edge, and no edge with hysteresis thresholds belong to Canny edge detection. The makeup set mixed the labels. The teaching team will check the wording and scoring with the graders. For the exam, keep the two taxonomies apart: Harris gives flat, edge, corner, while Canny gives strong edge, weak edge, no edge via thresholds. Do not swap the taxonomies.

9.1.4 Loss Flavours by Task

Match the loss to the task shape. Binary picks use sigmoid plus binary cross entropy. Multi-class picks use softmax plus cross entropy. Multi-label chains coarse then fine, with a loss at each stage.

For binary choices we use binary cross entropy (a loss for yes-or-no scores built on the sigmoid curve). For multi-class choices we use cross entropy (a loss for many-way scores built on softmax). For multi-label work we chain them. First run binary or multi-class for the coarse label. Then run multi-class again for the fine label inside it. A dog-then-Labrador chain is multi-class plus multi-class. A defect-then-scratch chain is binary plus multi-class.

Exam note: match the loss to the task shape. Binary task uses sigmoid plus binary cross entropy. Multi-class task uses softmax plus cross entropy. When a question shows coarse-plus-fine labels, name both stages and both losses. Concept-first questions reward this task-to-loss link over rote recall.

That link closes the task shape story. One line holds the whole section.

What classification asks for in one line: map pixels to the best label plus honest scores. The next section turns that goal into a data pipeline that can learn the map.

9.2 Data-Driven Pipeline: Train and Predict

9.2.1 Collecting Labelled Data

No rule book can list every cat pose. The fix is data: show the model many tagged views so it learns the range itself.

No one can hard code cat versus dog with rules. The fix is data. For each label we collect n images (many photos of that class). We vary orientation, light, camera distance, and background. More views per label give a stronger model. Large size helps. Small size hurts. Ten clean studio shots will not cover street photos at night.

Standard sets help teams compare results. MNIST (a set of small handwritten digits) suits digit work. CIFAR-10 (a set of small colour photos in 10 classes) suits early classification work. Fashion-MNIST (a set of small clothing photos) suits a harder drop-in for MNIST. PASCAL (a photo set with detection labels) suits detection scoring. ImageNet (a large photo set with many classes) suits large-scale scoring. COCO (a photo set with objects in context) suits detection and segmentation scoring. Shared sets let groups compare numbers in a fair way. This benchmark culture in vision mirrors speech and language scoring on fixed sets.

Split the pool before training. The training set learns the weights. The validation set tunes the settings. The test set judges new-data skill once at the end. Training, validation, test split with weights learned on train is the spine.

We split the pool before we train. The training set (the images the model learns from) tunes the weights. The validation set (the images used to tune settings during training) tunes choices like k and thresholds. The test set (the held-out images used once at the end) measures how the model handles new data. This three-way split is the spine of the pipeline. Keep the test set locked until the end. Open it early and its scores stop meaning broad skill.

Key texts for depth are Milan Sonka and Zelinski. Condensed handouts cannot hold full detail due to space limits. For the exam, read the texts for the full treatment of each method, not just the short summary lines.

9.2.2 Train Function and Predict Function

The pipeline is two functions. The train function takes images plus labels and returns weights. The predict function takes a new image plus those weights and returns scores.

The classifier becomes two functions. The train function (the step that learns weights from labelled features) takes images plus labels and returns a learned model. The predict function (the step that labels a new image with the learned model) takes a new image plus the learned model and returns scores.

Steps in training run in order. First gather images with labels. Next pull image features (numbers that describe edges, corners, gradients, or SIFT keys) from each image. Then feed features plus labels to a learning rule. The rule returns learned parameters (weights and biases that score new inputs). Steps in testing mirror this shape. First pull the same features from a new image. Next apply the learned weights. Then compare the top score with the true label to gauge error. Use the same feature code in train and test. A mismatch here breaks every score.

Image features named in the session include histogram of gradients, corners, edges, and SIFT. Each image yields a row of numbers. Many images stack into a table like a spreadsheet. Columns hold features. The last column holds the label. That table feeds standard learners such as SVM or random forest. In one course assignment this same flow sorted urban versus rural scenes: features to rows, rows to learner, learner to urban or rural tag.

Worked trace on a tiny pool. Say we have 6 images: 3 urban street shots and 3 rural field shots. Each image gives 4 feature numbers plus a label, so the table is 6 rows by 5 columns. Train an SVM on rows 1 to 4. Check settings on rows 5 to 6 as validation. Lock a fresh pair as test. The train step returns weights. The predict step scores the held-out rows. Final answer: two functions, one table shape, three splits. Sense-check: if train scores 100 percent but validation scores 50 percent, the features or settings do not carry over.

Picture the pipeline as a kitchen line. Prep cooks wash and chop (collect and featurize). The head cook sets the recipe (train weights). Servers plate new orders with that recipe (predict). Change the chop size mid-service and plates drift. Keep prep fixed and results stay steady.

Scope: this pipeline assumes tagged photos drawn from the same mix as future use. Assumption: labels are right and views cover the use case. When labels are noisy or night shots are missing from train, scores on fresh groups fall. Fix the data mix first, not the learner.

That box sets the valid range. Two common breaks follow.

Two traps show up often. First, leaking test photos into train: scores look great and mean nothing. Second, changing features between train and test: shapes stop lining up and the code fails or scores noise.

We add the label at the end of each row. Then we train a standard learner on the table. That row-per-image table is what SVM and random forest code expects.

9.3 Bag of Words From Patches to Histograms

9.3.1 Key Patches and SIFT Blocks

Whole-image averages blur detail. What if we cut the image into small patches, name each patch, and count the names?

Bag of words (a method that turns local patches into a histogram and then classifies the histogram) came to vision from text work. Early vision use is linked to work by Sivic and colleagues around 2006, in print in a Springer article in 2007. The core thought is plain. Whole-image stats blur detail. Local patches keep detail. So we detect key patches (small blocks of interest such as edges, lines, corners, and SIFT regions) and we describe each patch with numbers.

Each SIFT key holds an x location, a y location, a sigma value (the scale or blur level of the key), and a principal orientation (the main gradient direction). The patch also holds gradient and histogram-of-gradients data. We bin these values into a histogram per block. A histogram here means counts per visual word. Cats tend to give one histogram shape. Human faces tend to give another. People photos share more mass with each other than with cat photos. That gap lets a learner separate them.

Think of sorting mail by postcode. Each letter holds a code. Counts of codes per bag tell us where the bag came from. Here each patch gives a visual word. Counts of visual words per image tell us the image class. The mail postcode counts map straight to visual words counts: letter to patch, postcode to visual word, bag mix to image class. The analogy breaks at order: word order matters in a sentence, but bag of words drops patch positions and keeps only counts.

Key patches plus SIFT numbers plus histogram counts form the feature. Location \(x, y\), scale sigma, orientation, and gradient bins per patch feed the count vector. Close count vectors mean same class.

Concretely, one SIFT key might read \(x = 120\), \(y = 80\), sigma \(= 2.4\), orientation \(= 35\) degrees, with 128 gradient-bin numbers attached. Cluster those 128-number vectors across the pool into, say, 500 visual words with k-means. Each patch then maps to its nearest word. Each image becomes a 500-long count row. That row is what the learner sees.

9.3.2 From Histograms to Labels

Once each image becomes a histogram row, the task turns tabular. Say each image gives 15 or 16 feature columns. With 10,000 images we hold a 10,000 by 16 table. Each row is one image histogram. Each column is one visual word count. The last column holds the label. That table feeds a learner such as SVM or random forest.

Urban versus rural assignment chain. Step 1: pull SIFT and edge and corner keys from each scene photo. Step 2: map each key to a visual word. Step 3: build one histogram row per image. Step 4: stack rows into a table, about 10,000 by 16 in the sketch. Step 5: train an SVM and a random forest on the table. Step 6: tag held-out scenes as urban or rural. Final answer: richer features win — SIFT plus histogram of gradients beats plain edges and plain corners because the richer descriptor captures scale sigma and direction. Sense-check: if masks look blotchy, the fault is often weak features, not the learner name.

The distance thought also fits here. The gap between two human faces in feature space is small. The gap between a human face and a cat face is large. So a distance-based rule (a rule that labels a new point by its closest stored points) can work. In numbers: face-to-face gap 0.3, face-to-cat gap 0.9, so the new face joins the face group. Retrieval with language models uses the same thought today. Close chunks in embedding space count as related, just as close histograms count as same class.

Draw the chart: put visual word index on the x-axis from 1 to 500. Put count on the y-axis. Urban scenes spike at vertical-edge words. Rural scenes spike at texture words. Two peaks at different spots mean easy separation. Flat overlapping humps mean hard separation.

Scope: bag of words drops geometry. Assumption: counts alone carry the class signal. When layout matters — a wheel above a road versus below sky — counts tie and the model stalls. That gap motivates later spatial models.

Inside that valid range, two tuning traps still bite.

Two traps: too few visual words blur cat and face humps together, while far too many words split one hump into noise. Tune the word count on validation data. Also, do not mix histogram scales between train and test.

9.3.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Are we grouping the descriptors to decide who they belong to? A: Yes. Each key point gives a feature set: x, y, sigma, orientation, plus gradient bins. One image gives many such sets. We bin them into a histogram. We flatten that histogram into one row. Rows from one class sit close together in feature space. Rows from other classes sit far apart. The learner then marks which group a new row belongs to. Grouping descriptors by sigma and orientation is what turns scattered patches into one comparable row.

9.4 Nearest Neighbour Classifier

9.4.1 Mathematical Formulation

No weights to learn here. To tag a new photo, find the stored photo that looks most like it and copy that tag.

A nearest neighbour classifier (a rule that copies the label of the closest stored example) needs no fitted weights. It stores the training set. At test time it scores gaps and takes a majority vote among the k closest items. The k-nearest neighbours rule (the same vote with k neighbours instead of one) smooths the choice by polling more stored points. With \(k = 1\) we copy the single best match. With \(k = 5\) we poll five stored rows and take the top label.

Purpose: tag a new image with zero training cost by reuse of stored rows. Inputs: test vector plus all train vectors plus their labels. Outputs: predicted label by smallest gap or majority vote.

Inputs and outputs in one place. Input test vector \(x_{test}\) in \(R^{d}\) with \(d\) pixel entries. Input train pool with one vector \(x_{train}\) in \(R^{d}\) per stored image plus its label. Output: predicted label. Parameter \(k\) sets how many stored rows vote. Distance choice sets how gaps are scored: Manhattan L1 or Euclidean L2.

Two gap scores were named. The verbal form for the first is the absolute gap summed over pixels between test and train. The verbal form for the second is the square root of the summed squared gaps. Let \(x_{test}\) in \(R^{d}\) be the test vector with \(d\) entries, and let \(x_{train}\) in \(R^{d}\) be one training vector with the same length. Let \(x_{test,i}\) be entry \(i\) of the test vector and \(x_{train,i}\) be entry \(i\) of the train vector.

The L1 or Manhattan gap, described as the sum of absolute pixel gaps between test and train vectors, is:

\[d_{1}(x_{test}, x_{train}) = \sum_{i=1}^{d} |x_{test,i} - x_{train,i}|\]

The L2 or Euclidean gap, described as the square root of the summed squared gaps, is:

\[d_{2}(x_{test}, x_{train}) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d} (x_{test,i} - x_{train,i})^{2}}\]

Here \(d\) is the count of entries, \(i\) is the entry index from 1 to \(d\), and \(d_{1}\) and \(d_{2}\) are scalar gap values in the same units as pixel intensity. Small gap means close match. Large gap means weak match. The predicted label is the label of the stored item with the smallest gap when \(k = 1\), or the majority label among the \(k\) smallest gaps when \(k > 1\).

CIFAR-10 grounds the sizes. The facts are 50,000 training images, 10,000 test images, 10 classes such as aeroplane, automobile, bird, cat, and deer, and image size 32 by 32 with 3 colour channels. The CIFAR vector length 3072 follows from direct product:

\[d = 32 \times 32 \times 3 = 3072\]

So each image becomes \(x\) in \(R^{3072}\), a row vector with 3,072 entries. The full training pool holds 50,000 such rows plus labels, with 60,000 images in total when train and test are summed. Shapes check: test row 1 by 3072 against train row 1 by 3072 gives one scalar gap per pair.

Steps for one query: score the test vector against every stored train vector with L1 or L2, sort the gaps small to large, take the first \(k\) rows, count labels, return the top label. That stored-rows gaps vote flow is the whole method.

Picture the gap chart. Put stored images on the x-axis grouped by class. Put gap on the y-axis. The frog group sits low near 0.1 to 0.8. Plane and cat groups sit higher near 0.3 to 0.45 as match scores or larger as gaps. The lowest bar names the label. One-line takeaway: the shortest bar wins.

Scope: this rule assumes dense coverage of each class and a gap that tracks shape. Assumption: same size, same scale, same light across train and test. When views shift or light swings, raw pixel gaps stop tracking class and votes drift.

9.4.2 Worked Examples

Frog match vote that returns frog label with top score. A new frog photo is scored against stored groups for frog, plane, and cat. The stated gaps-turned-scores read 0.1 against one frog view, 0.6 against a smaller-scale frog, 0.7 against a frog with shifted colours, 0.8 against the best frog match, and lower values such as 0.3 to 0.45 against plane and cat views. Sort high to low as match scores: 0.8 frog on top, then 0.7, 0.6, then 0.45 cat, 0.3 plane. Final answer: frog, since 0.8 is the top score. Sense-check: exact pixel match was not needed — near shape plus near texture still won the vote.

The frog case shows one query. A second pool with eight images shows how the stored map votes across labels.

Eight image dog versus cat pool where stored vote gets cat. Images 1 to 8 carry labels: image 1 dog, image 2 cat, image 3 dog, image 4 cat, image 5 cat, image 6 cat, image 7 cat, image 8 dog. Training stores a map from label to list. Dog maps to images 1, 3, 8 with their feature rows. Cat maps to images 2, 4, 5, 6, 7 with their feature rows. A new image arrives. Score its gap to every stored row, giving values such as 0.1, 0.2, 0.8, 0.9 across the pool as match scores. The max vote lands on a cat row at 0.9. Final answer: cat. Sense-check: five cat rows versus three dog rows also tilt priors toward cat, so keep \(k\) odd and small to cut ties.

A tiny L1 trace cements the math. Test pixels \([10, 20]\), train A \([12, 18]\), train B \([40, 60]\). L1 to A is \(|10-12| + |20-18| = 4\). L1 to B is \(|10-40| + |20-60| = 70\). Copy A's label. L2 to A is the square root of 8, about 2.83. L2 to B is the square root of 2500, which is 50. Same winner. Final answer: train A wins under both gaps.

A code demo sharpens the picture. It uses TorchVision CIFAR-10 with two image steps: tensor conversion and normalization. It holds the test split as is. It splits the training pool into 40,000 for training and 10,000 for checks, about a 90 to 10 split of the training pool. Data loaders feed the batches. The train step loops over stored rows, scores train-to-test gaps, collects predicted labels, and moves on. With raw pixel gaps the accuracy sits near 20 percent. That means about 8 in 10 test images miss. Richer features such as SIFT or histogram of gradients lift the number, but the time cost stays high. Complexity and cost in brief: train cost near zero, test cost grows with rows, memory holds all rows.

When to use this rule and what to use instead: pick nearest neighbours for a fast baseline on small pools with fixed views. Pick a parametric map when test speed matters or views shift. Raw pixels plus voting cannot carry a live product alone.

9.4.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Training looks like just storing images and labels in a map. Is that all? A: Yes for this method. Dog maps to its image numbers and feature rows. Cat maps to its image numbers and feature rows. No weight fitting happens. All the work moves to test time, when each new image is scored against every stored row and the top vote wins. That lazy style is why train is fast and test is slow.

Exam note: when asked to run one nearest neighbour query by hand, list each stored L1 or L2 gap value, mark the smallest gap or largest match score, and copy that label. Show the full list so the grader can track the vote. Nearest neighbour hand query steps are list gaps, mark best, copy label.

9.4.4 Industry Applications

Real-world use: early shop-floor safety and entry monitoring used this simple flow. Even a 20 percent cut in missed entries or mishaps was a large win on the floor. The same distance thought now drives embedding search in language systems, where close vectors count as related passages. The pattern is one: store rows, score gaps, copy the best tag.

9.5 Train, Validation, Test and Cross-Validation

9.5.1 Why Test Alone Falls Short

Would you set exam marks with the final paper itself? Tuning on the test set is that same leak — great marks now, poor skill later.

Settings like k need tuning. The hyperparameter (a setting we pick before training, such as k) shapes results. If we tune k on the test set, we bend the model to that one set. Later data will then surprise it. That bend is bias toward the test set (fitting choices to the final exam instead of learning broad skill).

Think of times tables. A teacher drills that two times three is six. A learner says eight. A good teacher corrects the error during practice drills while the learner still trains. That live fix is the role of validation data. Without it the learner meets the error first in the final exam. With it the learner fixes the error while training still runs and practice can still help. So we split data three ways: train to fit, validation to tune, test to judge once.

Keep the roles fixed. Train fits the model weights. Validation tunes the settings such as k and thresholds. Test judges broad use: will the same model work in new places and on new groups. Train fits weights, validation tunes hyperparameters, test judges broad use.

Tuning on test adds bias because each tweak picks the setting that flatters that one sample. The reported score then mixes model skill with luck on that slice. Fresh groups break the spell. Validation gives a practice ground to tune k and thresholds before the final check, so the test keeps its role as a fair judge.

9.5.2 Folds and Population Spread

Cross-validation repeats scoring on rotating subsets of data. Each fold is one rotating slice used as a mini test set. Mean accuracy across folds shows steadiness across groups.

Cross-validation (repeated scoring on rotating subsets of data) checks spread across groups. Human faces vary by region and background. North, south, east, and west groups in one country already differ in traits. A single fixed test slice may miss whole groups. Folds fix this by shuffling and re-slicing the pool into overlapping train-plus-mini-test rounds.

A fold (one rotating slice used as a mini test set) mirrors a sample of the full pool. Say fold one holds five positives and three negatives. Fold two holds three positives and five negatives. Fold three holds three of each. Each fold mix shows a different face of the data. Averaging scores across folds shows how steady the model is. Training on 98 positives and 2 negatives in one block would hide the rare group. Spread folds force the model to meet each group.

Fold mix with five positives and three negatives. Fold 1: 5 pos, 3 neg, accuracy 0.75. Fold 2: 3 pos, 5 neg, accuracy 0.62. Fold 3: 3 pos, 3 neg plus 2 extra mixed, accuracy 0.70. Mean accuracy is the sum over 3, which is 0.69. Final answer: mean 0.69, spread 0.62 to 0.75. Sense-check: a single lucky fold at 0.75 would overstate skill, while the mean scores steadiness.

The count of folds is also tuned. We try one fold, two folds, three folds, five folds, six folds, and more. We track mean accuracy or mean precision and recall across folds. The curve rises, peaks, then drops. The peak marks the sweet spot where validation tunes folds and mean accuracy peaks. In one sketch the peak sat near seven folds. Other data sets peak at three or four. There is no fixed best number. Measure it on validation data, not test data. Past the peak the score drops again as slices get too thin or too alike.

Picture the fold curve. Put fold count on the x-axis from 1 to 10. Put mean accuracy on the y-axis. The line climbs from 1 to about 7, crests near seven, then sags. One-line takeaway: tune the count to the crest, not to habit.

Scope: folds probe spread inside the collected pool. Assumption: shuffles still reflect future groups. When new regions or cameras arrive that were never sampled, even good fold means miss. Collect wider data for true broad use.

Two traps: tuning fold count on test data leaks the same bias back in, and averaging without looking at spread hides one failing group. Report both mean and worst fold.

9.5.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: What goes wrong if we use only train and test with no validation? A: We tune settings to fit the test set. That adds bias to that one sample. The model then fails on fresh groups. Validation gives a practice ground to tune k and thresholds before the final check, so the test keeps its role as a fair judge of broad use. Train then test alone confuses tuning with judging.

That covers the no-validation failure. A follow-up asks why folds still add value on top.

Q: Why do we use cross-validation if tuning already used validation? A: To probe spread. Folds are random sub-samples of the pool. Scoring on each fold mix of positives and negatives shows how the model treats each mix. Mean accuracy across folds reflects steadiness across groups, which a single split can hide. One split can be lucky. Five folds rarely are all lucky at once.

Spread checked, the last practical question is how to set the fold count itself.

Q: How do we pick the number of folds? A: Sweep the count and track the mean score. Start low, move up, and plot cross-validation accuracy. Keep the count at the peak. Past the peak the score drops again. The peak in the sketch was seven, but each data set has its own peak. Tune the count like any other setting, on validation only.

9.6 Cost and Failure Modes of Nearest Neighbours

9.6.1 Time and Memory Behaviour

Training here is just memorizing. The bill arrives at test time, when each new photo must be matched against the whole store.

Training cost is tiny. We just store images, feature rows, and labels. In order terms that is \(O(1)\) per stored item to memorize. Test cost is large. One new image must be scored against every stored row. With 60,000 stored rows that is 60,000 gap computations plus a vote. With 70,000 rows that is 70,000 gap computations. Test time grows with data size. Double the rows and you double each answer time. Test cost grows as rows grow.

That flip hurts deployment. We want short training to be fine, but we need fast response at use time. Here training finishes fast while each single decision drags. A model that trains in one hour but needs seven hours to answer one batch will not ship. Live cameras, shop alarms, and phone apps need answers in milliseconds, so slow per-image voting fails the budget.

Cost summary: train near zero, test linear in rows, memory linear in rows times row width. With \(N\) rows of width \(d\), test work per query scales as \(O(N d)\) and store scales as \(O(N d)\).

Memory also grows. The full pool of rows must stay on hand. Larger images and richer descriptors swell each row. Small 32 by 32 images are cheap. Full-size photos are not. A 3072-number row at 4 bytes sits near 12 KB. Times 50,000 rows that is about 600 MB before labels and overhead. Richer SIFT rows push it higher.

9.6.2 Shift and Intensity Brittleness

Pixel gaps see numbers, not objects. A shift of a few cells rewires which pixel meets which, so the summed gap jumps while a person still sees the same face.

Pixel-to-pixel gaps break under small shifts. The model sees numbers, not objects. Move the subject by a few pixels and each cell compares to a new neighbour. The summed gap jumps even though we still see the same face.

Take a face photo. Paint one cheek black. Call the base image \(f(x, y)\) where \(x\) is the horizontal spot and \(y\) is the vertical spot. Flip it left to right as \(f(-x, y)\) so the black patch moves to the other cheek. The flip face image with horizontal and vertical spots shows how brittle this is. The two grids now mismatch at many cells. A threshold rule such as same person if total gap is under 200 else different person can fail. Say the summed gap reaches 456 due to the shift and paint. The rule says different person though the person never changed. That false reject of faces shows the core weak point. The method keys on raw values, not on stable shape. Total 456 versus limit 200 is the number to quote.

The same holds for dark versus bright copies of one photo, or a slightly cropped copy. Histogram equalization can ease light gaps, but shifting and re-scoring each query by hand is slow and ad hoc. Without stable features the accuracy stays near 20 percent on raw pixels. SIFT and histogram of gradients help because they encode local shape and direction, yet the per-query voting cost stays.

Think of matching two copies of a form where one copy slid in the scanner. Cell-by-cell math flags many false gaps. A human still reads the same words. The scanner slid form with cell math false gaps is the same story: machine counts cell gaps, human reads shape. The machine needs shift-tolerant features to do the same.

Black cheek flip threshold walk-through. Base face \(f(x,y)\) with pale cheeks. Paint left cheek black, 400 dark cells. Flip horizontal to \(f(-x,y)\): black patch swaps cheeks. Score absolute gaps cell by cell: 400 cheek cells mismatch by about 1.0 each plus edge moves add 56 more, total 456. Threshold 200 says different person. Final answer: false reject at 456 over 200, though identity never changed. Sense-check: a person sees one face with moved paint, not two people.

Scope: raw-pixel voting assumes fixed framing and fixed light. Assumption: same grid, same gain, same crop. When framing slides or gain shifts, gaps lose meaning. Stable keys or learned weights are needed outside that box.

Inside that narrow box, two practical traps still bite.

Two traps: trusting a single threshold across lights, and blaming k when features are the fault. If day shots pass and night shots fail at the same threshold, fix light handling first. If tiny shifts flip votes, fix features first.

9.6.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why does a small pixel shift hurt nearest neighbours so much? A: The method sums cell-by-cell gaps. A shift moves each edge to a new cell. Background cells turn into subject cells and back. Each such swap of background and subject adds error. Edge moves shift the cell pattern, background swap adds gaps, and the total can cross the match threshold even for two copies of the same photo, so the vote picks the wrong label. Stable shape features reduce the harm, but raw pixels stay brittle.

Exam note: when asked for pitfalls of nearest neighbours, give both heads: large response time at test plus weak handling of shift, light, scale, and clutter. Quote the 20 percent raw-pixel accuracy and the threshold case with total 456 versus limit 200 to show the false reject.

9.7 Linear Classifiers: A Parametric Step

9.7.1 Mathematical Formulation

Storing every photo is dear at test time. What if we learn ten templates instead and score new photos by dot product?

A linear classifier (a parametric rule of the form score equals weights times inputs plus bias) trades memory for learning. Instead of storing every image, it learns one weight matrix and one bias vector that map any input to class scores. Test time then costs one matrix hit, not 50,000 gap votes.

Start with size. The facts are image width 32, image height 32, colour channels 3, classes 10. Flatten the cube into one row. The length is:

\[d = 32 \times 32 \times 3 = 3072\]

So one image is \(x\) in \(R^{3072}\), a row with 3,072 entries. In column form for math we use \(x\) in \(R^{3072 \\times 1}\), a column with 3,072 rows and 1 column. The product checks as 32 times 32 is 1024, times 3 is 3072. The session audio slips once to 370 before the speaker corrects to 3072, and the arithmetic plus CIFAR-10 shapes confirm 3072 as the value used throughout.

Flatten image to 3072, multiply by weight matrix, add bias, read scores. Let \(W\) in \(R^{10 \\times 3072}\) hold class templates and \(b\) in \(R^{10 \\times 1}\) hold offsets. Then scores follow \(s = W x + b\).

Let \(W\) in \(R^{10 \\times 3072}\) be the weight matrix with 10 rows for classes and 3,072 columns for pixels, where each entry \(W_{j,i}\) scales pixel \(i\) for class \(j\). Let \(b\) in \(R^{10 \\times 1}\) be the bias vector with one offset per class. Let \(s\) in \(R^{10 \\times 1}\) be the score vector with one score per class. The linear map, described in words as weights times flattened image plus bias, is:

\[s = W x + b\]

Matrix shapes line up as 10 by 3,072 times 3,072 by 1, which gives 10 by 1, plus a 10 by 1 bias. Each class gets its own template row in \(W\). Scoring is template matching: dot the image with each class template row, add the bias, and read the 10 scores such as 0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 0.01, and so on. The dot of image with class template rows is the operation to remember. Texts often write this as logits \(l = W x + b\); here we use scores \(s\) to match the lecture.

A tiny case makes the shapes concrete. Say an image has only 4 pixels and there are 3 classes: cat, dog, ship. Then \(x\) in \(R^{4 \\times 1}\), \(W\) in \(R^{3 \\times 4}\), \(b\) in \(R^{3 \\times 1}\), and the score \(s\) in \(R^{3 \\times 1}\) follows the same rule:

\[s = W x + b\]

Here 3 by 4 times 4 by 1 gives 3 by 1, plus 3 by 1. One score serves each of cat, dog, ship. Shape check passes: rows of first match columns of second, output rows equal class count.

Views of the same rule help. The algebraic view reads scores from \(W x + b\). The visual view reads one template per class stored in rows of \(W\): each row looks like a blurry class average. The geometric view reads a hyperplane (a flat boundary that splits positives from negatives) placed by \(W\) and \(b\). Linear borders handle straight splits but struggle with rings, XOR shapes, and multi-angle borders. An SVM with a kernel can bend those splits on small pools near 10,000 to 50,000 rows, but tuning grows heavy. Stacking \(W x + b\) with a nonlinear step at each layer later gives curved and folded borders, which is the path toward neural nets.

On signs: the sketch reports the winning raw value near minus 96.8 under its own sign setup before calibration. Raw scores before softmax or sigmoid can be negative, zero, or positive — they are not probabilities yet. A minus sign does not mean error. Read the sign setup in context as uncalibrated scores, then let the later squash map them to chances. Domain check holds: \(s\) in real space, probabilities only after the squash.

9.7.2 Worked Examples

Four pixel toy with cat, dog, ship where gap nudges weights. Input \(x\) holds 4 pixel values, say \([1, 0, 2, 1]\). \(W\) holds 3 rows of 4 weights. Add \(b\) with 3 entries. The result holds 3 scores. Suppose the target for a cat image in one-hot form is \([1, 0, 0]\) for cat, dog, ship. Suppose the raw scores read \([0.4, -1.0, 0.2]\). The gap for the true class is \(|1 - 0.4| = 0.6\). That 0.6 is the loss for this image under a simple absolute-gap reading. The learner then nudges \(W\) and \(b\) to shrink that gap across the pool. Final answer: loss 0.6 on the cat slot, weights step to close it. Sense-check: 0.4 is short of 1 by 0.6, minus 1.0 sits far from 0, so the push is strongest there.

Flattening comes first at full scale. Resize each photo to 32 by 32 with 3 channels. Lay the cube out as one line of 3,072 numbers. Attach one weight per number per class. That gives the 10 by 3,072 matrix plus 10 biases. Feed a cat photo. The cat template row should score high while other rows score low. The gap to the true one-hot target drives learning. Output multiply of matrix with vector plus bias keeps class count: 10 by 3072 times 3072 by 1 plus 10 by 1 gives 10 by 1, one score per class.

Real-world link: this flatten plus matrix plus bias pattern is the same final layer used in deep nets before softmax or sigmoid. Learning it here makes later nets familiar. LeNet-style nets reuse this head after learned features.

Scope: one flat cut per class. Assumption: classes split near-linearly in pixel or feature space. When positives ring negatives or corners alternate like XOR, one template per class cannot trace those shapes. That limit motivates activations and deeper nets next.

Two traps: reading raw scores as probabilities — minus 96.8 is fine before squash — and transposing \(W\). If shapes refuse to line up, check rows equal classes and columns equal 3072 before touching the code.

9.8 Losses, Margins, and Training the Linear Map

9.8.1 Mathematical Formulation

Scores alone do not train. We need one number that says how wrong we are, then push weights to shrink it.

Ground truth (the true label in numeric form) uses one-hot vectors. A cat among cat, dog, ship is \([1, 0, 0]\). Only the true slot is 1. The rest are 0. The goal is to push scores close to that target.

Purpose: turn score gaps into one trainable number. Inputs: scores plus one-hot truth. Outputs: mean loss over the batch that SGD steps down.

The loss function (a number that says how far predictions sit from truth) is averaged over the pool. In words: mean gap between predicted scores and true labels across all images, with mean loss as average batch cross entropy whose back gradient steps weights. Write per-image loss as \(L_{i}\) for image \(i\) and mean loss over \(N\) images as:

\[L = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} L_{i}\]

Here \(N\) is the image count, \(i\) is the image index from 1 to \(N\), \(L_{i}\) in real space is the loss on image \(i\), and \(L\) in real space is the mean loss. Training seeks small \(L\). Sum check: bounds 1 to \(N\) stated, no bare sum.

For binary picks the squash is the sigmoid function (an S-curve that maps any score to the range 0 to 1). The verbal form is one over one plus e to the power minus z. The sigmoid curve maps score range for binary cross entropy use. With \(z\) in real space as the raw score and \(\\sigma(z)\) in interval 0 to 1 as the squashed value:

\[\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-z)}\]

Values above the midpoint read as positive, below as negative, with 0.5 at the middle. Spot-check: \(z = 0\) gives 0.5, large positive \(z\) nears 1, large negative \(z\) nears 0. Paired with binary cross entropy, this loss judges cat or not cat. In words for one image with true \(y\) in set 0,1 and predicted \(p\) in interval 0,1, the loss is minus true times log predicted minus one-minus-true times log one-minus-predicted:

\[L_{bce} = -(y \log p + (1 - y) \log (1 - p))\]

Work it: truth 1, predicted 0.9 gives minus log 0.9, about 0.105. Truth 1, predicted 0.1 gives minus log 0.1, about 2.30. Confident wrongs cost far more than near misses.

For many-way picks the squash is the softmax function (a normalizer that turns raw scores into probabilities that sum to one). The verbal form is exponent of this class over sum of exponents of all classes. The softmax exponent of class over sum of exponents yields probabilities. With raw scores \(s_{j}\) in real space for class \(j\) and output \(p_{j}\) in interval 0,1:

\[p_{j} = \frac{\exp(s_{j})}{\sum_{k} \exp(s_{k})}\]

Here \(j\) marks the target class, \(k\) runs over all classes, \(\\exp\) is the natural exponent, and the \(p_{j}\) values sum to one across \(j\). Shift check: adding one constant to all \(s\) leaves \(p\) fixed. The 10 by 1 vector such as 0.7, 0.1, and so on is this \(p\) vector, and the top entry names the label. Paired with cross entropy, the per-image loss in words is minus log of the predicted chance for the true class:

\[L_{ce} = -\log p_{true}\]

where \(p_{true}\) in interval 0,1 is the softmax chance placed on the correct label. Example: true cat with \(p = 0.7\) gives loss about 0.36. With \(p = 0.1\) loss is about 2.30. Same moral as binary: doubt costs a little, confident error costs a lot.

A max-margin loss (a loss that rewards wide gaps between classes) reads the same splits in a different way. Two lines can both give zero errors yet differ in margin (the clear space between the line and the nearest points). The line with larger clear space on both sides generalizes better. Margin as clear space where the line sits far from points and safer points win is the rule. That is why SVM-style training prefers the wide-margin line even when error counts tie. Decision lines are picked by the loss, not by error count alone.

9.8.2 Worked Examples

Loss picks the line where margin breaks ties. Sketch one shows a line that leaves one positive on the wrong side and one negative on the wrong side, giving loss 2. Sketch two shifts the line so all points sit on the right side, giving loss 0. Sketch three tilts another way and also gives loss 0. Zero ties with zero. The margin as clear space breaks the tie. The line with larger support gap wins because far points are safer than near-grazing points. Final answer: pick the zero-loss line with the widest gap. Sense-check: grazing lines flip under tiny noise, wide lines hold.

Training runs the same loop as all gradient methods. Turn each image into a vector. Set \(W\) and \(b\). Push the vector through to get scores. Squash with softmax for 10 classes. Score the gap with cross entropy against the one-hot label. Average over the batch to form mean loss. Send the gradient back. Step the SGD optimizer to update \(W\) and \(b\). Repeat. The five epoch demo with linear percent double accuracy runs 5 epochs with stochastic gradient descent. Loss falls steadily across epochs. More epochs past a point add little.

Five epoch demo from stored votes to learned templates. Nearest neighbours on raw pixels sit near 20 percent. The linear map reaches near 40 percent on CIFAR-10 in the demo, about double the 20 percent raw-pixel nearest neighbour score. Adding L2 regularization lifts it toward 45 to 48 percent in the notes sketch. Later LeNet-style nets lift it toward 55 to 58 percent. Cross-fold checks show nearest neighbours swinging near 0.14 to 0.25 across folds while the linear map holds near 0.40. Final answer: 20 to 40 is double, steadier across folds, before nonlinear layers take over. Sense-check: double plus tighter fold spread marks real learning, not one lucky split.

The nonlinear limit stays in view. Rings with positive inside and negative outside, or XOR grids with alternating corners, need more than one straight cut. One template per class plus one flat cut cannot trace those shapes. That limit motivates activations and deeper nets in the next session, plus later moves to segmentation, detection, tracking, and vision transformers for context.

Scope: softmax plus cross entropy assumes one true tag per image and scores that sum to one. Assumption: labels are one-hot and batches reflect the use mix. For multi-tag photos, swap to per-tag sigmoid plus binary loss instead of forcing one winner.

Outside that box the loss choice misfires. Two practical traps follow.

Two traps: reading 0.7 as 70 percent sure in a calibrated sense — raw softmax can be bold while wrong — and training past the loss floor until validation sags. Watch validation, not just train loss.
