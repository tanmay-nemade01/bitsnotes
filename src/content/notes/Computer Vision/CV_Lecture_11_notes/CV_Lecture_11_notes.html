11.1 Classification, Detection and Pixel-Level Segmentation

11.1.1 What Versus Where in Vision Pipelines

Every photo asks two questions in order. First, what is present. Second, where is it. Classification answers what. Detection and segmentation answer where. If you mix up the two, you pick the wrong tool for the task.

Every vision pipeline starts with a what question. What is present in the image. A cat. A dog. A tumor. A car. A dangerous zone. Image classification answers this what question for the whole image. It takes a nominal image and predicts one overall class. The next question is where. If there is a cat, where is the cat in the image. If there is a dangerous zone, where is that zone. Segmentation and detection answer this where question. They localize. They support exact analysis of the image. This is the core idea of segmentation as pixel classification: each spot in the image earns its own label.

Think of a shelf photo in a store. Classification says this photo shows cleaning goods. That is useful for sorting photos. It does not tell a robot arm which bottle to grab. For grabbing, the arm needs spot-level spots. Detection gives a rough box around each bottle. Segmentation gives the exact outline of each bottle. The what-to-where step grows more exact at each stage.

Segmentation is pixel-level classification. For every pixel point, the model predicts which object that pixel belongs to. This pixel belongs to a dog. This pixel belongs to a cat. This pixel belongs to sky. By labeling each pixel, the method tells where a specific object sits. This helps localize and analyze the image better than a single image-level label.

A picture helps. Imagine a grid laid over the photo. Classification paints the whole grid with one word. Detection draws boxes on the grid. Segmentation colors each tiny cell of the grid. The horizontal axis of that mental chart is position across the image, and the vertical axis is the label choice. The takeaway is simple: finer cells mean finer control.

11.1.2 Detection Gives a Region While Segmentation Gives Exact Pixels

Object detection predicts a general area where an object sits and labels that area. A box around a dog says this area holds a dog. A box around a cat says this area holds a cat. Each box has a class. Segmentation goes further. It says this exact pixel belongs to a dog and that exact pixel belongs to a cat. So detection gives region-level position. Segmentation gives pixel-level position.

11.1.3 Per-Pixel Softmax Formulation

A softmax — a score-to-share converter — turns raw class scores at one pixel into shares that sum to one. For pixel position \((x, y)\), let \(z_c(x, y)\) be the score for class \(c\). Then the per-pixel softmax share equation across classes is:

\[p(c \mid x, y) = \frac{\exp(z_c(x, y))}{\sum_{k} \exp(z_k(x, y))}\]

Here \((x, y)\) is pixel position with \(x\) as column index and \(y\) as row index, \(z_c(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}\) is the score for class \(c\) at that pixel, \(k\) runs over all classes such as cat, dog, and sky, and \(p(c \mid x, y)\) is the predicted share for class \(c\) at that pixel. In compact inline form this is \(p(c \mid \text{pixel})\), where \(c\) is a class label such as cat, dog, or sky, and \(\text{pixel}\) is one image position. The output lies in \([0, 1]\) and sums to 1 across classes at each pixel.

In words: the session describes per-pixel prediction as a softmax over classes, such as 0.0 for cat and 1.0 for sky at one pixel. The reconstructed form is inline \(p(c \mid \text{pixel})\) where \(c\) is a class label such as cat, dog, or sky, and \(\text{pixel}\) is one image position. A fuller block form is \[p(c \mid x, y) = \frac{\exp(z_c(x, y))}{\sum_{k} \exp(z_k(x, y))}\] where \((x, y)\) is pixel position with \(x\) as column index and \(y\) as row index, \(z_c(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}\) is the score for class \(c\) at that pixel, and \(k\) runs over all classes. The output lies in \([0, 1]\) and sums to 1 across classes at each pixel.

A softmax — a score-to-share converter — turns raw scores into shares that sum to one. We compute a score per class at each pixel. We then share out the total so each pixel gets a class mix. The top share decides the pixel label.

Take numbers. At one pixel the scores are \(z_{\text{cat}} = 2.0\), \(z_{\text{dog}} = 1.0\), and \(z_{\text{sky}} = 0.0\). Exponentiate: \(\exp(2.0) \approx 7.39\), \(\exp(1.0) \approx 2.72\), and \(\exp(0.0) = 1.0\). The sum is about \(11.11\). Shares are \(7.39/11.11 \approx 0.67\) for cat, \(2.72/11.11 \approx 0.24\) for dog, and \(1.0/11.11 \approx 0.09\) for sky. The top share is cat, so the pixel earns the cat label. The shares sum to 1. A shift check helps: adding the same constant to all three scores leaves all shares fixed, since both top and bottom scale by the same factor. This is why softmax cares about gaps, not raw levels.

Scope: The per-pixel softmax assumes pixels can be scored on their own from local plus context cues, and that classes at one pixel compete to sum to one. Assumption: each pixel has exactly one true class. When a rim pixel truly straddles two objects, the single-label rule forces a hard pick. That forced pick is where rim errors start, and later soft-share methods ease it.

Picture the scores as three bars at one pixel. The cat bar is tallest. Softmax turns bar heights into pie shares. The tallest bar gets the largest slice, but all slices together fill the pie. The takeaway: softmax does not create new proof; it turns scores into fair shares.

Two traps bite here. First, reading a 0.67 share as 67 percent sure in a real-world sense. It is only the model's share under its own scores, not a calibrated fact. Second, thinking a high share means a large object. One bright sky pixel can score 1.0 for sky while the image holds almost no sky. Share size at a pixel says nothing about area size in the image.

11.2 Semantic, Instance and Panoptic Segmentation

11.2.1 Three Ways to Label Pixels

One photo can be labeled three ways. Do you need kinds, copies, or both plus background. That one question picks the segmentation style.

Segmentation splits by goal. Semantic segmentation — same label for all pixels of one kind — labels every pixel by kind only. Instance segmentation — separate label per object copy — labels each copy apart. Panoptic segmentation — copies plus background in one map — labels copies and background together.

A beach photo makes the split concrete. Three people stand on sand near water. Semantic output uses one label for all people. All three people get the same person color. Sand gets one color such as purple. Water gets one color such as yellow. The map says where water ends and where sand starts and where people stand. It does not say how many people stand there.

Instance output counts copies. Person one gets one color. Person two gets a second color. Person three gets a third color. The map says how many people are present and which pixels belong to which person. Panoptic output keeps the copy count and also labels non-count items. People keep copy colors. Tree keeps its own color. Sky keeps its own color. Other scenery keeps its own color. The map gives full scene parsing plus counts.

Think of paint cans. Semantic work has one can per kind. All people share the person can. Instance work opens a new can per copy. Person one, person two, and person three each get their own can even when the paint looks alike. Panoptic work paints both the cans for copies and the cans for background in one full map. The analogy breaks where copies touch: two touching people still need distinct cans even when the edge is hard to see.

11.2.2 When to Pick Which Type

Pick semantic form when kind areas matter. Cars as one group. Trees as one group. Road as one group. This fits scenery parsing. Pick instance form when counts matter. How many cars sit on a street. How many cats sit in a room. Each cat gets its own label even when cats touch. Pick panoptic form when both matter. How many people are in view and what does the rest of the scene hold. Trees, sky, and road plus per-person copies. Real-world: street scene parsing uses semantic maps. Crowd and traffic counts use instance maps. Full scene understanding for driving uses panoptic maps.

When to pick which: use semantic maps for area plans, instance maps for counts, and panoptic maps for full parses with counts.

Picture a street top view. Semantic colors all cars blue. Instance colors car one blue, car two green, and car three orange. Panoptic keeps those car colors and also colors road gray, trees dark green, and sky light blue. The horizontal axis is position across the street, the vertical axis is label depth from kind to copy. The takeaway: more label depth answers more questions but costs more work.

Scope: Semantic labels assume kind alone drives the choice. Assumption: copies of one kind can safely share a label. That breaks when copies touch or overlap, since one shared blob hides the count. Instance and panoptic forms drop that assumption by giving each copy its own tag.

Do not read same color as same stuff. Color in a demo map marks group choice, not true photo color. Two groups may look red and blue on screen while both are gray cars in the photo. Also, do not expect semantic maps to count. Three touching people form one blob in semantic form.

11.3 Gestalt Perception Principles

11.3.1 Seeing Wholes From Parts

Your eyes fill gaps before you notice. Why does a half-hidden dog still read as a dog. Because sight links fragments to known wholes.

Human sight fills gaps fast. A blurred photo with half the detail gone still reads as a village scene. Viewers spot a tree base where people can sit. They spot a dog from hind legs and a hunchback and a tail and part of a face. They spot cattle lying on grass. They sense an ocean and waves. No edge is crisp. Yet the mind links parts into wholes. Prior views help. We have seen trees and dogs and fields many times. So we link new fragments to known wholes.

Two line drawings push the point home. One drawing shows no triangle. The mind still fills a bright triangle. One drawing shows spikes and chains going into ground. The mind still fills a spiked war hammer even with gaps and blocked parts. The mind closes shapes. It links motion. It links near parts. It keeps track through blocks. That is why Gestalt thought matters for segmentation. It gives rules for grouping that code can copy. The triangle spikes mind fills gaps closure demo is the key anchor here: no drawn triangle, yet a bright triangle pops out.

Think of a half-erased word on a board. You read the full word from a few strokes plus past reading. Sight works the same way with shapes. The analogy breaks where prior views mislead: a strange new object with no past match fills in wrong.

Picture a blur chart. The horizontal axis is image sharpness from blur to crisp, and the vertical axis is recognition ease. Even at low sharpness the curve stays high for known scenes, then climbs steeply as edges return. The takeaway: prior shape knowledge lifts recognition long before edges turn crisp.

11.3.2 Similarity, Proximity, Common Fate, Common Region

Gestalt groups by nearness and likeness and shared motion and shared area. Four rules lead: proximity, similarity, common fate, and common region. Each rule is a test for belonging as one.

Gestalt groups by nearness and likeness and shared motion and shared area. Proximity — nearness grouping — says near parts belong together. Fingers sit next to a palm. So a finger implies a palm nearby. A palm implies fingers nearby. Similarity — likeness grouping — says like parts belong together. Five fingers share shape. So they share the name finger. No one calls one a toe. A human hand looks like other human hands. It does not look like a monkey hand in fine shape.

Common fate — shared motion grouping — says parts that move together belong together. A finger tip moves. The next joint moves with it. The forearm on the same arm moves in a linked way. A far knee does not move with that finger. So finger and forearm on one arm share fate in that action. Knee does not. Common region — shared area grouping — says parts in one bounded area belong together. The palm forms one region. That region differs from the back of the hand or the wrist area. Position plus boundary defines the group.

The fingers palm forearm continuity symmetry hand story ties all four. Tips plus joints move as one fate. Near bones sit in one region. Like fingers share shape. This is the professor's hand anchor: fingers, palm, and forearm belong as one through nearness, likeness, shared motion, and shared area.

11.3.3 Continuity, Symmetry and Closure

Continuity — smooth path grouping — says paths keep smooth direction. A forearm leads to upper arm with triceps and biceps. It does not jump to a foot or to glute muscles. Symmetry — mirror grouping — says mirrored parts pair up. Left and right sides of a face pair. Closure — closed shape grouping — says started shapes seek an end. If a line starts and almost closes, sight closes it. That closure makes a shape pop out.

A hand shows all three. Bones and skin keep smooth order from palm to forearm to upper arm. Left and right fingers mirror in layout. The outline closes into one hand shape even when part of the edge hides. The same logic maps to pixels. Two orange tiger pixels sit in near spots. They share orange strength. They sit inside one smooth closed orange patch. They shift together across frames while green grass pixels do not. So they belong to one object. Two ground pixels with different strengths do not share fate. So they split even when both are ground.

Imagine tracing a curve with your finger. You keep smooth direction rather than jumping. Closure is the urge to join the ends. Symmetry is the pairing of left and right. The takeaway chart is simple: smooth plus mirrored plus closed reads as one object, while jagged plus broken reads as many.

Scope: Gestalt rules assume clean grouping cues line up. Assumption: nearness, likeness, smooth path, and shared motion point the same way. When light or overlap breaks one cue, rules clash. Green tree plus green grass share likeness but differ in source, so likeness alone merges what should split.

Do not treat any single Gestalt cue as proof. Near spots may hold distinct objects. Like colors may come from distinct sources. Smooth paths may cross by chance. Always stack at least two cues, such as likeness plus shared motion, before calling one object.

11.4 Top-Down and Bottom-Up Grouping

11.4.1 Two Rules for Belonging Together

Two tests compete. Do these pixels look alike. Or do they come from the same object even when looks shift. The first test is bottom-up. The second test is top-down.

Top-down grouping — same source grouping — says pixels belong together since they come from the same object. A tiger pixel here and a tiger pixel there belong together since both come from the tiger. This holds even when light shifts strength a bit. Bottom-up grouping — same look grouping — says pixels belong together since they look alike. This orange pixel and that orange pixel belong together since both look orange. Both rules often agree. Both can fail in edge cases.

Grass and tree show the clash. Both can look green. Bottom-up look alone would merge tree green and grass green. Top-down source knowledge would keep tree pixels with tree and grass pixels with grass. So choice of rule shapes the method. Early color histograms lean bottom-up. Object-aware nets can add top-down priors.

Think of sorted mail. Bottom-up sorting groups by envelope color. All green envelopes land as one pile. Top-down sorting groups by sender. All letters from one sender land as one pile even when envelopes differ in shade. The mail analogy breaks where sender is unknown: with no address list, only color is left.

Rule Test Wins when Loses when
Bottom-up same look Do strengths match Light is even, objects differ in color Tree green meets grass green
Top-down same source Do pixels share an object Light shifts, shape priors known No object model exists yet

When to pick which: use look tests for fast early splits, and source tests once shape or object priors exist.

11.4.2 Movement Across Frames

Common fate becomes a frame shift test. Object pixels share one shift, background shows almost none. The frame shift motion grouping equation is:

\[M(x, y, t) = I(x + dx, y + dy, t + 1) - I(x, y, t)\]

Here \(I(x, y, t) \in \mathbb{R}\) is strength at column \(x\), row \(y\), and frame \(t\), \((dx, dy)\) is shift in columns and rows from frame \(t\) to frame \(t+1\), and \(M(x, y, t)\) is the mismatch after shifting. In inline form this is \(M(x, y, t) = I(x + dx, y + dy, t + 1) - I(x, y, t)\). Object points share similar \((dx, dy)\). Background points show near-zero shift.

In words: the session links common fate to next-frame shifts, where object pixels shift together and background shows little shift. A reconstructed inline form is \(M(x, y, t) = I(x + dx, y + dy, t + 1) - I(x, y, t)\) where \(I(x, y, t) \in \mathbb{R}\) is strength at position \((x, y)\) and frame \(t\), and \((dx, dy)\) is shift in columns and rows. Object points share similar \((dx, dy)\). Background points show near-zero shift.

A video is frames on a time line. Frame one plus frame two plus more frames make motion. Take a finger flick. Tip and next joint share shift. Far background does not. The same test works for tiger versus grass. Tiger pixels shift as one hunk when the tiger moves. Grass pixels do not follow that shift. Current still-image work uses one frame at a time. Video work adds this shift test. Real-world: motion-based foreground split and video object tracking use shared-shift grouping.

Run numbers. Suppose a tiger patch at \((x, y) = (50, 80)\) in frame \(t\) moves to \((52, 80)\) in frame \(t+1\), so \((dx, dy) = (2, 0)\). Then \(I(52, 80, t+1) - I(50, 80, t)\) is near 0 for tiger pixels, since the same fur lands on the shifted spot. Grass at \((10, 10)\) has \((dx, dy) \approx (0, 0)\), so its mismatch is also near 0 without shift. The split cue is shared \((2, 0)\) for tiger versus near-zero for grass. Sense-check: one shared shift marks one moving hunk.

Scope: The shift test assumes small, rigid-like motion between frames and steady light. Assumption: \((dx, dy)\) is shared across the object and tiny enough that strengths match after shifting. Fast jumps, occlusions, or flickering light break the match, so \(M\) grows even for true object pixels.

Picture two overlaid frames. The horizontal axis is column \(x\), the vertical axis is row \(y\). Tiger pixels draw one arrow of length 2 to the right. Grass pixels draw dots. The takeaway: one shared arrow means one moving group.

Do not apply motion logic to a single still photo. One frame has no \(t+1\). Also, do not read zero mismatch as proof of stillness. A uniform wall gives zero mismatch for many shifts, since any \((dx, dy)\) lands on the same strength.

11.5 Histogram-Based Segmentation and Modes

11.5.1 Histogram Counts of Strength Values

How do you find groups with no labels at all. Count strengths. Tall bars hint at objects.

A histogram — a count bar chart — counts how many pixels hold each strength value. For each pixel \(f(x, y)\), we plot its red-green-blue values and count repeats. In words: the session plots orange pixels bunching together and green pixels bunching elsewhere. The reconstructed count form is \[H(v) = \sum_{x} \sum_{y} [f(x, y) = v]\] where \(f(x, y)\) is pixel strength at column \(x\) and row \(y\), \(v\) is one strength level such as 0 to 255, \(H(v) \in \mathbb{N}\) is the count of pixels with value \(v\), and \([\cdot]\) is 1 when true and 0 when false. For color work the same count runs per channel. This is the histogram count bars form: bars built from counts at each level.

A tiger sketch makes it concrete. The session uses rough gray-scale bands as a teaching sketch. Green sits near 0 to 50. Orange sits near 100 to 175. Brown ground without grass sits near 180 to 255. Orange pixels bunch in the middle band. Green pixels bunch in the low band. Brown pixels bunch in the high band. Each bunch suggests one object. This is bottom-up thought. Same look means same group. The tiger orange green brown histogram bands anchor this: orange bunches mid, green bunches low, brown bunches high.

The histogram count bars equation plus channel split is:

\[H(v) = \sum_{x} \sum_{y} [f(x, y) = v]\]

Here \(f(x, y)\) is pixel strength at \((x, y)\), \(v \in [0, 255]\) is one strength level for 8-bit data, \(H(v)\) is the count bar height at \(v\), and the bracket is 1 when the pixel equals \(v\) and 0 otherwise. For RGB work, build \(H_R(v)\), \(H_G(v)\), and \(H_B(v)\) separately, one per channel.

Take numbers. Suppose a tiny 4-pixel row has strengths \([100, 102, 100, 200]\). Then \(H(100) = 2\), \(H(102) = 1\), and \(H(200) = 1\). Two bars hint at two groups: a mid bunch near 100 and a high lone value at 200. Sense-check: bar heights sum to pixel count, here \(2 + 1 + 1 = 4\).

Picture the chart. The horizontal axis is strength \(v\) from 0 to 255. The vertical axis is count \(H(v)\). Low humps sit left, mid humps sit center, high humps sit right. The takeaway: humps mark candidate objects, gaps mark candidate cuts.

11.5.2 Modes, Thresholds and Boundary Errors

A mode — a high-count peak — is a local high bar in the histogram. A threshold — a cut value — is a split point between peaks. The plan is simple. Find peaks. Cut between them. Label each pixel by its band. One band for big balls. One band for mid balls. One band for small balls. Three peaks mean three groups. The grayscale balls big mid small rim threshold faults demo tests this exact plan.

Worked mini-example — gray balls with rim faults. Take gray balls on a plain floor: big balls near strength 50, mid balls near 130, and small balls near 210. Ideal bars peak at 50, 130, and 210. Place cuts at \(T_1 = 90\) and \(T_2 = 170\). Rule: strength below 90 means big, 90 to 170 means mid, above 170 means small. A rim pixel on the edge of a big ball reads 95 because it mixes ball plus floor. The hard cut forces it to mid, even though it belongs to the big ball rim. That forced rim label shifts the outline by one pixel. Over many rim pixels the shape error accumulates. Bold answer: cuts at 90 and 170 label cores right but force rim pixels wrong, so rim error is the cost of hard thresholds.

Two faults bite fast. First, exact modes are hard to compute on raw values. Real values read 250, 251, 253, 254, 255 near one peak. Noise splits the top. The strict max jumps about. Smoothing with Gaussian kernels helps but leaves some noise. Small errors at edges add up. A pixel on the rim of a big ball shares a border with a mid ball. Hard cuts force it to one side. Forced labels on rims shift shapes. Balls cannot overlap in real space, so rim faults read as large shape faults. This is the rim pixel forced threshold boundary error anchor: forced cuts make boundary error accumulate along every edge.

Second, counts drop space. A histogram counts values, not spots. One hundred orange pixels in one tiger hunk give the same bar as one hundred orange pixels strewn about. The bar alone cannot say they form one closed object. Tree green versus grass green shows the same gap. Same green bar, different spots and sources. Space must join color.

Scope: Histogram cuts assume each object owns a distinct strength band and light stays even. Assumption: modes stay apart and thresholds sit in true valleys. When bands overlap or light drifts across the photo, one global cut cannot fit all spots, so local cuts or richer cues must join.

Do not chase the exact top bar as the true mode. Noise makes tops jump among 250, 251, and 253. Do not read a tall bar as one object either. Tall bars count values, not closed spots. Both traps push toward brittle cuts.

11.5.3 Derivatives for Peaks and Why They Stay Brittle

Peak hunting uses change tests on the histogram. The derivative peak tests are:

\[H'(v) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad H''(v) < 0\]

Here \(H(v)\) is the count at level \(v\), \(H'(v)\) is change per level (first-order slope), and \(H''(v)\) is change of change (second-order bend). A flat top with \(H'(v) = 0\) plus a hump bend with \(H''(v) < 0\) marks a mode top. In inline form this is \(H'(v) = 0\) for a flat top and \(H''(v) < 0\) for a hump top.

In words: the session notes peaks can be sought with first-order or second-order change tests. The reconstructed inline forms are \(H'(v) = 0\) for a flat top and \(H''(v) < 0\) for a hump top, where \(H(v)\) is the count at level \(v\), \(H'(v)\) is change per level, and \(H''(v)\) is change of change. Noise makes these tests jump. Many tiny spurious tops appear. Which tops count as true modes. Three or four. No fixed rule tells. A small extra hump may hint at ultra-small bearings. It may also be noise. Humans see three main humps with ease. Code needs a fixed rule. Hard-coded rules miss or split groups. Overlapping humps for small and mid bearings resist clean cuts.

Take numbers. Smooth counts near a true peak read \([40, 48, 50, 48, 40]\) at \(v = 48\) to \(52\). Slopes \(H'(v)\) go \(+8, +2, -2, -8\), crossing 0 at the top, while \(H''(v)\) stays negative. Add noise and the row reads \([40, 49, 44, 50, 40]\). Now \(H'(v)\) crosses 0 twice, faking two tops. A Gaussian kernel smooths the row before testing, which damps jitter but can also flatten a true small hump. Sense-check: smoothing trades false tops for missed small groups.

Old use cases fit this limit. Black-and-white aerial photos for runway spotting worked when the ask was coarse. World War II and Cold War era tasks used such coarse splits when compute was weak. Modern 0 to 255 color with 16 million combos overwhelms fixed cuts. Real-world: coarse aerial reconnaissance and simple black-white document splits still use histogram cuts. Fine photo parsing does not.

Exam note: Expect a short question on why histograms lose space and why that forces richer methods. Answer in three beats: mode noise on values like 250 to 255 fakes peaks, 100 strewn orange pixels match 100 grouped orange pixels in counts, and hard rim cuts force edge pixels wrong.

11.6 K-Means Clustering for Segmentation

11.6.1 Pixels as Points in Color Space

What if cuts could move by themselves. Turn each pixel into a point, drop hubs among them, and let nearest-hub wins replace fixed cuts.

Each color pixel becomes a point. In words: the session treats \(f(x, y)\) for RGB as a three-number vector such as 128, 128, 128 and white as 255, 255, 255. The reconstructed inline form is \(p = (R, G, B)\) where \(R \in [0, 255]\) is red strength and \(G \in [0, 255]\) is green strength and \(B \in [0, 255]\) is blue strength. Each axis spans 0 to 255 for 8-bit color. The full set spans about 16 million combos, since each channel spans 0 to 255 for 8-bit per channel and three channels give 24-bit color with \(256^3 = 16,777,216\) shades.

Distance decides kin. Close points in this space look alike. Far points look apart. The session names Euclidean, Hamming, or other gaps as options. The main inline form is \(d(p, c) = \|p - c\|_2\) where \(p \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is a pixel point, \(c \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is a center, and \(\|\cdot\|_2\) is straight-line length. Smallest gap wins. Each point joins the nearest center.

The Euclidean gap and assignment rule is:

\[d(p, c_k) = \|p - c_k\|_2 = \sqrt{(R - R_k)^2 + (G - G_k)^2 + (B - B_k)^2}\]

Here \(p = (R, G, B) \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is one pixel point, \(c_k = (R_k, G_k, B_k) \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is hub \(k\), and \(d(p, c_k)\) is the straight-line gap. Rule: join the hub with least gap. Hamming gaps suit binary codes, but RGB uses Euclidean gaps.

Take numbers. Pixel \(p = (200, 50, 50)\) sits near red. Hub \(c_1 = (210, 60, 60)\) and hub \(c_2 = (50, 200, 50)\). Gaps are \(d(p, c_1) = \sqrt{100 + 100 + 100} \approx 17.3\) and \(d(p, c_2)\) well above 200. The pixel joins \(c_1\). Sense-check: small gap means like color, large gap means apart.

11.6.2 K-Means Steps and Local Minima

A center — a group hub — is one of \(K\) hubs in color space. The K-means squared objective is:

\[\min_{c_1,\dots,c_K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{p \in C_k} \|p - c_k\|_2^2\]

Here \(C_k\) is the set of points joined to hub \(k\), \(c_k \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is hub \(k\), and \(\|\cdot\|_2^2\) is squared length. The loop seeks hub spots that make total squared gap small.

A center — a group hub — is one of \(K\) hubs in color space. The steps run in a loop. Seed \(K\) hubs at random. For each point, score gaps to all hubs \(c_1, c_2, c_3\). Put point \(p\) in the group with the least gap. Move each hub to the mean of its joined points. Repeat. Hubs drift. Points rejoin. The loop stops near a local low of total gap. In words: the session shows red, blue, orange, and yellow hubs drifting as points bunch to the nearest hub. The reconstructed block objective is \[\min_{c_1,\dots,c_K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{p \in C_k} \|p - c_k\|_2^2\] where \(C_k\) is the set of points joined to hub \(k\), \(c_k \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is hub \(k\), and \(\|\cdot\|_2^2\) is squared length. Ties adapt as hubs shift. The end state can be a local low, not the global low.

Use the procedural spine here. Purpose: replace fixed cuts with adaptive hubs. Inputs: all pixel points plus hand-set \(K\). Steps: seed hubs, assign by least gap, move hubs to group means, repeat. Trace: start with random hubs, watch red and blue hubs drift toward dense clouds. Complexity: each round scores every point against \(K\) hubs, so large photos with large \(K\) run slow. The live demo took about 30 to 40 seconds for one image.

This fixes two histogram pains. No fixed cut values. Points pick groups by gap. Hubs adapt so modes emerge from data. The session codes this on a dog photo. Steps are load image, read as a NumPy array, flatten pixels to rows, run K-means with \(K = 8\), reshape labels to image shape, show the label map. The map assigns each pixel to a region on its own. Foreground and background split much better than with raw histogram cuts. NumPy is the array tool named here: load as array, flatten to rows, cluster, reshape labels back.

Picture hub drift. The horizontal axis is red strength, the vertical axis is green strength. Hubs start scattered, then arrows pull each hub toward its cloud mean. The takeaway: hubs chase density, so cuts emerge from data.

Scope: K-means assumes round, even-sized clouds where mean marks the middle. Assumption: Euclidean gaps fit the data and \(K\) matches true group count. Stretched, curved, or tiny groups break the fit, since one mean cannot mark a crescent.

11.6.3 Hard Borders and the Missing K

Two limits stay. First, \(K\) stays hand-set. How many groups does one photo need. Elbow plots rarely settle it for photos. In the dog demo \(K = 8\) shows body and background but merges fine parts. A pink tongue reads grayish since its bright pixels join the nearest big hub. More hubs such as 9, 10, 11, or 12 would free the tongue pixels. But no built-in rule picks that number. For 16 million combos mapped to 8 groups, many shades must share. Grid search and hunch remain. Run time also bites. One image took about 30 to 40 seconds in the live demo.

Second, borders stay hard and straight. K-means cuts space with flat walls. Real shapes curve. Faces are round. Noses and eyes curve. Belly curves shade from light to dark. The dog belly demo shows the cost. Darker belly pixels join a dark group. Lighter body pixels join another group. The curve between them gets a straight hard wall, not a soft round edge. Human-made boxes have straight lines. Natural bodies do not. This is the straight walls miss round face nose curves anchor: flat walls cannot follow nose and belly curves.

Worked mini-example — dog demo hubs tongue belly curvature merging. Take the dog photo with \(K = 8\). Tongue pixels read bright pink near \((240, 150, 150)\). No small pink hub exists, so the nearest large hub is grayish near \((180, 170, 170)\). One hub owns light fur, another owns dark fur, and the straight wall between them slices the smooth belly shade. Raise \(K\) to 10 and a pink hub near \((240, 150, 150)\) can form, freeing the tongue. 11.7 Mixture Models and Expectation Maximization

11.7.1 From Hard Gaps to Soft Shares

Straight walls slice curves. What if groups could stretch and tilt instead. Soft blobs do that.

A discriminative gap method — a border-by-gap method — draws hard lines by least gap. K-means is of this kind. A generative mix method — a shape-by-chance method — draws soft blobs by chance scores. Each blob can stretch and tilt. Points get a share per blob, not a forced single tag. This soft form fits curves better than straight walls.

The mix uses Gaussians. A Gaussian — a bell hump — is set by a mean and a spread. In words: the session places a point \(x\) among blobs with distinct mean and spread values and scores the chance it sits in each blob. The reconstructed inline normal is \(\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \Sigma)\) where \(x\) is a pixel point, \(\mu\) is the mean hub, and \(\Sigma\) is spread and shape. A toy share vector in the session reads about 0.49 plus 0.31 plus 0.20 across three blobs, summing to 1.0. The top share points to the best blob. A 0.5 rule is used as a teaching cut. Above 0.5 means sits in that blob for that step.

Note on numbers: the spoken toy shares sound noisy on tape, at one point like 0.49 plus 0.3 plus 0.31, which would sum past 1. Shares across blobs must sum to 1 at each point, so the reconciled teaching triple is 0.49 plus 0.31 plus 0.20. The idea is fixed: top share climbs toward 0.6 while rivals fall toward 0.2 and 0.2 as blobs reshape.

The Gaussian bell for blob \(b\) is \(\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_b, \Sigma_b)\), where \(x \in \mathbb{R}^3\) is a pixel point in color space, \(\mu_b\) is the mean hub of blob \(b\), and \(\Sigma_b\) is its spread and shape (covariance). The share of point \(i\) in blob \(b\) is \(r_{ib} \in [0, 1]\), with \(\sum_b r_{ib} = 1\) at each point. The mix weight \(\alpha_b\) is the mean share of blob \(b\) across points.

Think of overlappingspotlights. Each spotlight is a blob with a bright middle plus soft falloff. A floor spot lit by three lamps gets a share from each lamp, such as 0.49 plus 0.31 plus 0.20. The brightest lamp wins the spot for now, but all three keep partial claims. The analogy breaks where blobs turn tiny: a very tight blob acts almost hard, like a wall again.

11.7.2 Expectation Step and Maximization Step

The loop has two beats. The expectation beat scores membership. Given current \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\), score for each point the chance it sits in blob one or blob two or blob three. The maximization beat reshapes blobs. Use the mean of share-weighted points to form a new \(\mu\). Use the share-weighted spread to form a new \(\Sigma\). Use mean shares to form new mix weights \(\alpha\). In words: the session says inch the blob so the top share climbs from about 0.49 toward 0.6 while rivals fall to about 0.2 and 0.2. The reconstructed weight update in plain form is \[\alpha_b^{new} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} r_{ib}\] where \(r_{ib} \in [0, 1]\) is the share of point \(i\) in blob \(b\), \(N\) is point count, and \(\alpha_b^{new}\) is the new mix weight for blob \(b\). New \(\mu_b\) and \(\Sigma_b\) come from the same share-weighted means. Each blob tugs points by reshaping itself. The loop repeats until shares settle. This is the Gaussian bell mix weight alpha update in full.

Work the update with numbers. Take \(N = 4\) points with shares in blob 1 as \([0.6, 0.5, 0.4, 0.5]\). Then \(\alpha_1^{new} = (0.6 + 0.5 + 0.4 + 0.5)/4 = 2.0/4 = 0.5\). New mean \(\mu_1\) is the share-weighted mean: sum of \(r_{i1} x_i\) divided by sum of \(r_{i1}\). If points are at \(x = [100, 110, 120, 130]\) with those shares, the top is \((0.6 \times 100 + 0.5 \times 110 + 0.4 \times 120 + 0.5 \times 130)/2.0 = (60 + 55 + 48 + 65)/2.0 = 228/2.0 = 114\). So the hub inches toward the high-share side. New spread \(\Sigma_1\) is the share-weighted variance around 114. Sense-check: larger shares tug harder, so the blob follows its fans.

A name-based method analogy is used in the session. Just as that method adapts hubs to hog points, this method adapts \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\) to raise point shares. Exact full derivations sit outside test scope. The goal is thought flow, not formula recall.

Scope: Mixture blobs assume data truly form overlapping bell humps and \(N\) is large enough to fill spreads. Assumption: blob family plus blob count are well picked, such as Gaussian blobs with three blobs for three color families. Wrong family or wrong count still fits, but fits the wrong shapes.

Picture the blobs as ellipses on a color chart. The horizontal axis is red strength, the vertical axis is green strength. Round K-means circles become tilted ellipses here. The takeaway: tilt plus stretch follows curved shade clouds that circles must slice.

Do not read soft shares as final labels. Shares are working weights that shift each round. Do not force a 0.5 cut too early either. Early 0.49 versus 0.51 gaps flip with ease before blobs settle.

Worked mini-example — three blobs shares climb toward 0.6. Take one pixel point \(x\) among three blobs with start shares 0.49 plus 0.31 plus 0.20. Top blob holds 0.49, just under the 0.5 teaching cut. Run expectation: score all points under current \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\). Run maximization: reshape each blob to its share-weighted mean and spread, with \(\alpha_b^{new} = (1/N)\sum_i r_{ib}\). The top blob stretches toward \(x\), so its share climbs toward 0.6 while rivals fall to about 0.2 and 0.2. Bold answer: 0.49 grows toward 0.6 as the blob reshapes to hog its points, proving soft reshape beats straight walls.

With about 100 blobs the map finds many fine color patches but runs long. The count of blobs stays hand-set, just as \(K\) stayed hand-set. The blob family also stays hand-set. 11.8.1 Color Plus Position Joins the Vector

11.8.2 Kernel Density and the Climb to the Peak

A kernel density estimator — a smooth hump counter — smooths counts and climbs to humps. A bandwidth — a search width — sets hump width. Call it \(h\). A mode is where points bunch most. The Gaussian kernel density bandwidth mode climb form is:

\[\hat{f}(x) = \frac{1}{n h^d} \sum_{i=1}^{n} K\left(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\right)\]

where \(x\) is the probe hub, \(x_i\) is point \(i\), \(n\) is point count, \(d\) is dim count, \(h > 0\) is bandwidth, and \(K(\cdot)\) is the Gaussian hump. A common 1D hump is \[K(u) = \exp\left(-\frac{\|u\|^2}{2}\right)\] where \(u = (x - x_i)/h\) and \(\|\cdot\|\) is length. Near the hump top, \(u\) nears 0 and \(K(u)\) nears 1.

In words: the session centers a Gaussian hump at \(x\) and scores nearby points \(x_i\) with spread 1. Close points give \(x - x_i\) near 0. Then \(\exp(0) = 1\), so the kernel score peaks. The reconstructed block form is \[\hat{f}(x) = \frac{1}{n h^d} \sum_{i=1}^{n} K\left(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\right)\] where \(x\) is the probe hub, \(x_i\) is point \(i\), \(n\) is point count, \(d\) is dim count, \(h > 0\) is bandwidth, and \(K(\cdot)\) is the Gaussian hump. A common 1D hump is \[K(u) = \exp\left(-\frac{\|u\|^2}{2}\right)\] where \(u = (x - x_i)/h\) and \(\|\cdot\|\) is length. Near the hump top, \(u\) nears 0 and \(K(u)\) nears 1. This uses a Gaussian kernel as the hump shape.

The climb rule is plain. Drop a hump. Sense where points bunch. Shift hub toward the bunch. Recompute hub as the bunch mean. Repeat until shifts stop. At the top, \(x - x_i\) stays near 0 for the hump core. The kernel says no move pays. So the hub parks at the peak. This is a mean shift. It tracks the bunch mean. The session asks why mean, not mode. Mean with a Gaussian hump is calmer. Raw mode jumps on values like 250, 251, 252. Gaussian smoothing damps that jitter while seeking high-count zones.

Run numbers in 1D. Points at \([10, 11, 12, 50]\) with \(h = 5\). Probe at \(x = 11\). Scores \(K((11 - x_i)/5)\) are near 1 for 10, 11, and 12, but near 0 for 50. The weighted mean stays near 11, so the hub barely moves. Probe at \(x = 30\). Near points pull weakly from both sides, and the hub drifts toward the dense trio at 11. Sense-check: dense trios win pulls, lone far points lose.

Scope: Kernel climbs assume humps are smooth and bandwidth \(h\) matches true bunch width. Assumption: \(h > 0\) is well picked and dims stay low enough to chart. Too-small \(h\) sprouts false humps, too-large \(h\) merges true humps, and high dims break the chart.

Picture the climb. The horizontal axis is strength or spot, the vertical axis is density \(\hat{f}(x)\). The hub is a ball on the slope. Each step moves the ball uphill to the bunch mean until it parks on the peak. The takeaway: mean shift is uphill walking with a smooth hump ruler.

Do not set \(h\) blind. Width 1 finds almost all tiny shifts but costs much compute. Width 10 runs fast but may skip true humps or stall. Also, do not trust a single climb. One hump can trap in a small dip, so many starts are needed.

11.8.3 Many Hubs and Attraction Basins

All hopeful paths that land there mark one mode.

Three humps near one true top all climb to that top. A far hump climbs to its own top. End tally says two modes found. No hand count of modes is needed. If the image histogram holds five humps, five basins emerge. If it holds eight, eight emerge. This fixes the biggest histogram pain. \(K\) in K-means and mix count in mixture models needed hand picks. Here basin count sets group count on its own. Once hubs park, nearby points join the nearest parked mode. Groups form. A soft tail can then refine. Use basin count as \(K\) for K-means. Or use basin count as mix count for mixture models. That hybrid — basins for count plus soft blobs for shape — is the 2002 tweak the session points to for reading.

11.8.4 Search Boxes, Step Size and Local Traps
