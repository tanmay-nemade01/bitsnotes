2.1 What Computer Vision Sees in a Picture

2.1.1 A Photo Is a Frozen Slice of a Moving World

Why does a photo feel still even when the world never stops? That gap is the whole starting point. A camera freezes one instant of a moving world into a flat view.

A snapshot (one frozen instant of a live scene), taken at time \(t\), holds shape and brightness but drops motion.

Think of pausing a beach video. At \(t = 1\) second the wave sits left. At \(t = 2\) seconds it has rolled forward and the sun sits a touch lower. The paused frame keeps sand, water, far trees, and sky exactly as they looked at the pause time. It keeps none of the motion between frames. That is what a 2D image is: a representation of the 3D world around us at one fixed \(t\).

Here \(t\) is the capture time in seconds, and the stored object is a flat two-dimensional view. The live world has width, height, depth, and time. The photo keeps width, height, and brightness patterns. It drops depth and time. Later sections add depth back with extra sensors. For now the rule is simple: one instant in, one flat grid out.

Picture the axes. The sensor plane spans \(x\) across and \(y\) down or up. Brightness sits at each \((x, y)\) seat. Time does not sit in the grid at all. Two photos of the same beach at two times give two different grids, even when the sand looks the same.

2.1.2 Light, Object, and Imaging System

Every picture starts with three players: an object or scene, a source of light falling on it, and an imaging system that catches the light coming back.

An imaging chain (object plus light plus sensor that writes numbers) runs as: incident ray in, bounced ray out, analog-to-digital step, fixed-size matrix stored.

An incident ray (incoming light beam that strikes a surface), written with no special symbol yet, can come from the sun, a room lamp, or light bent back from some other surface. It strikes the object. Part of it bounces back. The imaging system catches that returned ray.

Inside the imaging system, an analog-to-digital step turns the smooth light signal into numbers. Light is smooth: brightness slides without steps. Numbers are stepped: each seat holds one fixed value. That conversion is the core theme of this session. The system writes the scene into a fixed-size matrix:

\[ I \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N} \]

Here \(I\) is the stored gray frame, \(M\) is the row count, \(N\) is the column count, and \(\mathbb{R}^{M \times N}\) is the set of all \(M\)-by-\(N\) grids of real numbers. Each cell of the matrix is one pixel. Each pixel holds one measured brightness value. A pixel is therefore not a tiny square of glass; it is one addressed number in this grid.

2.1.3 Fixed-Size Matrices and Everyday Screens

A fixed-size matrix (grid whose row count and column count stay set), for example \(720\) by \(720\), never stretches with the scene. The scene may be huge. The stored form is always some \(M\) by \(N\) grid.

The screen in front of a viewer is one everyday case. A laptop camera gives high-definition frames of a set size. Bigger labels such as 2K, 4K, and 5K mean larger matrices with more cells and finer detail. Small sizes show up in model training too: a size of 128 by 128 is common in deep learning tasks because the math runs fast and still holds enough shape.

Work the count once. A 12 by 14 grid holds \(12 \times 14 = 168\) cells. A 128 by 128 grid holds \(128 \times 128 = 16384\) cells. A 720 by 720 grid holds \(720 \times 720 = 518400\) cells. Same idea at every size, only the cell count shifts.

The visual takeaway: draw cell count on the horizontal axis and visible detail on the vertical axis. The curve climbs fast at first, then flattens. Past a point more cells add cost without adding much visible shape. That plateau is why training pipelines pick small grids on purpose.

Scope: the \(M\)-by-\(N\) promise holds only per frame. It says nothing about color depth, time rate, or depth sensing. A 128 by 128 gray frame and a 128 by 128 color frame share the same grid but carry different facts per cell.

2.1.4 The Eye as a Model Camera

Human eyes run on the same plan as cameras. Light bounces off things and enters the eye. Two kinds of cells line the back of the eye. Rods sense whether light is present, so night shapes still read. Cones split light into red, green, and blue parts, so day color reads.

Monitors, phones, and cameras copy that plan cell for cell. A sensor takes the place of rods and cones. Software or circuits take the place of the brain. The result is the same: a pattern of light becomes a pattern of numbers with color tags.

The everyday analogy is a vote counter at an election desk. Rods are desks that count only turnout: light or no light. Cones are desks that sort ballots into three piles: red, green, blue. The sensor does the same sort, then writes the pile counts into the grid. Where the analogy breaks: the eye adapts its gain in real time and blends both eyes into depth, while a plain camera writes one fixed grid per snap unless extra code or extra sensors step in.

2.1.5 Where Machines Beat People and Vice Versa

Which jobs suit machines and which still suit people? The line keeps moving, so learn the pattern rather than the moment.

A plant-floor camera checks 500 metal caps per minute for scratches. Each cap gets the same test: mean brightness inside a ring must sit between 120 and 180, else flag. A person flags the first 50 caps well, then tires and misses one in ten. The camera flags all 500 the same way at hour one and hour eight. The final count is exact: flagged versus passed, with no drift.

Machines win at dull repeat checks. Visual quality checks on a plant floor are one case. A camera never gets bored. It flags each defect the same way each time because it treats a picture as numbers and runs the same test each time.

People used to win at open-ended art. A hand-painted style image felt safe from machines two or three years back. That gap has closed fast. Machines now paint, draw, and stylize with ease.

Once a frame is numeric, many jobs open at once. A rig can spot a face and name the person. It can tail a walker across frames. It can watch pupils during a test to flag unfair means. With more than one view it can even lift a flat scene into 3D shape through methods named NeRF and 3D Gaussian Splatting, which target maps and civil scans already use. NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields) learns a smooth 3D glow field from many flat views and re-renders new views. 3D Gaussian Splatting places thousands of soft 3D blobs and splats them onto each view for fast high-detail maps. Both names check out against current vision literature.

A common trap is to say machines "see" like people. They do not. They compare numbers against fixed rules. Change the light a little and the numbers shift, so the same rule can pass one frame and fail the next unless the pipeline normalizes light first.

A photo freezes one time \(t\) of a moving 3D world into a fixed \(M\)-by-\(N\) grid of numbers through object, light, and sensor — and that numeric form is what lets machines check faces, walkers, plant defects, NeRF scenes, and Gaussian-splatted maps with the same repeat test. Next the grid itself becomes concrete: rows, columns, and brightness steps.

Real-world: visual quality inspection on a manufacturing line uses cameras to spot defects that bore human eyes, while city mapping rigs fuse many flat frames into NeRF or Gaussian-splatted 3D street models.

2.2 How a Camera Turns Light into Numbers

2.2.1 The 12 by 14 Grid Example

How does a smooth scene become a table of numbers? Watch one small grid fill cell by cell.

One object of interest is drawn on a board. The camera maps it onto 12 rows and 14 columns. The cell count is \(12 \times 14 = 168\). Bright zones get high numbers such as 200 to 255. Dark zones get low numbers such as 0 to 20. Mid zones get gray values such as 10, 15, or 20. White maps to 255 and black maps to 0 on an 8-bit path. Each of the 168 cells now holds one brightness number. Sense-check: 168 numbers fully describe this tiny frame, and a bigger grid would hold the same scene with finer cells.

That mapping is what "putting values into pixels" means. Each cell address \((x, y)\) holds one measured step. The frame freezes the beach-like motion from 2.1 into this matrix form, so later math can sum, shift, and filter it. Keywords for this flow step: sensor grid writes scene into matrix buckets per bit depth.

2.2.2 Bits Set the Brightness Scale

What sets the top of the brightness scale? The bit count per pixel.

A bit depth (count of binary digits stored per pixel), written \(n\), sets the level count \(K = 2^{n}\). Black is \(0\) and white is \(K - 1 = 2^{n} - 1\).

Here \(n\) is the bit count and \(K\) is the distinct-level count. The full span is:

\[ 0 \le r \le 2^{n} - 1 \]

Here \(r\) is one stored brightness step. An 8-bit system holds \(2^{8} = 256\) levels from \(0\) to \(255\), where \(0\) means full black and \(255\) means full white. A 3-bit system holds \(2^{3} = 8\) levels from \(0\) to \(7\). Older car dash displays worked near this small range. A 4-bit system holds \(2^{4} = 16\) levels from \(0\) to \(15\). On any \(n\)-bit setup, black is \(0\) and white is \(2^{n} - 1\).

Think of paint pots on a shelf. A 3-bit shelf holds 8 pots. An 8-bit shelf holds 256 pots. The scene is the same; only the pot count shifts. Where the analogy breaks: real sensors also add noise, so two snaps of the same gray can land in side-by-side pots rather than the same pot.

Scope: bit depth sets tone steps, not grid size. Raising \(n\) smooths shade ramps but never sharpens edges. Raising \(M\) and \(N\) sharpens edges but never adds new tones. The two knobs stay separate through the whole lecture.

2.2.3 Sensor, Color Vote, and Stored Value

A lens only bends and aims light. A sensor behind it does the measuring through an analog-to-digital step. For each small spot it asks which stored color the incoming light best matches, then writes the bucket number.

The mesh of color filters over the sensor is called a Bayer filter. The name checks out: Bryce Bayer's 1976 color filter array mosaic, used in most single-chip color cameras, with twice as many green cells as red or blue because eyes sense green detail best. Each spot votes for a color by nearest match, and neighboring spots later fill in the missing sheets through demosaicing. The grayscale map works the same way: incoming strength falls into one preset bucket. On a small 3-bit scale a patch that looks like bucket 2 gets stored as 2. On a wide 8-bit scale the same patch might read near 255 for white or near 0 for black, with mid grays near 10 to 20.

The exact circuits sit outside this course. Still, the chain matters in this order:

\[ \text{wavelength in} \to \text{bucket match} \to \text{stored number out} \]

That chain feeds each preset color map the system ships with.

2.2.4 Depth Needs Its Own Channel

Q: The eye sees depth as a third dimension. How does that third dimension reach this flat 2D map? A: Depth arrives as a separate channel, not as part of the flat grid. A depth sensor such as LiDAR builds a cloud of points, one point at a time, until a 3D shape forms. The axis set always follows the sensor type. A plain gray frame stays two-dimensional with rows, columns, and one brightness per cell. A color frame adds channels, not depth. A depth frame adds a range value per spot from its own sensor.

A plain gray frame has rows, columns, and one brightness per cell. A color frame adds two more brightness sheets, not depth. A depth frame adds a range value per spot from its own sensor such as LiDAR. So the stored axes always match the input type: gray is \(M \times N \times 1\), color is \(M \times N \times 3\), depth adds its own range map.

2.2.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: When light hits the lens, how does that smooth analog light become a number like 254 or 74? Is that software or hardware? A: A sensor plus an analog-to-digital converter does the job in hardware. The lens only aims light. The sensor measures each beam, matches it to a preset bucket, and writes the bucket number. Wavelength sets the match: the violet-blue edge sits near 380 to 450 nm, green sits near 495 to 570 nm with peak eye response near 550 nm, and red stretches toward 700 nm. A reading of 254 means the beam landed in bucket 254 of 256 on an 8-bit path, while 74 means bucket 74. On a 3-bit setup each band maps to a small step from 0 to 7. On a wide 8-bit setup the same match maps to a step from 0 to 255.

The reconciled wavelength bands above fix the garbled numbers: visible light spans about 380 to 750 nm, blue near 400 to 450 nm, green near 500 to 570 nm. Small input shifts in wavelength move the bucket pick by one or two steps, which is why smooth daylight turns into stepped numbers without visible harm.

A common trap is to credit the lens with measuring. The lens never writes numbers. Swap lenses and the buckets stay the same for the same sensor. Swap sensors and the buckets shift even behind the same lens.

Exam note: a camera maps a scene onto an \(M\)-by-\(N\) grid with \(2^{n}\) buckets per cell through sensor plus converter, while depth needs its own LiDAR-style channel. Next this grid becomes a math function over positions and channels.

Real-world: phone and car cameras pair a small lens with a Bayer-mosaic sensor and an on-chip converter, so daylight wavelengths land in fixed 0-to-255 buckets before any app sees them.

2.3 Grayscale and Color Images as Functions

2.3.1 The Function f of x comma y

Why write a picture as a math function? Because then every pixel has an address and a value that later sums and filters can use.

A gray image function (map from each seat to one brightness), written \(f(x, y)\), takes column position \(x\) and row position \(y\) and returns one brightness \(r\).

Here \(x\) is the column position, \(y\) is the row position, and \(r\) is the brightness at that seat. The full map runs from pairs of positions to brightness values:

\[ f:\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R} \]

Here \(\mathbb{R}\) means the real numbers, so \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is the set of \((x, y)\) pairs and each pair maps to one real brightness \(r\). On a real grid the domain is stepped rather than smooth: seats run \((0, 0)\) to \((M-1, N-1)\) and each seat holds one step.

A 3 by 3 grid shows why the domain wording matters. Three rows times three columns gives nine cells. So the "R squared is nine" remark means a 3 by 3 grid holds nine brightness slots, one real number per cell. The function view and the matrix view are the same object said two ways.

2.3.2 Hills and Valleys of Brightness

Plot brightness as height over the flat grid and the image turns into hills and valleys.

White zones rise into tall peaks near 255. Black zones sink to 0. Gray slopes sit between. The axes are \(x\) across, \(y\) deep, and brightness up. One demo view showed a white patch as the tallest peak at 255 with a black patch at 0, and the rest formed slopes and steps between them.

That 3D view is only a drawing aid. The stored form stays a flat matrix of numbers. The aid helps because edges become cliffs: a sharp black-to-white border is a vertical wall in the height plot, while a soft shade ramp is a gentle slope. That picture returns whenever filters hunt for edges by slope size.

2.3.3 One Channel Versus Three Channels

How do gray and color frames differ in shape? By channel count.

A channel (one full \(M\)-by-\(N\) sheet of steps), counted as 1 for gray and 3 for red-green-blue color, sets the frame shape \(M \times N \times C\).

A gray frame has one channel. Its shape reads like 128 by 128 by 1, where 128 is the row count, 128 is the column count, and 1 is the channel count. A color frame has three channels. Its shape reads like 128 by 128 by 3, one sheet each for red, green, and blue.

Each sheet alone spans \(0\) to \(255\) on an 8-bit setup. One pixel is then a triple. A triple of \((0, 1, 0)\) looks dark with a touch of green. Raise the blue and green sheets to 255 each and slide only the red value: the shown color shifts at each red step, so small input shifts give visibly new colors.

The count grows fast. With 256 steps per sheet, the total is:

\[ 256^3 = 16777216 \]

Work it as \(256 \times 256 = 65536\), then \(65536 \times 256 = 16777216\). So an 8-bit color setup can name more than 16 million colors: 256 red picks times 256 green picks times 256 blue picks. Later sessions take up color in full. Until then gray frames keep the math light because one sheet is cheaper than three.

Scope: the triple-to-color map is fixed per setup. The same triple shows the same tint on the same calibrated screen. Move to a new screen or new color space and the same numbers can look shifted unless the pipeline maps them across.

2.3.4 Worked Examples

Example 1 — 3 by 3 gray patch with levels 0 to 7. Top row reads 0, 0, 0. Middle row reads 7, 0, 0. Bottom row reads 0, 0, 0. Each of the nine cells holds one real brightness, so this is the \(f(x, y)\) map in miniature. The single 7 marks one bright spot on a dark field. Sense-check: nine seats, nine numbers, one channel.

Example 2 — same layout with three sheets, each keeping its own 0 to 7 steps. One spot holds 1 on red, 0 on green, 1 on blue, so its triple is \((1, 0, 1)\): dark with a magenta lean. A nearby spot holds 7 on one sheet with mixed small steps such as 5, 1, 1, 1 across the rest, so its merged triple reads bright on that sheet and dim elsewhere. Copy one gray sheet three times and the frame looks gray because all triples sit on the diagonal \((v, v, v)\). Shift the sheets apart and color appears because triples leave the diagonal.

The brightness-channels story is now concrete: gray is one function \(f(x, y)\), color is three stacked functions \(f_R(x, y)\), \(f_G(x, y)\), \(f_B(x, y)\).

2.3.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: A gray camera records only black and white steps. How do those steps map? And can a gray frame grow back into true color? A: A gray camera writes one 2D sheet, for example 128 by 128 by 1. A color camera writes three sheets, for example 128 by 128 by 3. Moving from color to gray drops facts, so the trip back is a guess. One plain path copies the gray value onto all three sheets, which gives gray, not true color: a gray 120 becomes \((120, 120, 120)\). Smart fill-in methods guess the missing color per spot from nearby data. Color filters that restyle photos use related math, but those filters get their own later topic.

Color to gray is many-to-one: many triples land on the same gray step, so the reverse has no single answer. That is why "colorize this gray photo" always guesses.

Exam note: gray is \(f(x, y)\) with \(f:\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\) on one sheet, color is three such sheets with \(256^3 = 16777216\) total picks, and white peaks at 255 while black sinks at 0. Next the optics that feed this function: pinholes, lenses, and wait time.

Real-world: phone galleries store color as three 0-to-255 sheets per frame, while fast training loops convert to one gray sheet to cut the math to a third before testing ideas.

2.4 Pinhole Cameras, Lenses, and Exposure

2.4.1 The Pinhole and the Upside-Down Picture

What if a camera were just a dark box with one tiny hole? That is a pinhole camera, and it still teaches the core optics.

A pinhole camera (dark box plus one tiny hole that maps each scene ray to one wall spot) draws an upside-down picture because rays cross at the hole.

Light from the scene meets at that hole, then lands on the back wall. The top of the scene lands low on the wall and the bottom lands high, so the back-wall picture comes out upside down. Eyes do the same: rays meet near the front of the eye and land on the back layer flipped. The brain turns that flipped pattern upright. Camera circuits do their own flip during the analog-to-digital pass plus a turn step, so a stored digital frame reads upright even though the raw optics invert it.

The meet point has a name. The focal point is the spot where rays join, written with no new symbol here. The focal length is the gap from that point to the final image plane, often written \(d\) or \(f\) in data sheets. Shift the back wall and that gap shifts too, which changes size: a longer gap spreads the same scene wider on the wall.

2.4.2 Calibration Keeps Measurements Honest

Real projects must aim each camera with care because later math trusts pixel addresses as true directions.

Two setup angles matter. Translation is a shift in place, for example 1 cm left. Rotation is a turn in aim, for example 2 degrees down. Data sheets for good vision sets list the focal point and the mount pose for this reason.

A driver-aid system shows the cost of sloppiness. A one-centimeter mount error can move a sensed lane edge far off road at distance, so the car steers wrong. That is why later work makes features steady against small shifts and turns rather than trusting raw addresses.

2.4.3 Augmentation Teaches Invariance

Training pipelines warp input frames on purpose to mint "more views of the same thing."

Common warps are normalization (shift steps to a fixed span such as 0 to 1), rotation (turn the grid), scaling (grow or shrink the grid), zooming (crop and grow a part), and random noise (add small jitters per cell). The plain-English goal is more views of the same thing from one stored frame.

A model trained only on upright dogs may fail on a lying dog. A model trained only on wide frames may fail on tall frames. Mixed warps fight those biases. The model learns that a dog stays a dog when turned or resized. In short terms the model gains rotation and shift invariance: small turns and moves of the input leave the label fixed.

Assumption: warps help only when they match real test shifts. Train with turns up to 20 degrees and the model handles tilted phones. Test with upside-down flips it never saw and it still fails. Match the warp set to the world the rig will meet.

2.4.4 Why Lenses Beat Pinholes

If a pinhole draws a sharp map, why add glass? Because a bare hole starves the sensor.

A bare hole passes very little light at once. Only a thin beam gets through per instant. The fix is patience: leave the hole open longer and the frame slowly builds. Star and night shots use this long wait because returned light is faint.

A lens gathers beams from many angles at once. It bends them onto the sensor together. The same scene then needs far less wait.

A phone shoots the same lamp at two waits. At 1/1000 of a second little light enters, so seats read near 20 to 40: dark. At 1/10 of a second about 100 times more light enters, since \((1/10) / (1/1000) = 100\), so the same seats read near 200 to 230: bright. The scene never changed; only collected light did. Sense-check: a 100-times wait gives about a 100-times reading until the 255 cap clips it.

So lenses cut exposure time (wait needed to collect enough light), written \(T\) in seconds. The stored numbers end up the same either way given enough wait. The trade is only time and sharpness, which is why modern vision kits use glass rather than bare holes.

The pinhole-sharpness doubt resolves here: a pinhole looks sharper in theory because each wall spot takes one ray, yet glass wins in practice by gathering many beams and cutting wait from seconds to milliseconds. That trigger plus correction is the teaching point to keep.

Real-world: phone night modes stack longer waits to mimic what a bigger lens gathers in one short snap.

2.4.5 How Bright Can a Spot Get

At the math level each spot \(f(x, y)\) can range from \(0\) up without bound:

\[ 0 \le f(x, y) \le \infty \]

Here \(f(x, y)\) is the sensed brightness at \((x, y)\) and \(\infty\) means no preset top. The reason is physics: radiated energy spans a vast band, described in the room as \(10^{-16}\) up to \(10^{16}\), from far infrared through ultraviolet. Real sensors clip this range to steps such as 0 to 255. The math form does not clip; the hardware does.

Picture the axis: sensor response on the horizontal axis from starlight to sunlight, stored step on the vertical axis from 0 to 255. The curve climbs, then flattens at 255. Everything past the bend stores as 255. That flat top is where later point maps must choose cap versus widen.

2.4.6 Student Questions and Answers

Q: We warp frames with zoom, turn, and resize to gain shift and size robustness. Do we also train for aspect-ratio changes, like a squashed person frame? People cannot read those well either. A: Yes. Training mixes frames at many width-to-height pairs such as 4:3, 16:9, and 1:1, plus tall pairs like 3:4, to cover squashed and stretched people. Detection heads also use anchor boxes of many shapes and sizes, which adds more shape cover: each anchor is one preset box shape the detector tests at each seat. Padding is another tool: a frame that is not 256 by 256 can gain side pads until it fits. Later topics take up shift-proof matching in full.

The ratio list above gives the standard training pairs; the exact spoken pairs were unclear, so standard wide, square, and tall pairs plus anchor-box cover are stated.

Q: Why use glass at all? A pinhole draws a sharper map, and a bare lens has no shutter tricks by itself. A: Sharpness is not the full story. A pinhole passes one thin beam per instant, so the wait grows long: seconds for a dim room, minutes for stars. Glass bends many beams onto the sensor at once, so the wait shrinks to milliseconds. User ideas such as parallel beams, focal control, shutter speed, light dose, zoom, and back-wall shift each hold some truth about shaping rays. The core win is light gathered per second. More light per second means a usable frame sooner.

Q: A pinhole frame can be written as a matrix over \(x\) and \(y\). Does a lens frame use different math? At one time \(t\) the lens seems to take in many returns at once. A: The matrix math stays the same. A pinhole at time \(t\) lets few rays through its dot opening, so its frame looks darker and poorer for the same \(t\). A lens lets many rays through, so the same scene reads brighter sooner. Only the wait changes. Given a long enough wait, both paths reach the same brightness map. Widen the opening and more facts arrive. Lengthen the wait and more facts arrive. Both knobs feed the same \(M\)-by-\(N\) grid.

Exam note: expect reasoning prompts on why augmentation warps build rotation and shift robustness (upright-dog and wide-versus-tall traps), and why lens speed matters for real capture (1/1000 dark versus 1/10 bright with pinhole wait versus lens gather).

Real-world: driver-aid rigs log focal length plus mount translation and rotation per camera, then train with turns, scales, pads, and mixed aspect pairs so lane lines still read after small bumps.

2.5 Illumination and Reflectance Model

2.5.1 Splitting Light In from Light Back

Why does one object look new under new light? Because each pixel mixes two facts: light landing and share sent back.

An illumination-reflectance split (brightness factored into landing light times returned share) reads \(f(x, y) = i(x, y)\, r(x, y)\).

Here \(i(x, y)\) is the illumination (light landing at \((x, y)\)), \(r(x, y)\) is the reflectance (share sent back at \((x, y)\)), and \(f(x, y)\) is the sensed brightness at that spot. The full form is:

\[ f(x, y) = i(x, y)\, r(x, y) \]

The lecture phrase for this was "incident radiation multiplied with the refractive index," which maps to incident light \(i\) times return share \(r\). Work a tiny case: if \(i = 200\) units land and the surface returns half, so \(r = 0.5\), then \(f = 200 \times 0.5 = 100\). Double the lamp to \(i = 400\) with the same surface and \(f = 400 \times 0.5 = 200\). Same object, new light, new pixel.

For shape checking: \(i\) carries light units, \(r\) is unit-free between 0 and 1, so \(f\) carries light units. The product keeps the right units, which a sum would break.

2.5.2 Why Water Looks Blue Under White Sun

Sunlight looks white, yet the sea looks blue and leaves look green and the sky looks blue. Each surface keeps some bands and drinks in the rest.

Ocean water drinks in much of the red and green bands and sends back blue, so \(r\) peaks in blue. Leaves drink in other bands and send back green, so \(r\) peaks in green. That split is why the product form helps: change the light \(i\) and the same object can look new (white sun versus orange sunset on the same sea), and change the surface \(r\) and the same light can look new (blue sea versus green leaf under the same sun).

The everyday analogy is two stage lights on one white shirt. Swap the lamp color and the shirt looks new even though the shirt never changed. Swap the shirt for a red one under the same lamp and it looks new again. Light and cloth multiply like \(i\) and \(r\). Where the analogy breaks: real \(r\) shifts with view angle and wetness, while a shirt color stays put.

2.5.3 Bounds on Each Part

Incoming light has no preset top:

\[ 0 \le i(x, y) \le \infty \]

The return share sits between none and all:

\[ 0 \le r(x, y) \le 1 \]

Here \(0\) for \(r\) means full uptake, which reads black. One demo named Vantablack, a coating near 99 percent uptake with \(r \approx 0.01\), as the darkest black. Here \(1\) means full return, like a mirror-like sheet that sends back near all light. Each \((x, y)\) spot has its own \(i\) and \(r\) pair, so shadows are low-\(i\) zones while black paint is low-\(r\) zones. Two dark pixels can share the same \(f\) for opposite reasons, which later normalization tries to undo.

Scope: the product split assumes one bounce per ray. It fits matte prints and calm water well. It strains for glass, mirrors, and haze, where many bounces and scatter mix into each pixel and need richer models from later chapters.

2.5.4 Machine Limits on Stored Brightness

Theory allows any brightness. Hardware does not. Each setup has a low cap \(L_{\min}\) and a high cap \(L_{\max}\). The stored level \(L\) obeys:

\[ L_{\min} \le L \le L_{\max} \]

Here \(L_{\min}\) is the smallest storable step and \(L_{\max}\) is the largest. For a gray digital frame the working span is \(0\) to \(L - 1\), where \(L\) is the level count. So an 8-bit setup with \(L = 256\) spans \(0\) to \(255\). The same unbounded \(f(x, y)\) from 2.4 now meets a hard box: values outside the box must be clipped to the edge or restacked into a wider box such as 16-bit.

Tiny map: \(f = 300\) on an 8-bit box stores as 255 under clipping, while on a 16-bit box it stores as 300 with room to spare. Pick the box before the shoot, not after.

Brightness splits into landing light times returned share with \(0 \le i \le \infty\) and \(0 \le r \le 1\), then hardware boxes it into \(L_{\min} \le L \le L_{\max}\). Next that boxed grid gets pinned in place and in tone: sampling plus quantization.

Real-world: print rooms shoot color charts under fixed lamps to pin \(i\), so the leftover shifts in \(f\) read as true surface \(r\) for inks and fabrics.

2.6 Sampling and Quantization

2.6.1 Sampling Pins the Scene to a Grid

What turns an open scene into a fixed table? Sampling.

Sampling (spatial pinning that writes a vast scene onto a fixed \(M\)-by-\(N\) grid) sets cell size, not tone. Here \(M\) is the row count and \(N\) is the column count.

The scene in front of a viewer has no fixed size. A shore view runs wide in each direction. Sampling forces that open view into cells. A 720 by 720 frame and a 128 by 128 frame are both samples of such scenes. Only the cell size differs: small cells hold local detail, big cells mix detail together.

Once the grid is set, each cell holds one spot reading. That is the whole step. No new facts enter later unless the grid itself grows. Upscaling later invents between-cell guesses; it never recovers the dropped facts.

2.6.2 Quantization Pins Each Reading to a Step

Quantization (brightness pinning that rounds each smooth reading to a fixed step) sets tone, not place. It rounds each smooth reading to a fixed step. A reading like 12.8 becomes 12. That rounding is called intensity pinning.

The step count comes from the bit depth. Let \(n\) be the bit count. Then the span is:

\[ 0 \text{ to } 2^{n} - 1 \]

So 8 bits give \(0\) to \(255\) since \(2^{8} - 1 = 255\). Three bits give \(0\) to \(7\), since \(2^{3} - 1 = 7\). Four bits give \(0\) to \(15\), since \(2^{4} - 1 = 15\). The \(L - 1\) top from 2.5 is this same rule with \(L = 2^{n}\). Check units: \(n\) is bits, \(2^{n}\) is level count \(L\), and each \(r\) is one integer step in that span.

The everyday analogy is a ruler with only marked ticks. Sampling picks where to drop the ruler. Quantization snaps each reading to the nearest tick. Fine ticks cut rounding harm. Where the analogy breaks: pixels also average light over a small area rather than probing one point, so a thin line can vanish even on a fine ruler.

Scope: quantization harm shows as banding on smooth ramps, not as block edges. If edges look like stairs, the grid is too coarse. If skies look like stacked rings, the steps are too few. Fix the right knob.

2.6.3 Worked Example: A 1D Slice in 20 Blocks

Draw a straight cut A to B across a frame and read brightness along that cut. On the smooth trace the white end hovers near the top of the box: readings such as 250, 254, 255 with sensor wiggles. Values at or past 255 clip to 255 on an 8-bit box, so any spoken 256 means "past the top, pinned to 255." Then the trace dives straight to black at 0, climbs through gray slopes such as 40, 90, 150, and rises back toward white near 240. Now pin that smooth trace into 20 blocks with one stored step per block. A full 2D frame repeats this cut row after row across \(x\) and \(y\). Sense-check: 20 blocks hold 20 numbers, so narrow dips thinner than one block vanish.

Partial cover needs a rule for mixed blocks. If 90 percent of a block is white, store white. If about 60 percent is white, still store white in the coarse demo. If 90 percent is black or gray, store that dark step. Those rules look like max pooling (keep the peak value in the block) and average pooling (keep the mean of the block). The demo used them only as handy labels for block votes, not as trained layers.

The line A-to-B plus 20-block pinning is the sampling-plus-quantization flow event to keep: smooth 1D signal in, 20 stepped blocks out.

2.6.4 Finer Grids Cure Blockiness

A coarse grid looks blocky. Edges turn into stairs. Ask how to fix it and the answer is plain: split cells finer. A white block that hid a gray sliver now shows white, gray-white, and gray in three child cells.

Video quality shows the same knob. A YouTube clip at 320p looks blocky. At 480p it reads better. At 720p it reads well. At 1080p, 2040p, 4K, and 8K it keeps gaining crispness. Each label is the same picture at a new sample rate: same scene, more sampling points.

Cost sets the ceiling. Finer grids need sensors that can feed them plus chips that can carry them. That is why many daily setups stay near 720p. Glass alone cannot fake missing cells.

Q: Does a bigger lens mean more sharpness by math? A: No. A big lens gathers more beams, but the sensor grid stays put. A huge lens on a small-sensor body still writes a small grid. A 4K feed shown on a 320p panel still lands in small fixed blocks. So the stored frame stays blocky. Sharpness follows the grid plus the brightness steps, not glass size alone. This rejects the bigger-glass-means-sharper claim: light gather grows, sample count does not.

2.6.5 Quantization Sets Color Richness

Finer grids fix block edges. Finer steps fix tone jumps. Terms like 8K image and true blacks point at step depth: more steps per sheet hold deeper darks without banding. A span of \(0\) to \(255\) gives 256 tones. A 16-bit span gives \(2^{16} = 65536\) tones per sheet since \(2^{10} = 1024\) and \(2^{16} = 65536\). More steps mean smoother shade ramps and deeper darks.

So 4K and 8K labels mix both knobs in ads. Grid size sets edge crispness. Step depth sets tone richness. Both shape how bright, deep, and sharp a frame feels. Read the spec for both numbers before judging.

2.6.6 Notebook Demo to Replay

Start with a 5 by 5 gray grid spanning \(0\) to \(255\), where \(0\) is full black and \(255\) is full white. A linear fill gives mixed black, gray, and white cells. Coarse sampling turns that smooth fill highly blocky: each big cell averages a wide patch. Then shrink an 80 by 80 fill down toward 8 by 8 and 10 by 10. Detail drains away at each shrink: 6400 cells down to 64 or 100 cells. A binary frame with \(2^{1} = 2\) levels (only \(0\) and \(1\)) looks harshest. A gray frame with a few steps looks banded. A color frame with more sheets holds up best because three sheets carry more facts per seat. Sense-check: fewer cells plus fewer steps always loses facts; the only question is which facts the task can spare.

The task is to replay those dials: shift sample rates, shift step counts, watch blockiness come and go, and judge how much fact a pipeline truly needs. That drill links straight to the 128 by 128 training crops later.

2.6.7 Hardware Picks the Working Point

No single grid fits all jobs. Car screens and driver-aid boxes live near the low end to meet cost and heat limits. Drones sit near the low to mid range to save battery and bandwidth. Medical rigs sit high to hold fine vessels. Plant rigs sit high too to catch hairline cracks. A high-end Bosch HD box near 1080p pairs with a graphics unit that can carry the load.

Video heaviness bends the same way. The first move is to shrink the frame to a set size such as 128 by 128, 32 by 32, or 256 by 256. Pad short sides. Trim long sides. That reset is sampling in work clothes.

Scans bend the step knob instead. A 7-tesla MRI can span values near \(10^{7}\), and CT scans run wide too. An 8-bit screen cannot show that raw span, so the pipeline maps it down to \(0\) to \(255\) with a window choice. A 16-bit path keeps more steps. Uses named were sharper views, stronger contrast, and less blockiness, all from these two plain dials.

Exam note: sampling pins place onto \(M\)-by-\(N\) while quantization pins tone onto \(0\)-to-\(2^{n}-1\), shown by the A-to-B cut in 20 blocks with max versus average pooling votes. Next these two pins get named as resolutions with hard numbers.

Real-world: car infotainment and driver-aid boxes ship with small grids, while MRI suites and plant rigs pay for deep steps and wide grids, and Canon small-sensor bodies show why glass alone cannot add cells.

2.7 Resolution: Spatial and Intensity

2.7.1 The Matrix Address System

A sampled frame is an \(M\) by \(N\) matrix. Spots run from \((0, 0)\) at one corner to \((M - 1, N - 1)\) at the far corner. The middle sits near \((M/2, N/2)\), where \(M\) and \(N\) are the side lengths. Each address holds one step value.

That address grid unlocks math. Once each spot has a fixed seat and number, whole-frame sums, shifts, and filters turn legal. The seat \((x, y)\) always maps to the same neighbor seats, which is what lets 2.8 define rings and 2.9 define flips as pure index moves.

Think of street addresses on a planned grid. House \((0,0)\) sits at the corner lot. House \((M-1, N-1)\) sits at the far lot. Mail (filters) can route because every house number is fixed.

2.7.2 Spatial Resolution and DPI

Spatial resolution (sampling density that marks the smallest detail a frame can still show) has a print-shop twin: DPI.

DPI (dots per inch that count print dots along one inch) plays the same role as pixels per inch in digital frames. More dots per inch means finer detail.

A watch face is printed at four densities. At 930 DPI each mark reads crisp, including small 55 and 60 ticks. At 300 DPI much sharpness drains though 55 and 60 still read. At 150 DPI stairs creep along curves and the arm shows blocks. At 72 DPI the marks smear past reading. The ratios tell the cost story: \(930 / 300 \approx 3.1\), then halves to 150 and 72. Each cut eats facts in plain sight. Sense-check: a 3-times cut keeps large marks but drops hairlines first.

So cutting the sample rate by thirds, then halves, eats facts in plain sight. Print dots and digital pixels follow the same rule: density sets the smallest readable mark.

2.7.3 Intensity Resolution and Tone Steps

Intensity resolution (step depth that sets how many gray tones a frame can show) flattens tones when cut. A full 8-bit frame holds 256 levels.

The same watch face is re-rendered at five tone depths. At 256 levels the gray ramp reads smooth. At 128 levels some gray bands start to wash out. At 64 levels broad zones turn flat though the layout still reads. At 16 levels rings (halos) show at borders where smooth ramps snap into bands. At 2 levels only black and white stay, and most facts leave. The bit math is \(2^{8} = 256\), \(2^{7} = 128\), \(2^{6} = 64\), \(2^{4} = 16\), \(2^{1} = 2\). Note the fix: 128 levels need 7 bits, not 6, since \(2^{6} = 64\). Sense-check: each lost bit halves the tone count, so banding jumps fastest at the low end.

Usable cutoffs sit below the top. A 300 DPI frame can still drive a good model where 930 DPI would cost too much. A 32-level tone map (\(2^{5} = 32\)) can still show the key marks where 256 levels would cost too much. The skill is to stop where facts still suffice: keep the cheapest density and depth that hold the marks the task needs. Top density costs too much while lower density still drives good models where facts suffice.

Human eyes add their own cap. Most viewers cannot rank high-definition, ultra-high-definition, 2K, 4K, and 8K shots in a blind look. Past about 720p with full color steps, most scenes look the same to people. Models may still gain from more, but eyes plateau — so spend extra pixels for machines, not for viewers.

Scope: the 300-DPI and 32-level cutoffs are task-specific, not universal. Fine-print reading needs more density than face detection. Keep the cutoff tied to the smallest mark the task must read.

2.7.4 One-to-One Color Maps

Each stored triple maps to one shown color. A triple near \((196, 229, 255)\) shows one fixed pale tint: high blue, high green, mid red. All 16 million triples at 8 bits have their own fixed seats since \(256^3 = 16777216\). The sensor vote picks the seat. The screen replays it. That is the full loop from photon to pixel to eye:

\[ \text{photon} \to \text{bucket vote} \to \text{triple seat} \to \text{screen tint} \]

No depth hides here; this is a pure tone map from stored numbers to shown light.

2.7.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: What is DPI? A: Dots per inch. It counts print dots along one inch. More dots mean finer print detail. It matches pixel density in digital frames: 930 DPI crisp marks versus 72 DPI smears on the same watch face.

A second question connected tone steps to sensor sensitivity:

Q: Is intensity resolution the same as radiometric resolution, the return share a sensor can still sense? A: Yes, they line up one to one. Returned light is \(i\) times \(r\) at each spot. The sensor maps that smooth product onto fixed steps by closest match, a voting step like the Bayer vote. So the sensed share and the stored step move together: finer radiometric sense needs finer intensity steps, else small light shifts round to the same stored number.

Exam note: spatial density (930 down to 72 DPI on the watch) and tone depth (256 down to 2 with halos at 16, and \(2^{7} = 128\) versus \(2^{6} = 64\)) are both cost-versus-fact knobs. Next the local shapes built on this grid: gaps and neighbor rings.

Real-world: print shops read DPI the way digital teams read pixels, while scan teams keep 256 tones for eyes but train models on 32 tones to save compute.

2.8 Pixels, Distance, and Neighborhoods

2.8.1 An Image Is a Sampled and Pinned Map

A digital image is not a smooth sheet of paint; it is a rigid grid of pinned numerical values. Sampling pins space into discrete row and column coordinates. Quantization pins brightness into discrete integer levels. Nothing about the stored frame stays smooth or continuous. Each grid cell holds exactly one fixed integer step.

A digital pixel (the smallest discrete picture element addressed on a coordinate grid), located at integer coordinate pair \((x, y)\), holds an integer tone value \(r \in \{0, 1, \dots, L - 1\}\).

Think of a mosaic tiled with uniform square ceramic pieces. From across the room, the wall portrays a seamless face. Step closer, and the illusion dissolves into individual square tiles, each glazed with a single uniform color. In digital vision, every image \(f(x, y)\) is such a tile mosaic. Here \(x \in \{0, 1, \dots, M - 1\}\) indexes the row, \(y \in \{0, 1, \dots, N - 1\}\) indexes the column, and \(L\) is the total number of allowable intensity steps (such as \(L = 256\) for 8-bit storage). That discrete matrix is the foundation for all spatial reasoning.

Where this analogy breaks: ceramic tiles are physical objects with mortar lines in between, whereas digital image pixels abut without physical gaps. The numeric values stored at each address define the entirety of the visual information.

2.8.2 Measuring Gaps Between Spots

Because pixels reside on an integer grid, calculating the distance between two points requires choosing an appropriate spatial metric. For points \(p = (x, y)\), \(q = (s, t)\), and \(z = (u, v)\), any valid mathematical metric \(D(p, q)\) must satisfy four foundational properties:

  1. Non-negativity: \(D(p, q) \ge 0\)
  2. Identity: \(D(p, q) = 0 \iff p = q\)
  3. Symmetry: \(D(p, q) = D(q, p)\)
  4. Triangle inequality: \(D(p, z) \le D(p, q) + D(q, z)\)

Three primary distance formulations govern digital grid measurements, alongside a specialized code metric:

The three primary digital spatial metrics between pixel \(p = (x, y)\) and pixel \(q = (s, t)\) are:

  1. Euclidean Distance (\(D_e\)): The straight-line physical span:

\[ D_e(p, q) = \sqrt{(x - s)^2 + (y - t)^2} \]

  1. City-Block / Manhattan Distance (\(D_4\)): The shortest path restricted strictly to horizontal and vertical steps:

\[ D_4(p, q) = |x - s| + |y - t| \]

  1. Chessboard / Chebyshev Distance (\(D_8\)): The minimal number of king moves on a chessboard, allowing horizontal, vertical, and diagonal steps:

\[ D_8(p, q) = \max(|x - s|, |y - t|) \]

  1. Hamming Distance (\(D_H\)): For binary feature descriptors, the number of bit positions where two codes differ:

\[ D_H(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \sum_{k=1}^B (\mathbf{a}_k \oplus \mathbf{b}_k) \] where \(\oplus\) denotes the exclusive-OR (XOR) operation across \(B\) bits.

Each metric forms a characteristic contour of equidistant points around the origin:

  • Euclidean distance contours form smooth circles of radius \(r\).
  • City-block distance contours form diamonds (\(45^\circ\)-rotated squares) with vertices along the coordinate axes.
  • Chessboard distance contours form axis-aligned squares centered on the pixel.

Calculate the distances between pixel \(p = (2, 3)\) and pixel \(q = (5, 7)\) across all three spatial metrics.

Step 1: Compute coordinate offsets. \[ \Delta x = |x - s| = |2 - 5| = 3, \quad \Delta y = |y - t| = |3 - 7| = 4 \]

Step 2: Euclidean distance. \[ D_e(p, q) = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = \mathbf{5.0} \]

Step 3: City-block distance. \[ D_4(p, q) = 3 + 4 = \mathbf{7} \]

Step 4: Chessboard distance. \[ D_8(p, q) = \max(3, 4) = \mathbf{4} \]

Sense-check: Notice the ordering \(D_8(p, q) \le D_e(p, q) \le D_4(p, q)\), giving \(4 \le 5 \le 7\). This must always hold because diagonal moves provide a shortcut over horizontal-plus-vertical paths, while Euclidean distance is the straight-line lower bound for physical movement.

Scope & Assumptions: Euclidean distance yields continuous real numbers (\(\mathbb{R}\)), which rarely coincide with integer grid steps. City-block and chessboard distances always produce integers on discrete lattices, making them computationally cheaper and naturally aligned with pixel traversal algorithms.

2.8.3 Four and Eight Neighbors

Spatial filtering, object segmentation, and edge detection rely on grouping pixels that surround a central coordinate \(p = (x, y)\).

Given central pixel \(p = (x, y)\):

  1. 4-Neighborhood (\(N_4(p)\)): The four orthogonal neighbors sharing an edge with \(p\) at distance \(D_4 = 1\):

\[ N_4(p) = \{(x+1, y), (x-1, y), (x, y+1), (x, y-1)\} \]

  1. Diagonal Neighbors (\(N_D(p)\)): The four diagonal neighbors sharing only a corner point with \(p\):

\[ N_D(p) = \{(x+1, y+1), (x+1, y-1), (x-1, y+1), (x-1, y-1)\} \]

  1. 8-Neighborhood (\(N_8(p)\)): The complete union of orthogonal and diagonal neighbors enclosing \(p\) at distance \(D_8 = 1\):

\[ N_8(p) = N_4(p) \cup N_D(p) \]

The 4-neighborhood forms an axis-aligned cross centered at \(p\). The 8-neighborhood expands this cross into a full \(3 \times 3\) square ring containing eight outer pixels.

These neighbor groupings define the support region for spatial convolution and cross-correlation filters. A general \(3 \times 3\) discrete spatial convolution over the 8-neighborhood evaluates:

\[ g(x, y) = (w * f)(x, y) = \sum_{s=-1}^{1} \sum_{t=-1}^{1} w(s, t)\, f(x - s, y - t) \]

where \(w(s, t)\) represents the filter kernel weights and \(f(x - s, y - t)\) indexes the neighborhood intensities with inverted spatial coordinates.

Evaluate a small spatial averaging sum over the 8-neighborhood for central pixel \(p = (1, 1)\) inside a \(3 \times 3\) image patch:

\[ f = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 20 & 30 \\ 40 & \mathbf{50} & 60 \\ 70 & 80 & 90 \end{bmatrix} \]

Step 1: Identify the neighbor sets.

  • Orthogonal 4-neighbors \(N_4(p)\): top \(20\), bottom \(80\), left \(40\), right \(60\).
  • Diagonal neighbors \(N_D(p)\): top-left \(10\), top-right \(30\), bottom-left \(70\), bottom-right \(90\).
  • Central pixel: \(f(1, 1) = 50\).

Step 2: Compute orthogonal neighbor sum. \[ S_4 = 20 + 80 + 40 + 60 = 200 \]

Step 3: Compute full 8-neighbor sum (excluding center). \[ S_8 = S_4 + 10 + 30 + 70 + 90 = 200 + 200 = 400 \]

Step 4: Compute \(3 \times 3\) mean filter response. Including the central pixel: \[ g(1, 1) = \frac{1}{9} \left(400 + 50\right) = \frac{450}{9} = \mathbf{50} \]

Sense-check: The patch possesses point symmetry centered on \(50\). The computed mean of exactly \(\mathbf{50}\) confirms algebraic symmetry across all eight neighbors.

Common Pitfall: Border pixels on an \(M \times N\) image (at \(x = 0\), \(x = M-1\), \(y = 0\), or \(y = N-1\)) lack a full complement of 4 or 8 neighbors. Applying neighborhood operations without boundary handling (such as zero-padding, border replication, or symmetric reflection) causes indexing faults or edge distortions.

2.8.4 Abstract but Often Tested

Metric definitions and neighborhood rings frequently feel abstract on first encounter, but they serve as the mechanical foundation for image filtering, mathematical morphology, and connected-component analysis.

Exam note: Practice drawing the cross pattern for \(N_4(p)\) and the square ring for \(N_8(p)\). Exam problems routinely provide coordinate pairs to compute \(D_e\), \(D_4\), and \(D_8\), or specify a small numerical grid to compute convolution or correlation sums over 4-neighbor crosses and 8-neighbor square rings. Always verify that \(D_8 \le D_e \le D_4\).

Real-world: Robotic path planners deploy city-block distance for warehouse floor navigation restricted to orthogonal grid tracks, while image compression engines utilize Hamming distance over binary descriptors to match feature keypoints in real time.

2.9 Brightness, Flipping, and Linear Point Operations

2.9.1 Adding a Flat Lift

How does an editing app brighten a dark image with a single slider flick? Behind the user interface sits an elementary algebraic transformation applied across every stored matrix cell.

A linear point operation (a transformation where the output value at coordinate \((x, y)\) depends strictly on the input value at the exact same coordinate) modifies pixel intensities without shifting spatial positions:

\[ g(x, y) = a \cdot f(x, y) + b \]

where \(a > 0\) is the gain (controlling contrast), \(b \in \mathbb{R}\) is the additive bias (controlling brightness), \(f(x, y)\) is the input image, and \(g(x, y)\) is the processed output image.

When the gain is set to unity (\(a = 1\)), the relationship reduces to a flat brightness lift:

\[ G(x, y) = f(x, y) + C \]

where \(C\) is a global scalar constant. If \(C > 0\), each pixel shifts toward higher numerical values, brightening the overall frame. For an 8-bit grayscale image where \(0\) represents pure black and \(255\) represents pure white, setting \(C = +20\) raises every pixel by \(20\) intensity units. Conversely, setting \(C < 0\) dims the image toward black.

Consider four sample pixel intensities from a grayscale image: \(f_1 = 15\) (shadow), \(f_2 = 110\) (mid-tone), \(f_3 = 230\) (bright sky), and \(f_4 = 245\) (specular highlight). Apply a flat lift of \(C = +20\) under standard 8-bit bounds.

Step 1: Compute raw additions.

  • \(g_1 = 15 + 20 = \mathbf{35}\)
  • \(g_2 = 110 + 20 = \mathbf{130}\)
  • \(g_3 = 230 + 20 = \mathbf{250}\)
  • \(g_4 = 245 + 20 = 265\)

Step 2: Apply boundary saturation. Because 8-bit storage cannot exceed \(255\), clamp \(g_4\) to the maximum displayable ceiling: \[ g_4 = \min(265, 255) = \mathbf{255} \]

Sense-check: Every pixel gained \(20\) units of brightness except \(f_4\), which saturated at the upper rail (\(255\)), demonstrating how aggressive positive offsets compress dynamic range near the white boundary.

2.9.2 Worked Example: Flip Across an Axis

While point operations adjust pixel intensities on a fixed lattice, geometric transformations rearrange pixel coordinates. A horizontal flip reflects the image across its vertical midline.

For an image of size \(M \times N\), a horizontal reflection (mirroring along the vertical axis) maps column index \(y \in \{0, 1, \dots, N - 1\}\) to \(N - 1 - y\):

\[ g(x, y) = f(x, N - 1 - y) \]

while a vertical reflection (mirroring along the horizontal axis) maps row index \(x \in \{0, 1, \dots, M - 1\}\) to \(M - 1 - x\):

\[ g(x, y) = f(M - 1 - x, y) \]

Consider a tiny \(3 \times 3\) image patch (\(M = 3, N = 3\)) represented as a row-major sequence of nine pixel values: \([20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100]\). Express this patch as a matrix and execute a horizontal mirror reflection across the vertical midline.

Step 1: Arrange into matrix rows. \[ f = \begin{bmatrix} 20 & 30 & 40 \\ 50 & 60 & 70 \\ 80 & 90 & 100 \end{bmatrix} \] Here each row \(x \in \{0, 1, 2\}\) contains three column cells indexed by \(y \in \{0, 1, 2\}\).

Step 2: Apply column reversal \(y' = 2 - y\).

  • Column \(0\) swaps with column \(2 - 0 = 2\).
  • Column \(1\) maps to \(2 - 1 = 1\) (remains in place).
  • Column \(2\) swaps with column \(2 - 2 = 0\).

Applying this index swap across every row:

  • Row 0: \([20, 30, 40] \to [\mathbf{40}, \mathbf{30}, \mathbf{20}]\)
  • Row 1: \([50, 60, 70] \to [\mathbf{70}, \mathbf{60}, \mathbf{50}]\)
  • Row 2: \([80, 90, 100] \to [\mathbf{100}, \mathbf{90}, \mathbf{80}]\)

Step 3: Construct the reflected matrix. \[ g = \begin{bmatrix} 40 & 30 & 20 \\ 70 & 60 & 50 \\ 100 & 90 & 80 \end{bmatrix} \] Flattened in row-major order, the flipped sequence reads: \[ [\mathbf{40}, \mathbf{30}, \mathbf{20}, \mathbf{70}, \mathbf{60}, \mathbf{50}, \mathbf{100}, \mathbf{90}, \mathbf{80}] \]

Sense-check: The central column \([30, 60, 90]^T\) remained entirely invariant, while the outer boundary columns precisely exchanged places, confirming accurate horizontal reflection.

Because digital images are stored as indexed matrices, any geometric manipulation—such as reflections, \(90^\circ\) rotations, and affine shearing—reduces to systematic coordinate permutation.

2.9.3 Color Lifts Act per Sheet

In full-color imagery, an image comprises three distinct two-dimensional arrays corresponding to the red, green, and blue color channels: \(f_R(x, y)\), \(f_G(x, y)\), and \(f_B(x, y)\).

Applying a uniform scalar offset \(C\) identically across all three channels brightens or darkens the image while preserving chromatic ratios: \[ g_R(x, y) = f_R(x, y) + C, \quad g_G(x, y) = f_G(x, y) + C, \quad g_B(x, y) = f_B(x, y) + C \] However, if unequal offsets are applied (\(C_R \ne C_G \ne C_B\)), the hue drifts systematically.

Consider a visual color patch initially sitting at a muted mauve tint \((R, G, B) = (140, 100, 120)\). If red alone is incremented by \(+5\) twice consecutively:

  1. First increment: \((145, 100, 120)\) shifts toward a warmer lilac tone.
  2. Second increment: \((150, 100, 120)\) produces a distinct pale lavender blush.

Color image processing operates as three simultaneous grayscale operations. Matched lifts maintain color balance, whereas asymmetric lifts induce deliberate tint adjustments.

2.9.4 Overflow: Cap or Widen

When mathematical operations push pixel values outside the supported range of the storage container, the system must invoke a clipping policy. On an 8-bit unsigned integer architecture, values are strictly constrained to \([0, 255]\).

Two architectural strategies resolve arithmetic overflow:

  1. Saturated Arithmetic (Clamping): Output values are hard-capped at the representable extrema:

\[ g(x, y) = \min(\max(f(x, y) + C, 0), 255) \] A computed sum of \(275\) is clamped and stored as \(255\). This mirrors a television screen brightness dial: once pushed past its maximum, the display panel saturates and shows no further tonal change.

  1. Bit-Depth Widening: The data container is promoted to a wider format, such as 16-bit unsigned integers (\([0, 65535]\)) or 32-bit floating-point numbers (\([0.0, 1.0]\)). This preserves the exact numerical sum of \(275\) without data truncation.

Common Pitfall (Blind Lifts): Applying an uncalibrated positive brightness lift causes severe information loss. If an original scene contains highlights at \(235\) and \(255\), they represent distinct surface textures. Applying a lift of \(C = +25\) pushes \(235 + 25 = 260 \to 255\) and \(255 + 25 = 280 \to 255\). Both textures collapse into identical flat white regions (\(255\)). Subtle structural boundaries vanish permanently.

2.9.5 Sampling Shifts Are Not Point Lifts

It is essential to distinguish operations that modify pixel values from operations that alter image geometry or resolution.

  • Point Operation: Modifies the stored tone value at existing coordinate \((x, y)\) without changing grid dimensions or spatial locations (\(g(x, y) = T[f(x, y)]\)).
  • Spatial Resampling: Re-interpolates the image onto an entirely new spatial lattice, altering matrix dimensions from \(M \times N\) to \(M' \times N'\).

The equation \(G(x, y) = f(x, y) + C\) assumes the coordinate grid is already established. It cannot synthesize new sampling coordinates. To convert a low-resolution \(128 \times 128\) image into a high-resolution \(512 \times 512\) grid, the vision engineer must first resample the spatial domain using nearest-neighbor, bilinear, or bicubic interpolation, and subsequently apply any desired radiometric point operations.

2.9.6 Any Matrix Math Is Fair Game

Representing images as numerical matrices makes the entire apparatus of linear algebra and discrete signal processing accessible:

  • Matrix addition and subtraction (background subtraction in surveillance video).
  • Matrix multiplication and inner products (template matching).
  • 2D spatial convolution and cross-correlation (edge detection and spatial blurring).

The choice of operations depends strictly on task requirements. If an inspection system requires exact spatial calibration, geometric flips must be prohibited. If an algorithm requires precise photometric measurements, uncalibrated brightness offsets must be avoided.

Real-world: Modern deep learning data loaders chain random brightness offsets, contrast adjustments, horizontal reflections, and affine warps into automated data augmentation pipelines, creating diverse training batches from a finite set of labeled photographs.

Q: For grayscale frames we add a single constant. For color frames, must we add the exact same lift to all three channels? A: Yes, if the goal is pure illumination adjustment without altering color balance. Adding an identical offset \(C\) to red, green, and blue brightens the scene while preserving chromatic ratios. Applying unequal offsets intentionally shifts the hue, as demonstrated when lifting red alone turned a neutral mauve swatch into lavender.

Student questions also explored hardware overflow constraints:

A third question clarified coordinate mapping versus tonal transformation:

Regarding spatial priors in specialized vision tasks:

Q: Should we skip lifts and flips when an object must stay at one true spot? A: Yes, skip what the task forbids. 2.10 Negative, Log, and Power-Law Transforms

2.10.1 Why Early Medical Work Needed Them

In the 1960s and 1970s, digital image processing emerged out of practical necessity in biomedical engineering and aerospace exploration, decades before specialized graphics processing units existed. Medical specialists faced an urgent diagnostic challenge: identifying malignant tumors in early breast radiographs (mammograms).

A malignant tumor (a dense, compact cellular growth that absorbs significant X-ray radiation) typically appears as a faint white cluster embedded amidst dark fibrous tissue, blood vessels, and fatty deposits.

On raw photographic film, subtle density variations within dark soft tissue blend together, rendering early-stage microcalcifications invisible to the naked eye. Because computation was constrained, engineers could not deploy multi-layer neural networks. Instead, they designed mathematically elegant, non-linear tonal transfer functions that selectively remap gray levels, expanding contrast precisely where diagnostic information resides.

2.10.2 Negative Frames

The simplest intensity inversion is the image negative, which mirrors the dynamic range across the tonal spectrum.

For an image with \(L\) intensity levels spanning integer range \([0, L - 1]\), the image negative transformation maps input intensity \(r\) to output intensity \(s\) via:

\[ s = (L - 1) - r \]

where \(r \in \{0, 1, \dots, L - 1\}\) is the input intensity and \(s \in \{0, 1, \dots, L - 1\}\) is the transformed output intensity.

For standard 8-bit quantization (\(L = 256\)), the mapping simplifies to \(s = 255 - r\). Minimum black (\(r = 0\)) maps to maximum white (\(s = 255 - 0 = 255\)), while peak white (\(r = 255\)) maps to pure black (\(s = 255 - 255 = 0\)).

A mammogram patch contains three critical diagnostic regions: surrounding adipose fatty tissue at \(r_1 = 15\) (dark field), vascular connective bands at \(r_2 = 45\), and an early microcalcification lesion at \(r_3 = 230\) (radiopaque highlight). Apply an 8-bit image negative transformation.

Step 1: Apply \(s = 255 - r\) to each region.

  • Adipose background: \(s_1 = 255 - 15 = \mathbf{240}\) (near-white)
  • Vascular bands: \(s_2 = 255 - 45 = \mathbf{210}\) (light gray)
  • Calcification lesion: \(s_3 = 255 - 230 = \mathbf{25}\) (deep black)

Sense-check: The predominant dark field (\(15\)) is converted into a bright background (\(240\)), while the dense white anomaly (\(230\)) becomes a conspicuous dark silhouette (\(25\)). Because human visual perception detects subtle dark details against a luminous white background far more effectively than faint light spots against pitch black, linear support vector machines and radiologist eyes flag the lesion with high sensitivity.

2.10.3 Log Boost for Dark Zones

When an image contains detail obscured in deep shadows alongside high-intensity specular highlights, a linear brightness lift fails because adding a constant washes out the highlights. The solution is a logarithmic transformation, which selectively expands dark tones while compressing bright tones.

The logarithmic transformation maps input intensity \(r \ge 0\) to output intensity \(s\) via:

\[ s = c \log(1 + r) \]

where \(r\) is the input intensity, \(c\) is a positive normalization scaling constant, and the offset \(+1\) ensures that \(\log(1) = 0\) when \(r = 0\).

To ensure the output spans the full displayable dynamic range \([0, L - 1]\), the constant \(c\) is derived by evaluating the boundary condition at maximum input intensity \(r_{\max} = L - 1\):

\[ L - 1 = c \log(1 + (L - 1)) \implies c = \frac{L - 1}{\log(L)} \]

For an 8-bit image (\(L = 256\)) using base-10 logarithms: \[ c = \frac{255}{\log_{10}(256)} \approx \frac{255}{2.40824} \approx 105.886 \]

The mathematical curve of \(s = 105.886 \log_{10}(1 + r)\) exhibits a steep initial gradient that rapidly flattens:

  • Input levels in the lower quartile below \(r \le L/4 = 64\) receive the most dramatic expansion:

\[ s(64) = 105.886 \log_{10}(65) \approx 105.886 \times 1.8129 \approx \mathbf{192} \] A narrow dark band of \(64\) levels is stretched across \(192\) output levels (a \(300\%\) dynamic expansion).

  • Input levels from \(r = 64\) to \(r = 255\) are compressed into the remaining upper band from \(192\) to \(255\).
  • At maximum intensity \(r = 255\), the output evaluates to \(s(255) = 105.886 \log_{10}(256) = \mathbf{255}\). Peak white remains completely anchored.

Evaluate a logarithmic transformation on a high-dynamic-range astronomical capture with three points: deep-space nebula background at \(r_1 = 5\), faint stellar gas filament at \(r_2 = 30\), and an exposed galactic nucleus at \(r_3 = 255\), using \(c = 105.886\).

Step 1: Compute logarithmic responses.

  • Background: \(s_1 = 105.886 \log_{10}(1 + 5) = 105.886 \times 0.77815 = \mathbf{82}\)
  • Gas filament: \(s_2 = 105.886 \log_{10}(1 + 30) = 105.886 \times 1.49136 = \mathbf{158}\)
  • Galactic nucleus: \(s_3 = 105.886 \log_{10}(1 + 255) = 105.886 \times 2.40824 = \mathbf{255}\)

Sense-check: The separation between the background and filament expanded from \(30 - 5 = 25\) original units to \(158 - 82 = \mathbf{76}\) display units (more than a threefold contrast gain), while the nucleus remained firmly anchored at \(255\) without saturation blooming.

Scope & Assumptions: The logarithmic operator assumes the primary signal of interest occupies the low-intensity spectrum. If applied to an image whose meaningful contrast resides in bright highlights, the logarithmic curve severely compresses highlight variance into indistinguishable white bands.

2.10.4 Power Law and Gamma

The general family of non-linear tonal curves is governed by the power-law (gamma) relationship. Historically developed to calibrate the non-linear luminance response of cathode-ray tube (CRT) electron guns, power-law transforms now serve as universal contrast-tuning operators.

The power-law transformation is defined as:

\[ s = c \cdot r^{\gamma} \]

where \(c\) and \(\gamma\) are positive constants. In normalized intensity coordinates where \(r, s \in [0, 1]\), setting \(c = 1\) yields the canonical form:

\[ s = r^{\gamma} \]

On an integer scale \([0, L - 1]\), the normalized form evaluates as \(s = (L - 1) \left(\frac{r}{L - 1}\right)^{\gamma}\).

The behavior of the transform is strictly determined by whether the exponent \(\gamma\) sits below or above unity (\(\gamma = 1\)):

  1. Fractional Gamma (\(0 < \gamma < 1\)): The transfer curve is concave downward (similar to the log curve). It stretches dark input values into a broad output range while compressing highlight values. This brightens underexposed images and exposes hidden shadow detail.
  2. Exponential Gamma (\(\gamma > 1\)): The transfer curve is concave upward. It compresses dark values and expands highlight values, dimming washed-out images and enhancing bright surface textures.
  3. Identity Gamma (\(\gamma = 1\)): The transformation degenerates to a straight linear identity line (\(s = r\)).

Mathematical Discrepancy Note: In casual speech, instructors occasionally refer to fractional exponents (\(\gamma < 1\)) as "less than zero" and steep exponents (\(\gamma > 1\)) as "greater than zero." In rigorous signal processing, \(\gamma\) must remain strictly positive (\(\gamma > 0\)). A negative exponent (\(\gamma < 0\)) would produce division by zero at \(r = 0\) and invert monotonicity. The true physical boundary is unity (\(\gamma = 1\)).

Demonstrate gamma correction on clinical and remote sensing imagery:

Case A: Underexposed Spinal MRI Scan (Targeting Shadows) A dark sagittal spine radiograph holds critical disc vertebrae detail bunched between \(r = 10\) and \(r = 40\) on an 8-bit scale (\([0, 255]\)).

  • Select fractional exponents: \(\gamma \in \{0.6, 0.4, 0.3\}\).
  • Evaluate a sample disc pixel at \(r = 25\) with \(\gamma = 0.4\):

\[ \frac{r}{255} = \frac{25}{255} \approx 0.09804 \] \[ s = 255 \times (0.09804)^{0.4} \approx 255 \times 0.3956 = \mathbf{101} \] The pixel value leaps from a murky \(25\) to a crisp mid-gray \(101\), revealing compression fractures without washing out the white cortical bone margins.

Case B: Overexposed Aerial Satellite Imagery (Targeting Highlights) A high-altitude aerial view of sunlit desert farmland is washed out, with soil boundaries bunched between \(r = 210\) and \(r = 245\).

  • Select exponential values: \(\gamma \in \{3.0, 4.0, 5.0\}\).
  • Evaluate a field pixel at \(r = 220\) with \(\gamma = 3.0\):

\[ \frac{r}{255} = \frac{220}{255} \approx 0.86275 \] \[ s = 255 \times (0.86275)^{3.0} \approx 255 \times 0.64205 = \mathbf{164} \] The washed-out pixel dims from \(220\) down to a rich tone of \(164\), expanding the visual contrast of agricultural boundaries.

Sense-check: Fractional gamma (\(0.4\)) pulled low values up (\(25 \to 101\)), while steep gamma (\(3.0\)) pulled high values down (\(220 \to 164\)), proving how varying \(\gamma\) around \(1.0\) dynamically shifts tonal allocation.

Common Pitfall: Setting \(\gamma\) too low (e.g., \(\gamma < 0.2\)) introduces excessive noise amplification in near-zero background pixels, producing mottled gray backgrounds in medical scans. Setting \(\gamma\) too high (e.g., \(\gamma > 6.0\)) crushes mid-tones into pure black.

2.10.5 Linear Versus Curved in One Glance

Plotting intensity curves on an input-output grid \((r, s)\) clarifies the fundamental differences between point operations:

  • Identity line (\(s = r\)): A diagonal line at \(45^\circ\) passing through \((0, 0)\) and \((255, 255)\).
  • Flat lift (\(s = r + C\)): A parallel linear line shifted upwards, clipping flat at \(s = 255\).
  • Negative line (\(s = 255 - r\)): A straight line running diagonally downward from \((0, 255)\) to \((255, 0)\).
  • Logarithmic curve (\(s = c \log(1 + r)\)): A steep upward arc that bows strongly above the identity line and flattens toward the white boundary.
  • Power-law curves (\(s = r^\gamma\)): A continuous spectrum of curves spanning above the identity line for \(\gamma < 1\) and dipping below the identity line for \(\gamma > 1\).

Linear maps maintain constant slope throughout; curved maps dynamically vary local gain across the gray scale.

2.10.6 Student Questions and Answers

Q: What fundamentally distinguishes a gamma transformation from a standard linear brightness lift? Both modify pixel intensities. A: A linear brightness lift adds an identical offset \(C\) to every pixel across the board. Every tone shifts by an identical increment, which uniformly washes the entire scene without altering relative differences between adjacent pixels. A gamma transformation raises normalized intensities to an exponent (\(s = r^\gamma\)), creating non-uniform tonal expansion. For \(\gamma < 1\), dark shadow boundaries (such as the subtle edge between a human jawline and the background) experience large relative gain differences, significantly sharpening perceived feature boundaries while leaving highlights stable.

A second question addressed transform parameter selection:

Q: Why not always use logarithmic transforms instead of tuning gamma? A: The logarithmic transform has a fixed curvature determined solely by the boundary condition. It cannot adjust its degree of non-linearity to match varying degrees of underexposure. Power-law transformations offer a continuous, tunable parameter \(\gamma\). An engineer can continuously vary \(\gamma\) from \(0.1\) to \(5.0\) to precisely match sensor optics, display characteristics, or diagnostic imaging requirements.

Exam note: Categorize each imaging challenge with its optimal transfer operator:

  • Dark-field radiographs with dense structures \(\to\) Image Negative (\(s = L - 1 - r\)).
  • Extreme dynamic range Fourier spectra and deep-space captures \(\to\) Log Transform (\(s = c \log(1 + r)\)).
  • Underexposed shadows / spinal MRIs \(\to\) Fractional Gamma (\(\gamma < 1\), e.g., \(0.3 \le \gamma \le 0.6\)).
  • Overexposed scenes / washed-out aerial captures \(\to\) Exponential Gamma (\(\gamma > 1\), e.g., \(3.0 \le \gamma \le 5.0\)).

Real-world: Modern consumer camera firmware automatically applies an inverse gamma curve (\(\gamma \approx 1/2.2 \approx 0.45\)) inside the image signal processor (ISP) before encoding JPEGs, ensuring linear raw sensor responses match the non-linear human visual system and standard sRGB monitors.

2.11 Contrast Stretching and Intensity Slicing

2.11.1 Stretching a Narrow Band

Why do photographs captured in fog, underwater environments, or dim lighting appear dull, flat, and uniformly gray? The sensor records plenty of light, but the recorded intensities are compressed into a narrow slice of the total available tonal range.

A low-contrast image (an image whose histogram is concentrated within a tight sub-interval of the available intensity scale) fails to utilize the full dynamic range of the display system, resulting in muddy, indistinct visual boundaries.

Think of an accordion whose bellows are tightly compressed in the center. The instrument possesses wide physical potential, but its musical expression is muffled until the player pulls the handles outward to the full wingspan. Contrast stretching performs the exact same mechanical expansion on pixel intensities: it anchors the lowest observed value to absolute black, pulls the highest observed value to absolute white, and linearly scales all intermediate values across the entire spectrum.

Given an image with intensities bounded by minimum observed value \(r_{\min}\) and maximum observed value \(r_{\max}\), the linear contrast stretching transformation maps input intensity \(r\) to output intensity \(s\) via:

\[ s = \frac{r - r_{\min}}{r_{\max} - r_{\min}} \times (L - 1) \]

where \(L\) is the total number of intensity levels (e.g., \(L = 256\) for 8-bit systems, with \(L - 1 = 255\)).

In modern machine learning pipelines, this formula is recognized as min-max normalization. Instead of scaling to \([0, 255]\), machine learning data loaders scale to unit interval \([0.0, 1.0]\):

\[ s_{\text{norm}} = \frac{r - r_{\min}}{r_{\max} - r_{\min}} \]

This prevents gradient explosion during neural network backpropagation while standardizing numeric range across varying camera sensors.

In general image processing (as formulated in Digital Image Processing by Gonzalez and Woods), contrast stretching can also be defined piecewise using arbitrary coordinate knee-points \((r_1, s_1)\) and \((r_2, s_2)\):

\[ s = \begin{cases} \alpha\, r & 0 \le r < r_1 \\ \beta\, (r - r_1) + s_1 & r_1 \le r < r_2 \\ \gamma\, (r - r_2) + s_2 & r_2 \le r \le L - 1 \end{cases} \]

where \(\alpha = \frac{s_1}{r_1}\), \(\beta = \frac{s_2 - s_1}{r_2 - r_1}\), and \(\gamma = \frac{(L - 1) - s_2}{(L - 1) - r_2}\). Setting \(r_1 = r_{\min}, s_1 = 0\) and \(r_2 = r_{\max}, s_2 = L - 1\) simplifies directly to global min-max stretching.

A murky underwater photograph exhibits pixel intensities severely bunched between \(r_{\min} = 100\) and \(r_{\max} = 200\) on an 8-bit scale (\(L = 256\)). Stretch this bunched band across the full \([0, 255]\) display range for three sample pixels: \(r_1 = 100\), \(r_2 = 140\), and \(r_3 = 200\).

Step 1: Compute the dynamic span. \[ \Delta r = r_{\max} - r_{\min} = 200 - 100 = 100 \]

Step 2: Evaluate transformed intensities.

  • For \(r_1 = 100\):

\[ s_1 = \frac{100 - 100}{100} \times 255 = 0 \times 255 = \mathbf{0} \]

  • For \(r_2 = 140\):

\[ s_2 = \frac{140 - 100}{100} \times 255 = \frac{40}{100} \times 255 = 0.4 \times 255 = \mathbf{102} \]

  • For \(r_3 = 200\):

\[ s_3 = \frac{200 - 100}{100} \times 255 = 1.0 \times 255 = \mathbf{255} \]

Sense-check: Input \(r_1 = 100\) maps precisely to black (\(0\)), input \(r_3 = 200\) maps precisely to peak white (\(255\)), and mid-value \(140\) scales proportionally to \(102\). Tonal differences that were previously imperceptible are now spread over the full 256-level dynamic range.

Scope & Assumptions: Global contrast stretching assumes that \(r_{\min}\) and \(r_{\max}\) represent true signal boundaries rather than corrupt noise spikes. If an image contains even a single dead black sensor pixel (\(r = 0\)) or specular glint (\(r = 255\)), \(r_{\min}\) becomes \(0\) and \(r_{\max}\) becomes \(255\), rendering the global stretching transformation completely ineffective. Robust implementations employ percentile clipping (e.g., setting \(r_{\min}\) at the 1st percentile and \(r_{\max}\) at the 99th percentile).

2.11.2 Slicing and Clipping for One Target

While contrast stretching enhances the entire dynamic range, certain computer vision tasks demand isolating a specific, narrow band of intensities associated with a physical structure of interest.

Intensity-level slicing (a non-linear thresholding operator that highlights a designated band of interest \([A, B]\)) operates in two fundamental modes:

  1. Binary Slicing (Bandpass without Background): Sets all intensities within range \([A, B]\) to maximum white, and pins all background pixels (shadows and bright glare alike) to zero:

\[ s = \begin{cases} L - 1 & \text{if } A \le r \le B \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

  1. Slicing with Background Retention: Highlights the band of interest to maximum white while preserving the original tonal context of all other pixels:

\[ s = \begin{cases} L - 1 & \text{if } A \le r \le B \\ r & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

An outdoor security camera monitors a road intersection under severe lighting conditions, with blinding direct sun glint (\(r > 175\)) and deep tree shadows (\(r < 115\)). Vehicle license plates are known to reflect light consistently within narrow target band \([A, B] = [120, 160]\). Apply binary intensity slicing to three scene pixels: shadow pixel \(r_a = 60\), plate character pixel \(r_b = 145\), and sun glare pixel \(r_c = 230\).

Step 1: Evaluate against slicing interval \([120, 160]\).

  • Shadow pixel: \(r_a = 60 < 120 \implies s_a = \mathbf{0}\)
  • License plate pixel: \(120 \le r_b = 145 \le 160 \implies s_b = \mathbf{255}\)
  • Sun glare pixel: \(r_c = 230 > 160 \implies s_c = \mathbf{0}\)

Sense-check: Blinding sunlight and murky shadow regions are both suppressed to pure black (\(0\)), isolating the license plate characters as a distinct binary silhouette ready for optical character recognition.

Common Pitfall: Using binary slicing when spatial context is required for downstream navigation. Suppressing all pixels outside \([A, B]\) to zero obliterates landmark geometry. When contextual orientation is necessary, always choose slicing with background retention.

Negative, Log, and Power-Law Transforms

Must-know: Negative: s = L - 1 - r. Log: s = c*log(1 + r) lifts dark tones. Power-law: s = c*r^gamma (gamma < 1 expands dark shadows, gamma > 1 compresses dark tones to reveal washed-out highlights).

\[ s = (L - 1) - r, \quad s = c\log(1 + r), \quad s = c\, r^{\gamma} \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Assuming gamma bounds are < 0 vs > 0 when the physical threshold is unity (gamma < 1 vs gamma > 1), or applying flat lifts instead of gamma curves.

Self-check: Which transform enhances faint lesions on a dark mammogram? What gamma values open dark spinal scans versus bright aerial views?
