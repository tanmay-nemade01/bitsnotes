14.1 Deformable Parts Models and Region-Based Detection

This DPM template heat map refresher opens our detection story. Before fast single-pass models existed, finding people in photos meant comparing new images against a learned average and checking each candidate region twice.

Think of the earliest detector as a bouncer with a single reference photo. Every newcomer gets held next to the photo, and only close matches walk in. That reference-photo idea is the whole starting point.

A deformable parts model, often shortened to DPM, is one of the earliest attempts at detection. We take many images, build a template image from them, and compare new images against that template. Wherever the comparison lights up we read a heat map, declare that people exist at that spot, and draw a bounding box around it.

Picture a metal detector sweeping a beach. It hums quietly over plain sand and shrieks over a buried coin, and the loudness map tells you where to dig. A template works the same way: slide it across the photo, record a match score at each spot, and dig where the map glows hottest. The analogy breaks in one place: the detector senses metal itself, while the template only measures likeness to an average, so a person in a strange pose can stand right under the coil and still score low.

A template \(T\) is the average appearance learned from many cropped training images, and a heat map \(H\) is the grid of match scores produced by sliding \(T\) across a new image \(I\). At each position \((u, v)\), with \(u\) the horizontal shift and \(v\) the vertical shift, the score reads:

\[ H(u, v) = \text{similarity}(T, I_{u,v}) \]

Here \(I_{u,v}\) is the patch of the new image centered at \((u, v)\), and \(\text{similarity}\) is a match function such as normalized cross-correlation, which peaks when the patch looks like the template. Spots where \(H\) rises above a cutoff \(\tau\) (tau), the detection threshold, become detections, and each surviving peak gets a bounding box, a rectangle drawn around the reported person.

A region proposal network works differently. It detects different regions inside the image first. For each region it predicts whether a person is inside that region or not, and the surviving regions become bounding boxes. The R-CNN family and all of its variants follow this pattern: propose a few thousand candidate regions \(R_1, \ldots, R_M\), run a classifier \(f(R)\) on each one to score person against background, and keep the winners.

Both approaches share one trait: they look at the image twice. First they look at the image and predict regions. Then they pass the image through a network again to check whether each predicted region is correct. DPM behaves the same way in spirit, with information never flowing in one single go. That double look, propose and then verify, is the cost the next method removes.

One number from earlier scoring needed a fix. A grading mix-up had reported the average score as 63.4, and the corrected value is 69. For 20 different classes that mean average precision, the average of per-class precision scores, is good enough for our purposes, and further training and tuning can push it higher.

Scope: Template matching assumes new objects look like the average template, so it holds for upright, front-facing people at a fixed scale and breaks on odd poses, heavy occlusion, or large scale shifts. Assumption: Region methods assume the proposal stage covers every true object, so any person the proposer never proposes can never be detected no matter how strong the classifier is.

Picture the heat map as a photo-sized sheet. The horizontal axis marks pixel columns and the vertical axis marks pixel rows, while brightness marks the match score. A bright blob over a torso with dark sand around it is the landmark to read, and the takeaway is direct: brightness is likeness to the average, not proof of a person.

Two traps catch beginners here. First, a hot spot is similarity, not certainty, so clutter shaped like a torso can glow without holding a person. Second, neither method sees the image once: the template scans every location and region pipelines propose regions and then re-examine them, so counting only one pass understates the real cost.

Templates plus double-checking gave the first working detectors, and their two-pass price tag is exactly what single-pass detection wipes out next.

Factory floors still show why this history matters: DPM drove early pedestrian detection in the PASCAL VOC era, and the R-CNN family ruled leaderboards from 2014 to 2016, so every modern detector is best read as an answer to their slowness.

14.1.1 Template Matching with Heat Maps

DPM builds a template from multiple images, slides new images against it, and reads the resulting heat map. Hot spots mark where people are, and each hot spot gets a bounding box. This is the earliest detection attempt in our story, and it sets up everything that follows: a fixed average to match, a score at every location, and a cutoff that turns scores into boxes.

14.1.2 Two-Pass Region Scoring

Region-based methods split the work into two passes: propose regions, then classify each one. The proposer proposes candidate boxes and the classifier verifies them, so the image is seen twice and the compute bill doubles. That double look at the image is exactly the cost that the next method removes.

14.2 Single-Pass Detection and the Image Grid

YOLO, short for You Only Look Once, removes the second glance that region methods need, and the single glance plus grid analogy is where the new design starts.

Why scan a crowd twice when one careful look can name every face? That single-glance question is the hook for this whole design.

YOLO drops the region prediction network entirely. There is no separate network that proposes regions first. The model looks at the image once and directly predicts whether an object sits at each spot. That single glance is the whole point of the name.

Picture a bingo card laid over a photo. Each square owns whatever falls inside it, and the caller reads all 49 squares in one pass instead of circling candidates first and re-checking them later. The card breaks down for objects that sprawl across many squares, which is why the grid idea later gives way to anchor boxes.

We start with a conventional image and divide it into a 7 by 7 grid. Either 5 by 5 or 7 by 7 are standard choices here. Later versions such as YOLO 8 and YOLO 9 drop the grid idea, but from YOLO 1 through YOLO v4 the grid stays, so we stick with it. Every row and column runs 1 through 7, splitting the full image into 49 cells. Each cell can be called a cell or a block.

An \(S \times S\) grid splits the image into \(S^2\) cells, here \(S = 7\) for 49 cells, and a cell owns an object only when that object's center falls inside it, even when the box itself spills across neighbors. Each cell then predicts its own boxes, so detection becomes a local job: the network reads the full image once through its convolutional layers and emits one fixed prediction vector per cell.

For every cell we predict whether an object is present and how sure we are. Suppose the input image holds a bicycle and a dog. We want the word "dog" plus a bounding box, where a bounding box means the four corners: top left, top right, bottom left, bottom right. We also want confidence scores. YOLO takes the image, splits it into 7 by 7, and returns boxes plus scores through one network pass.

Each predicted box carries an \(x, y\) coordinate plus a width and height. Here \(x\) and \(y\) mark the center of the bounding box, while \(w\) is the box width and \(h\) is the box height. All four are plain numbers describing box geometry: \(x\) runs across the image, \(y\) runs down it, \(w\) spans the box horizontally, and \(h\) spans it vertically, all drawn relative to the image size. A confidence score rides along with each box, giving five numbers per prediction.

Scope: The grid design assumes each object center lands in a distinct cell, so it holds for spread-out objects and breaks when many small objects crowd into one cell. Assumption: One forward pass must carry all evidence, so the method assumes the convolutional features alone, with no second look, are enough to place and name every box.

Draw the mental figure: the horizontal axis runs across pixel columns, the vertical axis runs down pixel rows, and 7 vertical plus 7 horizontal lines carve 49 squares. The landmark is the cell holding the dog's center, which glows as the responsible square even though the dog's body covers several cells. The takeaway is that ownership follows the center, not the area.

Do not mix up box corners with box centers: the lecture's four-corners picture describes where the drawn rectangle lands, but the network predicts the center \((x, y)\) plus size \((w, h)\), and converting between the two forms is where beginners slip. Also, one pass means one fixed grid, so objects whose centers share a cell compete and only the stronger survives, which the limit section unpacks.

One pass over a 7 by 7 grid turns detection into 49 local predictions, and the next step is learning what numbers each cell actually predicts.

Speed is why this design spread: by folding propose and verify into a single forward pass, YOLO ran in real time on plain hardware and carried over to unseen image styles with little extra training, from security cameras to early self-driving prototypes.

14.2.1 Why One Pass Matters

Seeing the image twice costs time and machinery. One network that proposes regions plus another pass that verifies them means two rounds of heavy compute. YOLO folds both jobs into a single pass, which is why it runs fast on plain hardware and generalizes to new image styles with little extra training: there is no proposal list to score, just one forward sweep that emits boxes and labels together.

14.2.2 Dividing the Image into Cells

The grid turns detection into a local job. Each of the 49 cells is responsible for objects whose centers fall inside it. If the image is 256 by 256 pixels, each cell covers about 256 divided by 7 pixels per side, which works out to roughly 36 to 37 pixels:

\[ 256 / 7 \approx 36.6 \]

So one cell spans a patch of about 36 by 36 pixels. Small objects inside one cell still get their own predictions, as the next section shows, because each cell predicts its own boxes and the box may extend well past the cell borders.

14.3 Confidence Scores and Class Probabilities

Prediction inside a cell happens in two layers, and the object chance times class chance split is the definition to learn first.

How can one cell admit something is there and name it in the same breath? It asks two questions in order: is anything here, and if so, what is it?

First, we predict the probability that an object is present in the cell at all, without caring what it is. Say the chance that a dog is present in some cell is 0.7. That number is written \(P(\text{object})\), the probability that the cell holds any object, a value between 0 and 1 where 0 means empty and 1 means certain.

Think of a night watchman with a torch. First he asks whether anyone stands in the dark corner at all, and only then does he ask who it is. The torch beam is \(P(\text{object})\): bright means someone is there, dim means the corner is empty. The picture breaks if we skip the first question, because naming a person in an empty corner is pure guesswork.

Second, we predict boxes. For every cell we make two box predictions, box one and box two, each with its own confidence score CS1 and CS2. So each cell proposes two candidate boxes, and each candidate says how sure it is that an object sits there. Each candidate also carries its own geometry, the center \(x\), the center \(y\), the width \(w\), and the height \(h\).

A confidence score \(C\), with \(C\) in \([0, 1]\), tells us whether an object sits in the predicted box, while the class probabilities name which object it is. The plain reading puts the split in words first: each box knows where it is and whether it holds anything, and the cell as a whole knows what the thing would be. In words from the discussion, "the score is the probability of the object multiplied with the probability of the class given the object." Reconstructed in symbols:

\[ \text{score} = P(\text{object}) \times P(\text{class} \mid \text{object}) \]

Here \(P(\text{class} \mid \text{object})\) is the conditional probability of the class given that an object is present, the chance the object belongs to a given class assuming something is there, also between 0 and 1. The product rule of probability justifies the multiply: the joint chance of two things happening together equals the chance of the first times the chance of the second assuming the first happened.

For one cell the chance of a dog given an object might be 0.8. Multiplying gives the overall score:

\[ 0.7 \times 0.8 = 0.56 \]

So the cell reports 56 percent confidence that a dog sits there, and it attaches its bounding boxes to that claim. The same cell also scores other classes such as bicycle, car, and dining table, each with its own conditional probability.

Worked pair one: strong dog evidence. The cell predicts \(P(\text{object}) = 0.7\) and \(P(\text{dog} \mid \text{object}) = 0.8\). Multiply step by step: \(0.7 \times 0.8 = 0.56\). Final answer: 0.56, or 56 percent confidence that a dog sits there. Sense-check: both inputs sit above one half, so a product near one half feels right, and this score survives a 0.5 cutoff.

A low object probability drags every class score down. Take a cell with \(P(\text{object}) = 0.2\) where the chance of a bicycle given an object is 0.7:

\[ 0.2 \times 0.7 = 0.14 \]

The confidence score drops to 0.14. We still admit the patch might hold part of a bicycle, but the low score marks it for removal later.

Worked pair two: weak bicycle patch. The cell predicts \(P(\text{object}) = 0.2\) and \(P(\text{bicycle} \mid \text{object}) = 0.7\). Multiply step by step: \(0.2 \times 0.7 = 0.14\). Final answer: 0.14, or 14 percent confidence, which gets cut. Sense-check: an empty-looking patch can name a bicycle loudly yet still score low, because the object chance gates everything.

Scope: The product form assumes the class call only matters when an object is present, so it holds for cells with real objects and collapses gracefully toward zero on empty ground. Assumption: The two predicted boxes per cell share one class mix, so the design assumes at most one class per cell, which breaks for crowded mixed cells.

Picture a dial for the object chance feeding into a row of class dials. The horizontal knob sets \(P(\text{object})\) from empty to certain, each vertical dial sets one conditional class chance, and the outgoing wire multiplies them. The landmark is the dog wire glowing at 0.56 while the bicycle wire sits dim at 0.14. The takeaway: the object dial gates every class wire at once.

Beginners mix up the two layers: the box confidence says something is here, while the conditional class chance says what it would be, and only their product is the reportable score. A second trap is reading a high conditional chance as a detection: \(P(\text{bicycle} \mid \text{object}) = 0.9\) on an empty cell with \(P(\text{object}) = 0.1\) still scores only 0.09.

Q: Does the confidence score come as one single value while the bounding box needs all four of \(x\), \(y\), width, and height predicted? Several students asked this confidence score single value question in different words.

A: Yes. The confidence score is one single value, and the bounding box prediction covers all four numbers \(x\), \(y\), width, and height. Every box prediction carries five values in total: the four geometry numbers plus the one confidence value.

Each cell reports the joint score \(P(\text{object}) \times P(\text{class} \mid \text{object})\) per class, and stacking those scores across all 49 cells fixes the network output size next.

The split mirrors the field standard: detection heads predict box geometry plus an object confidence, then a classifier names the contents, so the same two-layer pattern shows up in every modern detector family.

14.3.1 Worked Numbers for Dog and Bicycle

A concrete pair makes the arithmetic stick. A cell with strong dog evidence gives \(0.7 \times 0.8 = 0.56\), which survives. A weak bicycle patch gives \(0.2 \times 0.7 = 0.14\), which gets cut. The pattern is always the same: object chance times class-given-object chance equals the reported score, so strong object evidence lifts every class and weak object evidence sinks every class.

14.3.2 Small Objects Sharing One Cell

Imagine a 256 by 256 image split 7 by 7, with one tiny person and another tiny person inside the same cell. The cell still predicts two boxes. The object probability says yes, something is here. The class-given-object probability says the thing is a person. A cell holding both a dog and a bicycle gets scored for each class the same way, and thresholding later keeps the winners: each class gets its own product, and only products above the cutoff live on.

14.3.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Is the confidence score a single value while the bounding box needs four numbers for \(x\), \(y\), width, and height?

A: Yes, the confidence score is one single value per box while the bounding box covers four numbers. Every prediction is five values in total: \(x\), \(y\), width, height, and the confidence score.

14.4 Output Tensor and Thresholding

All 49 cells predict at once, so the full prediction has a fixed size, and the 1470 count is the number to derive by hand.

If every square on the bingo card shouts at once, how many numbers land on the table? Count them once and the network shape is fixed forever.

Stack every prediction together and the output shape is fixed. For the Pascal VOC dataset with 20 classes and two boxes per cell, each cell contributes 2 times 5 box values plus 20 class values. In words from the discussion, "7 by 7 multiplied with 2 by 5, and 2 by 5 plus 20, where 20 is the number of classes and 5 covers x, y, w, h, and one confidence score." In symbols, with \(S\) as the grid size, \(B\) as boxes per cell, and \(C\) as class count:

\[ \text{output} = S \times S \times (B \times 5 + C) \]

Here \(S \times S\) counts the cells, \(B \times 5\) counts the box values per cell with 5 meaning the four geometry numbers \(x\), \(y\), \(w\), \(h\) plus one confidence score, and \(C\) counts the per-cell class values.

Substituting \(S = 7\), \(B = 2\), \(C = 20\):

\[ 7 \times 7 \times (2 \times 5 + 20) = 7 \times 7 \times 30 = 1470 \]

Step by step: \(2 \times 5 = 10\) box values per cell, \(10 + 20 = 30\) values per cell, \(7 \times 7 = 49\) cells, and \(49 \times 30 = 1470\).

The output tensor is the flat vector of all cell predictions, of length \(S \times S \times (B \times 5 + C)\), here 1470 numbers for \(S = 7\), \(B = 2\), \(C = 20\). Each cell owns a slice of 30 numbers: 10 for its two boxes plus 20 for its shared class mix. That is why the class count is added once per cell rather than once per box.

Tensor count for Pascal VOC with seven grid cells per side, two boxes per cell, and twenty classes. Per cell: \(2 \times 5 = 10\) box values, plus \(C = 20\) class values, giving \(10 + 20 = 30\) values. Total: \(49 \times 30 = 1470\). Final answer: 1470 outputs. Sense-check: swapping in \(C = 1000\) classes gives \(10 + 1000 = 1010\) per cell and \(49 \times 1010 = 49490\), so the count grows with the class list exactly as expected.

So the network ends with 1470 outputs. With 1000 classes instead of 20, the last term grows to 1000 and the output count grows with it. No region selection step remains, because each cell predicts its own boxes and the thresholding step below picks the keepers.

Picture a market where every stallholder shouts their price at once. The loudest honest stall wins the customer, and the mutterers get ignored. Thresholding works that way: each box shouts its confidence score, and only shouts above the cutoff stay on the table. The picture breaks when two stalls sell different goods at equal volume, because one cutoff cannot crown two winners in one cell.

Once all boxes exist, each with a confidence score, we cut the weak ones. Keep a box only when its score beats a cutoff such as 0.5, 0.6, or 0.7, and drop the rest. This cutoff technique is called non-maximal suppression. After thresholding, only the strong boxes remain, and each surviving cell is read as belonging to its top class, for example bicycle, with the dog boxes for that cell ignored.

Scope: Fixed thresholding assumes one global cutoff separates true boxes from junk, so it holds when scores split cleanly and breaks on cluttered scenes where true and false boxes score alike. Assumption: Each cell predicts one class mix shared by both its boxes, so the strongest class takes the cell and weaker classes in the same cell vanish even when their boxes fit well.

Picture the score histogram: the horizontal axis runs from 0 to 1 in confidence, the vertical axis counts boxes, and most boxes pile near zero with a thin tail past 0.5. The landmark is the cutoff line at 0.5, 0.6, or 0.7 slicing the tail from the pile. The takeaway: the 0.56 dog survives the 0.5 line while the 0.14 bicycle falls with the pile.

Two traps live here. First, the 20 class values are shared across both boxes in a cell, so adding 20 once per cell is correct and doubling it to 40 double-counts the class mix. Second, thresholding keeps the strongest class per cell, so a good dog box can die simply because a bicycle in the same cell scored higher.

Q: Why are we adding 20 to the per-cell prediction? What does that math of classes shared per cell mean?

A: The 20 is the number of classes. Each box already predicts \(x\), \(y\), width, height, and the object probability. On top of that the cell must predict what it holds: the conditional probability of each class given an object, such as cat, bird, or TV. The final single score per class is the object probability multiplied with the class-given-object probability, so the cell needs one value per class, shared across its two boxes.

A fixed 1470-vector plus a score cutoff replaces the whole proposal pipeline, and sharing one class mix per cell is exactly the bargain that creates the next limit.

Fixed output shapes with a cutoff cleanup became the standard trick for real-time detectors: one forward pass, one flat vector, then cheap thresholding instead of a second network.

14.4.1 Why the Class Count Is Added

Each cell predicts one set of class probabilities shared across its two boxes. The two boxes differ in geometry and object confidence, but the conditional class mix is per cell. That is why 20 is added once per cell rather than once per box: \(B \times 5 + C\) with \(B = 2\) and \(C = 20\) gives \(10 + 20 = 30\) per cell, not \(2 \times (5 + 20) = 50\).

14.4.2 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why are we adding twenty classes to the per-cell prediction, and what does that math mean for the shared conditional mix?

A: Twenty counts the classes, one conditional probability per class given an object, such as cat, bird, or TV. Each box already carries \(x\), \(y\), width, height, and the object probability, so the cell adds one value per class on top, shared by both boxes. The final score per class is the object probability times the class-given-object probability.

14.5 One Class Per Cell: The YOLO v1 Limit

Sharing one class mix per cell buys speed at a price, and the hard assigned single class correction is the warning to hear.

What happens when a dog and a bicycle share one small square? Only one name fits on the label, so one of them gets lost.

Because the class mix is predicted once per cell, every cell is hard assigned to a single class. Both boxes in a cell share the same class verdict. This is a real con of YOLO v1: the cell cannot report two winners.

Think of a parking bay painted for one car. Two small motorbikes could fit side by side, but the ticket machine prints one ticket per bay, so the second bike parks free or gets towed. One cell works the same way: two objects fit, but only one class label prints. The picture breaks for large objects, which sprawl across many bays yet still answer to the single bay holding their center.

Picture a cell holding both a dog and a bicycle. The cell can only report one winner, so it may call the patch a bicycle and lose the dog. Both classes compete for the cell and only one survives, which is not fair to scenes with overlapping objects. Scores decide the contest: the class with the strongest object-times-conditional product takes the cell, and the weaker class falls below the cutoff even when its box fits well.

Hard assignment means each grid cell commits to exactly one class, the argmax over its class scores, and both boxes in that cell inherit the verdict. With scores \(s_c = P(\text{object}) \times P(c \mid \text{object})\) for each class \(c\), the cell reports \(\text{argmax}_c \, s_c\) and drops the rest. A dog scoring 0.56 against a bicycle scoring 0.61 in the same cell loses the cell 0.61 to 0.56, and thresholding then wipes the dog box out.

A concert photo shows the same flaw. One cell might hold two people plus a large banner. YOLO v1 assigns the whole cell to the banner and ignores the people, since their confidence scores run lower and thresholding wipes them out. This warning that a hard assigned single class cell ignores weaker people for the banner is the correction to carry: the model is not blind to the people, it simply has no label slot left for them.

The fix arrived immediately in YOLO v2 with anchor boxes, which let cells reason about multiple shapes and classes more flexibly. Anchor boxes sit outside our syllabus, so we note the fix and move on: later designs give each cell several shape priors, and different objects latch onto different priors instead of fighting over one label.

Scope: One class per cell holds for spread-out scenes where objects own separate cells and breaks on crowds, flocks, and overlapping objects sharing a cell. Assumption: The grid resolution is fixed at 7 by 7, so the design assumes the dataset's objects are large enough that center collisions stay rare.

Draw the mental figure: two score bars rise inside one square, dog at 0.56 and bicycle at 0.61, with a cutoff line at 0.5. Both bars clear the line, yet only the taller bar gets the label. The takeaway: clearing the cutoff is not enough when a neighbor in the same cell scores higher.

The classic trap is blaming the box regressor for a missed dog when the class vote is the real killer: the dog box can fit perfectly yet still die because the cell voted bicycle. A second trap is hoping two boxes save the day: both boxes share the one class mix, so a second box adds geometry, never a second label.

One label per cell is the speed bargain of YOLO v1, and training decides how often that bargain pays off next.

Crowd scenes and small-object datasets still punish coarse grids today, which is why later detectors add finer grids, anchor boxes, and multi-scale heads aimed at exactly this failure.

14.5.1 How the Competition Plays Out

Scores decide the winner. The class with the strongest object-times-conditional product takes the cell. Weaker classes in the same cell fall below the cutoff and vanish, even when their boxes fit well. Run the numbers once: dog at \(0.7 \times 0.8 = 0.56\) against bicycle at \(0.9 \times 0.68 \approx 0.61\), and the bicycle takes the cell 0.61 to 0.56.

14.6 Training Dynamics and Generalization

Labels must find their cells and empty ground must learn silence, and the boost matching boxes plus starve empty ground intuition drives both moves at once.

How does the network learn to shout where the dog stands and whisper everywhere else? Every step pushes the right box up and the empty boxes down.

Training matches labels to the right cells. Suppose the label says a dog sits at some spot. The network adjusts its two candidate boxes, keeps the largest fitting one, and pushes that prediction toward a score of one. Over many steps the confidence for the right box climbs while the network merges multiple guesses into one strong header: overlapping candidates collapse into a single confident detection.

Boosting means raising the confidence of the box that best overlaps a ground-truth label toward 1, while starving means pulling the confidence of boxes over empty ground toward 0. With the score \(s = P(\text{object}) \times P(\text{class} \mid \text{object})\), training drives \(s \to 1\) on matched boxes and \(s \to 0\) on empty ones, so only cells the network trusts keep high scores.

Empty predictions get the opposite treatment. During mean average precision practice, some predictions had no ground truth behind them: a box was predicted where training labels marked nothing. The fix is to pull the confidence of such boxes down. With an object chance of 0.2 and a bicycle-given-object chance of 0.4, the product is small:

\[ 0.2 \times 0.4 = 0.08 \]

Work it through: \(0.2 \times 0.4 = 0.08\), or 8 percent. The stated training goal is unambiguous: over successive iterations the network drives this confidence score toward 0 so no prediction survives there. Only cells we are sure about keep high scores of the form \(P(\text{object}) \times P(\text{class} \mid \text{object})\).

Empty-ground trace with an object chance of 0.2 and a bicycle-given-object chance of 0.4. Start: \(s = 0.2 \times 0.4 = 0.08\). Training pulls the object chance down step by step, say \(0.2 \to 0.1 \to 0.02\), while the class term stays near 0.4: \(0.1 \times 0.4 = 0.04\), then \(0.02 \times 0.4 = 0.008\). Final answer: the score sinks below 0.01 toward 0 and the box dies at any sane cutoff. Sense-check: a box with no label behind it must vanish, and the product form lets the object term drag it down alone.

Because some boxes always slip through, YOLO v1 lands below region-based networks on raw accuracy. It trades a little precision for generality. It trained on the ImageNet collection, described as 10 million images, using stochastic gradient descent with a cosine annealing learning rate plus heavy data augmentation. The 7 by 7 grid passes through successive layers down toward 3 by 3, ending in the 1470 outputs for Pascal VOC, and thresholding turns those into final boxes.

Picture a gardener watering one prized plant while starving the weeds around it. Matched boxes drink and grow toward 1, empty boxes dry out toward 0, and the garden ends with a few strong stems instead of a flat lawn. The picture breaks if the gardener over-waters: pushing every box up at once floods the field with false alarms, which is why the starving half of the update matters as much as the watering half.

The payoff is generality. YOLO v1 scored 59.2 average precision on Pascal VOC at first, settling near 68 to 69, yet it beat fancier networks on unseen styles such as the Picasso dataset and other art-like images. R-CNN variants with higher benchmark scores needed heavy retraining on each random new dataset, while YOLO worked almost off the block. Give it an unseen painting and it still finds people, bottles, and dining tables by feature similarity. It cannot tell you that a figure is the Mona Lisa painting rather than a person, or that a shape is a cat painting rather than a cat. It reports what the features resemble, and fine-tuning sharpens it from there.

Scope: Whole-image reasoning holds when object context helps, as in everyday photos, and breaks when context misleads, as in paintings where a painted cat wears person-like brushwork. Assumption: Training assumes labels cover every true object, so a missing label teaches the network that a real object is empty ground.

Picture the learning curve: the horizontal axis counts training steps and the vertical axis marks the confidence score. The matched dog curve climbs from 0.3 toward 1 while the empty-ground curve sinks from 0.2 toward 0. The landmark is the split opening between them, which lets one cutoff separate keepers from junk. The takeaway: training does not just sharpen good boxes, it silences the field around them.

Beginners read the 0.08 as a failure of arithmetic when it is the system working: a small product on empty ground is exactly what the loss wants before it pushes the score to zero. A second trap is trusting benchmark numbers alone: R-CNN wins Pascal VOC on raw precision yet loses on unseen art styles, so generality and accuracy are different contests.

Boost the matched, starve the empty, and a slightly less precise detector generalizes further than its fancier rivals.

Art-style images and unseen photo collections are where YOLO keeps working while heavier pipelines stall, from museum digitization to robots meeting rooms they never trained on.

14.6.1 Boosting Good Boxes and Starving Bad Ones

Every training step does two things at once. Boxes overlapping true labels get their scores boosted toward one. Boxes over empty ground get their scores pulled toward zero. That push and pull is the data-driven heart of the method: the loss rewards the best-overlapping box per label and punishes every box that fires on nothing, until one strong header stands per object.

14.8 Vision-Language Models versus YOLO

A design question came up: with vision-language models around, when should a team pick a VLM and when should it pick YOLO? The answer turns on infrastructure, use case size, and who owns the model, and the natural language queries versus owned local detector contrast settles it.

Should the camera answer questions in English or just shout box labels on a tiny chip? The use case, not the hype, picks the winner.

A vision-language model, or VLM, fuses vision with language using transformer blocks. It trains on huge annotated collections across multiple modalities and answers natural-language queries such as asking whether violations appear in a region. Training one from scratch is heavy: first large-scale pre-training, then use-case-specific supervised fine-tuning (SFT), then reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), then direct preference optimization. The full run can take years. Teams pick VLMs when they lack training infrastructure but accept others owning their data, when they want fast delivery through fine-tuning on top, when queries arrive in free-form language, or when they are large organizations targeting many customers at once.

Think of a VLM as hiring a brilliant consultant who brings their own library, versus training YOLO as raising a guard dog that lives in your yard. The consultant answers any question in words but keeps the library, while the dog knows one yard perfectly and obeys only you. The picture breaks when the yard itself needs reasoning: the dog barks at intruders but cannot explain why the fence failed.

YOLO fits the opposite corner: small, local, owned. A student project with Qualcomm built small home cleaning bots that steer with LiDAR, asking only whether an obstruction sits ahead. That job needs a tiny centralized model on weak hardware, and YOLO nano fits. Shop floors, drones, and other local settings choose YOLO when the company wants to own the patents and intellectual property. Training is light: start from a model already trained on ImageNet textures and formats, annotate around 100 images, run about 100 iterations on a plain CPU, and detection starts working. R-CNN cannot touch that startup cost.

Manufacturing has not replaced YOLO, and nobody in that space is shipping VLM-based inspection now. Vision is a tough nut: in natural language a hallucination is forgivable, but on a shop floor one error can cost life and limb, so teams stay with the owned, testable detector. VLMs are still in their data-collection youth. People are being paid around 25 dollars a task to gather sets such as the Indian hawkers gathering dataset, and full autonomy is not here yet. YOLO stays for the next few years.

The advancing edge is real though. VLMs already fly in drone warfare in Ukraine and Russia and have been deployed in Indian counter-operations. Friends at a drone startup work on autonomous delivery and engagement, where the craft must reason, not just detect. YOLO can spot objects but carries no thought process, so those teams add language-model agents that decide and fly. The practical stack today is YOLO plus AI agents: each ROS topic, the robotic operating system channel, feeds one sensor, the camera topic feeds YOLO, and YOLO reports its output to an autonomous agent that acts. Agents connect to tools through MCP servers, and the choice between pure YOLO, pure VLM, or the hybrid stays murky and use-case driven. One business warning closes the topic: building on someone else's product invites a price hike tomorrow, so ownership keeps mattering.

Quality is judged with IoU, the overlap metric for boxes. An IoU, short for intersection over union, scores how well a predicted box matches the true box:

\[ \text{IoU} = \frac{\text{area of overlap}}{\text{area of union}} \]

Here the numerator is the shared area of predicted and true boxes, the denominator is their combined area, and the score runs from 0 for no touch to 1 for a perfect match. A predicted box scoring 0.8 against truth overlaps strongly, while one scoring 0.2 barely grazes it.

14.10 Dataset Size and Transfer Learning

How much data is enough is the data-science question of the day, and the animal dataset size plus transfer learning question gives the working answer.

Why do some projects click with 100 sharp images while others starve on millions? The sample only matters when it mirrors the whole.

Dataset size is subjective and varies project to project. Detecting buildings versus forests is easy: a small sample where vegetation marks the answer suffices. Detecting one person in a crowd or one operation sequence on a robotics floor needs far more data. The core question is pure data science: how large a sample represents the whole population?

Think of tasting soup. One spoon tells you the flavor of the whole pot when the pot is well stirred, but a lumpy stew needs a spoon from every corner. Buildings versus forests is the well-stirred pot, while one person in a crowd is the lumpy stew. The picture breaks when the recipe changes mid-meal: a sample that mirrored yesterday's population can miss today's once the menu shifts.

Two regimes follow. Training from scratch wants anything above 10,000 images, and one million images at minimum is the stated floor. Transfer learning changes the game because the YOLO model has already seen many textures and formats. Then 100 to 200 images can be enough. Transformers push this further: trained on the whole web, they scale up from a small sample easily. Some projects still fail with 10 million images while others click with 100 sharp ones, so generalizability is decided per use case.

Transfer learning means reusing a model pre-trained on a large collection such as ImageNet and fine-tuning it on a small new set, while training from scratch means starting from random weights with no prior knowledge. The population-versus-sample rule decides between them: when 100 to 200 labeled images mirror the full population of scenes, transfer learning suffices, and when they cannot cover the variety, the project needs 10,000 images or more, ideally a million.

Language models show the same pattern. The 7 billion parameter size became a standard because models near that size start forming intuitions of their own during training. Smaller 1.5 billion distilled models exist too, trading some of that emergent behavior for size.

Scope: The 100-image rule holds when the pre-trained backbone already knows the textures and the new task stays narrow, and it breaks when the new domain looks nothing like the old one. Assumption: The sample must mirror the population across light, angle, and background, so 200 near-identical frames count as one sample, not two hundred.

Picture two piles: a small neat stack of 150 labeled animal photos against a towering crate of a million web images. The horizontal axis marks dataset size on a log scale and the vertical axis marks accuracy, with the transfer curve rising fast and flat while the from-scratch curve starts near zero and climbs slowly. The landmark is the crossing near 10,000 images where scratch training finally catches up. The takeaway: sharp representative samples beat sheer volume until the task outgrows the backbone.

The common trap is counting frames instead of variety: ten minutes of video from one camera is one scene, not thousands of samples. A second trap is trusting the million-image floor blindly: a clean narrow job can click with 100 sharp images, while a broad wild job can starve on millions of dull ones.

Annotate the collected images, convert them to the training format, fine-tune the pre-trained model, and iterate until it generalizes.

14.11 Building U-Net from Scratch

Segmentation gets the from-scratch treatment in Keras with the TensorFlow Keras layers, and the convolution block dropout pooling with skip connections pattern is the unit to master.

What if the library U-Net never quite fits your scans? Build the U from blocks you own, test each block, and stitch them yourself.

The architecture mirrors the U-Net design: downsampling uses convolutions plus max pooling, upsampling uses transpose convolutions, and skip connections use concatenate. All four come from the layers library. The left arm squeezes the image into meaning, the right arm stretches meaning back into a mask, and bridges carry detail across.

Picture the U as a valley hike. The way down compresses the view into a pocket map, the way up expands the map back into the landscape, and ropes tossed across the valley hand fine detail from the descent to the climbers coming up. The ropes are the skip connections: without them the climbers return with the gist but lose the pebbles. The picture breaks for very deep valleys, where too many down-steps shrink the map past recovery.

First we take each image with its mask. Segmentation pairs look like one image plus one mask, and both must share the same dimensions. Preprocessing decodes the image into a tensor and does the same reduction for the mask.

A mask is the per-pixel label image paired with each photo, with the same height and width, where each pixel says which class that spot belongs to. An encoder is the downsampling arm that trades space for meaning through convolutions plus pooling, while a decoder is the upsampling arm that trades meaning back for space through transpose convolutions. Skip connections concatenate encoder features with decoder features at matching sizes so fine edges survive the squeeze.

Then we define a convolution block. Each block holds two 3 by 3 convolutions with ReLU activation, same padding, and a kernel initializer, followed by dropout and one max pooling step into the next layer.

The kernel initializer sets the starting kernel values before training. Starting from all zeros trains slowly, so we start from a uniform, normal, Gaussian, or random spread such as all ones or a Gaussian kernel, and the kernel then learns its final weights from there. Dropout is regularization against overfitting: it stops a few neurons from grabbing all the weight updates while the rest die off, which keeps the network from memorizing. Max pooling uses a 2 by 2 kernel to shrink the map, halving height and width while doubling the channel depth downstream. We also save each block output as a skip connection, because the encoder layer must later concatenate with its decoder partner.

Filter doubling with a random input shape block test. Feed a random input of shape 96 by 128 by 3 through the fresh block: two 3 by 3 same-padded convolutions keep 96 by 128, then 2 by 2 max pooling halves it to 48 by 64. Layer one uses 32 filters, layer two 64, then 128, then 256, so channels double as space halves. Final answer: the block maps 96 by 128 by 3 to 48 by 64 by 32 and the saved 96 by 128 by 32 skip waits for its decoder partner. Sense-check: space shrinks by 2 while channels grow, which is exactly the squeeze the U needs.

Good practice is to test every piece as we write it. We feed a random input of shape 96 by 128 by 3 through the fresh block and check what comes out. That habit also proves authorship: writers who test each piece leave a trail that generated-then-pasted code lacks.

The decoder block mirrors the encoder: one transpose convolution first to double height and width, a merge with the contractive input from the matching convolution layer through concatenation, then conventional convolutions to refine the blend. The two paths start separate and get stitched here, coarse meaning from below meeting fine detail from across.

The decoder block mirrors the encoder. One block runs a transpose convolution first, merges the result with the contractive input from the matching convolution layer, then applies conventional convolutions. The two paths start separate and get stitched here.

Assembly reuses the block code. The full U-Net uses four encoder layers calling the convolution block in a chain: layer one with 32 filters feeds layer two with 64, then 128, then 256. The decoder path upsamples through the transpose-convolution blocks, each also receiving its saved skip as the contractive input, concatenating encoder features with upsampled ones at every level. A final convolution, convolution 9 in the demo, produces the output mask. This recipe is fully flexible: a 5 by 5 convolution, dropout on or off, or batch normalization all slot into the same blocks. Fixed libraries hand us a set architecture, but hand-built blocks belong to us, which is why healthcare teams write custom extractors.

Training closes the demo. We set a custom input height, width, and channel count rather than a stock 128 by 128 or 256 by 256, build the U-Net, and compile with a sparse categorical crossentropy loss over softmax outputs plus the Adam optimizer:

\[ L = - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p_{i, y_i} \]

Here \(L\) is the training loss, \(N\) counts the labeled pixels in the batch, \(y_i\) is the true integer class of pixel \(i\), and \(p_{i, y_i}\) is the softmax probability the model assigns to that true class, a value in \([0, 1]\) where 1 means fully sure and right. The negative log punishes confident wrong pixels hardest: a pixel given 0.9 on its true class costs \(-\log 0.9 \approx 0.105\), while one given 0.1 costs \(-\log 0.1 \approx 2.303\). Sparse means the labels arrive as integers per pixel rather than one-hot vectors, and Adam is the adaptive optimizer that steps the weights. We train about 40 epochs with a buffer and batch size of 500. Loss falls while accuracy, or IoU if preferred, climbs. Custom prediction then compares true masks with predicted masks: the true segmentations look cleaner while the predicted ones still sit a bit off. That gap is normal for a standardized custom build. Both the detection and segmentation code will be shared for study, though neither is strictly in the syllabus.

Scope: Sparse crossentropy over softmax holds for mutually exclusive pixel classes where each pixel owns exactly one label, and breaks for multi-label pixels that need independent sigmoids instead. Assumption: Image and mask share dimensions and alignment, so any resize must touch both identically or edges learn the wrong addresses.

Picture the U: the horizontal axis is depth through the network, the vertical axis is spatial size. Feature maps shrink 96 to 48 to 24 down the left arm while channels climb 32 to 64 to 128 to 256, then the right arm mirrors back up with horizontal skip bridges at each level. The landmark is the bottom of the U at 256 channels holding the richest meaning in the smallest map. The takeaway: squeeze for meaning, bridge for detail, stretch for the mask.

Three traps recur. First, all-zero initializers stall learning since every neuron starts identical, so pick a uniform, normal, or Gaussian spread. Second, mismatched image and mask sizes corrupt every pixel label silently, so assert equal shapes before training. Third, dropout that stops neurons grabbing all updates helps training but must switch off at inference, or predictions jitter between runs.

Q: What is the kernel initializer and why does it matter with Gaussian start when we begin with a 3 by 3 kernel?

A: The initializer chooses the kernel starting values. All zeros learn slowly, so we start from a uniform, normal, Gaussian, or random spread and let training shape the weights from there. A good start shortens the path down the loss surface instead of crawling from a flat zero field. Dropout then stops a few neurons from grabbing all the weight updates, which keeps the network from overfitting.

Two convolutions, a skip saved, a pool down, a transpose up, a concat across, and a loss that punishes confident wrong pixels: that loop is the whole U-Net.

Healthcare organizations favor custom implementations over fixed detection libraries, writing their own extractors for scans that stock models never saw.

Key Industry Applications

Manufacturing lines everywhere run YOLO for inspection, with no current replacement: owned, testable detectors beat fluent models where one error can cost life and limb.

Qualcomm-style home cleaning bots steer on LiDAR plus tiny YOLO models, asking only whether an obstruction sits ahead on weak hardware.

Driver-assistance units use oriented OBB variants of YOLO, drawing tilted boxes around road objects that axis-aligned boxes fit poorly.

Drone teams in Ukraine, Russia, and Indian counter-operations fly VLM-guided craft, while delivery startups pair YOLO detections with reasoning agents through ROS topics and MCP servers.

Art-style and unseen image collections favor YOLO generalization over heavier R-CNN pipelines: YOLO trained on photos still finds people, bottles, and dining tables in paintings by feature similarity.

Healthcare organizations write custom U-Net extractors instead of fixed libraries, hand-building encoder-decoder blocks for scans that stock models never saw.

Edge devices from the Raspberry Pi up run 5 to 6 MB nano models, with 3 MB and 1.5 MB SqueezeNet builds below that, keeping decisions on the device instead of streaming frames to a server.

14.1 Deformable Parts Models and Region-Based Detection

\[ H(u, v) = \text{similarity}(T, I_{u,v}) \]

Top pitfall: A hot spot is similarity, not certainty

Self-check: Why do DPM and R-CNN both look at the image twice?

14.2 Single-Pass Detection and the Image Grid

Must-know: YOLO looks once over a 7 by 7 grid; each cell owns objects centered inside it

Top pitfall: Box corners describe the drawing but the network predicts center plus size

Self-check: Which cell owns an object whose box spans four cells?

Connects to: 14.1, 14.3

14.3 Confidence Scores and Class Probabilities

Must-know: Cell score equals object chance times class-given-object chance; dog 0.7 x 0.8 = 0.56 survives

\[ \text{score} = P(\text{object}) \times P(\text{class} \mid \text{object}) \]

Top pitfall: A high conditional class chance on an empty cell still scores near zero

Self-check: A cell has P(object)=0.2 and P(bike|object)=0.7. What is the score?

Connects to: 14.2, 14.4

14.4 Output Tensor and Thresholding

Must-know: Output is S x S x (B x 5 + C) = 1470; thresholding keeps boxes above 0.5-0.7

\[ 7 \times 7 \times (2 \times 5 + 20) = 7 \times 7 \times 30 = 1470 \]

Top pitfall: The 20 class values are shared per cell, so adding 40 double-counts

Self-check: Why is 20 added once per cell and not once per box?

Connects to: 14.3, 14.5

14.5 One Class Per Cell: The YOLO v1 Limit

Must-know: Each cell commits to one class; dog 0.56 loses to bicycle 0.61 in the same cell

Top pitfall: A perfect dog box can die when the cell votes bicycle

Self-check: Why can't two boxes in one cell report two different classes?

Connects to: 14.4, 14.6

14.6 Training Dynamics and Generalization

Must-know: Training boosts matched boxes toward 1 and starves empty boxes toward 0; YOLO trades precision for generality

\[ 0.2 \times 0.4 = 0.08 \]

Top pitfall: A small 0.08 product on empty ground is the loss working, not broken math
