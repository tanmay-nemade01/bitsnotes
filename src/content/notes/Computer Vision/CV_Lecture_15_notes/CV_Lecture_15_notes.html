15.1 Why Track Instead of Detecting Every Frame

15.1.1 The cost of detecting every frame

A detector can name everything in one still picture. Ask it to rename everything sixty times a second and the whole system chokes.

A detector such as YOLO can find a person or a car in a single frame. Running that detector on every frame of a video carries three costs: compute cost, hardware cost, and latency cost. Compute cost is the raw arithmetic per frame. Hardware cost is the bigger chip, extra power, and extra heat needed to sustain that arithmetic. Latency cost is the delay each frame suffers while waiting for an answer. A video analytics system that pays all three on all frames cannot stay real time.

Tracking breaks this bind. Detect once on the first frame, then follow the object across the interim frames with cheap mathematics instead of repeated detection. Detection answers "what and where" at full price. Tracking answers only "where did it go" at pocket change. That is how video analytics stays real time.

Detect once, track across the interim frames. A detector localizes objects from scratch in a frame. A tracker carries each known box forward using frame-to-frame motion. The detector runs rarely, the tracker runs always, and the pair meets the frame budget together.

Think of airport security. Officers check your passport once at the gate, then cameras follow you down the corridor to the plane. Nobody re-checks your passport every ten steps. The analogy breaks at one point: if you leave the corridor and re-enter, you get checked again. Trackers behave the same way. Lose the object and the detector must run again.

15.1.2 A real-time rhythm that works

Human persistence of vision is 1 by 16th of a second. Eyes blend changes that arrive faster than roughly every 16th of a second, and most viewing applications are built for human eyes. A practical rhythm follows from this: at 60 frames per second, run the detector on the first frame and again on the 5th or 10th frame, and track across the frames in between. Between frame 1 and frame 10, the remaining 8 frames are handled by tracking how far each object moved. Nothing on the road moves at the speed of light. A car holds 10, 20, or 40 kilometres per hour, so its frame-to-frame shift stays small. The tracker only needs to be fast enough to keep up with that shift.

Detector arithmetic at 60 frames per second. Suppose the detector needs 30 milliseconds per frame while each frame allows only about 16.7 milliseconds. Per-frame detection misses every deadline. Now detect on frame 1 and frame 10 only: the detector runs 6 times per second instead of 60, a 10-fold cut in compute, while the tracker covers the 9 interim frames with microsecond arithmetic. Sense-check: 6 detector runs cost 180 milliseconds per second of video, well inside budget, and the tracker fills the gaps.

Scope: this rhythm assumes the frame-to-frame shift stays small. It holds for road traffic at 10 to 40 kilometres per hour viewed at normal frame rates. It breaks for objects that cross the frame in one or two steps, for long occlusions, and for cameras that whip around. Each of those cases forces an early re-detection.

Picture the timeline as a row of ten boxes. Box 1 and box 10 carry a detector stamp; the eight boxes between them carry small arrows showing each object's shift. The arrows are short because road speeds are modest next to the frame rate. That short-arrow picture is the whole bet of tracking.

A common beginner trap is treating tracking as a replacement for detection. It is not. Tracking drifts, and drift compounds frame after frame. The detector is the anchor that corrects the drift, so the detect-every-5th-or-10th-frame rhythm is load-bearing, not optional.

Recap: per-frame detection costs too much compute, hardware, and latency, so the lecture detects rarely and tracks across the interim frames. Bridge: the next question is what "tracking" even means, because a cheap door sensor also reacts to motion without tracking anything.

Traffic surveillance and autonomous vehicles live on this bargain. Detect-once-then-track pipelines keep them real time on hardware that could never run a detector on every frame.

15.2 Motion Detection versus Object Tracking

15.2.1 Motion detection

Did something move? That yes-or-no question is the entire job of motion detection.

Motion detection (a yes-or-no trigger that fires when the scene changes), uses the simplest possible sensor. A sensor on a door is tripped when someone enters, an alarm sounds, and the job is done. Nobody follows the intruder from the sensor reading alone. On a manufacturing shop floor, the painting room is a harsh place — acid wash, layered paint, glue guns, hot equipment. Nobody should step inside while work runs. A sensor on the door trips the moment it is breached, an alarm rises, and the line stops. That is motion detection. It flags the breach and stops there.

The sensor reading carries no identity, no position history, and no velocity. It says "breach" the way a smoke alarm says "smoke": loud, useful, and finished in one step.

Motion detection reports that the scene changed. Its output is a binary flag plus a timestamp. No box, no track, no memory of where the intruder went next.

15.2.2 Object tracking

Tracking starts where the alarm stops: it stays with the object while it keeps moving.

Object tracking (following one object's position frame after frame), takes one step more: it follows the movement of the object. The key word is follow. To follow something, a system needs speed, direction, and related quantities, not just a yes-or-no trigger. Motion detection reports that an object moved. Tracking stays with the object while it keeps moving.

Following means maintaining a state for the object — at minimum its position, and in practice its velocity as well — and updating that state every frame. Lose the state and you are back to detection.

Object tracking maintains a per-object state across frames. Its output is a trajectory: a linked chain of positions, plus speed and direction derived from that chain.

15.2.3 Telling them apart

Short version: motion detection senses a change and raises a flag, while tracking locks onto the object and records where it goes frame after frame.

Motion detection Object tracking
Question answered Did something move? Where did this object go?
Output Alarm flag Trajectory with speed and direction
Memory None Per-object state across frames
When to pick it Fixed camera guarding a controlled zone Any job that must hold onto a moving target

When to pick which: controlled areas with fixed cameras — a shop floor, a mall entrance, a home door — suit plain detection. Anything that must keep hold of a moving target needs tracking.

Beginners often call a door sensor "tracking" because it reacts to people. It does not track. If the system cannot tell you where the person is five seconds after the alarm, it detected motion and nothing more.

Recap: detection flags a breach, tracking follows the intruder. Bridge: the cheapest form of following needs no detector at all — just an empty photograph of the scene and pixel-by-pixel subtraction, which is where the lecture goes next.

15.3 Background Subtraction and Frame Differencing

15.3.1 The background model idea

Photograph the empty room once. Everything that later differs from that photograph is a candidate intruder.

The simplest motion check starts with a background model (one stored picture of the empty scene, used as the reference for every later frame). Photograph a door with nobody present. That empty photograph is the background model. When a new frame arrives with a person in it, subtract the background model pixel by pixel. Pixels that match cancel out. Pixels that differ mark the newcomer. Threshold the difference and a mask of the person drops out.

In symbols, let \(B(x, y)\) be the stored background intensity at pixel \((x, y)\) and \(I(x, y)\) the new frame. Compute the absolute difference \(D(x, y) = |I(x, y) - B(x, y)|\), then apply a threshold \(T\): pixels with \(D(x, y) > T\) become foreground (mask value 1), the rest stay background (mask value 0). Three steps, no learning, no detector.

Background subtraction isolates newcomers by differencing. Match means background, mismatch above threshold means foreground. The threshold \(T\), a cutoff such as \(T = 20\) intensity levels, decides how big a difference counts as real.

Think of tracing paper over a drawing. Lay the new frame over the stored empty scene and every unchanged line vanishes; only fresh ink shows through. The analogy breaks where light itself changes: tracing paper cannot tell a new boat from a new shadow, and neither can raw subtraction.

15.3.2 Worked example: isolating a boat on water

Background 100, boat 10, threshold 20. Take a patch of empty water where every pixel reads an intensity of about 100. The spoken description in the session is "this pixel range is, say for instance, blue, say 100, all hundreds, same water colour intensity". Now a boat enters. Its dark shape drops the same pixels to about 10.

Step 1, setup: background \(B = 100\), boat frame \(I = 10\), threshold \(T = 20\). Step 2, substitution: \(D = |10 - 100| = 90\) at boat pixels, \(D = |100 - 100| = 0\) on open water. Step 3, threshold: \(90 > 20\), so mark boat pixels as foreground; \(0 < 20\), so open water stays dark. Result: a clean boat mask showing the boat shape on a dark field. Sense-check: the gap between 90 and 20 is wide, so small ripples in the water cannot fake a boat.

Picture the result as a dark rectangle with a bright boat-shaped blob in the middle. The axes are pixel rows and columns; brightness in the mask means "changed". One blob, one boat, no noise — that is the ideal subtraction picture, and it only survives while light holds still.

15.3.3 Adapting the model to the time of day

One empty photograph cannot serve the whole day. Store a small album instead and pick the page that matches the hour.

One fixed background fails as light shifts through the day. The fix is a set of backgrounds sampled across time. Watch the door through the day: morning frames with the lamp off, evening frames with the lamp on. To test a 5 p.m. input frame, subtract the 5 p.m. background, not the morning one. Matching each input frame against the background from the matching time slot absorbs much of the lighting drift. The rule stays the same — subtract, compare against the threshold, keep the mask — but the reference frame now fits the hour.

Morning lamp-off versus evening lamp-on. Suppose the door pixel reads 60 in the morning background and 140 in the evening background. A 5 p.m. frame reading 145 gives \(|145 - 140| = 5\), safely below a threshold of 20, so no alarm. Tested against the wrong morning page it would give \(|145 - 60| = 85\), a false intruder. Sense-check: matching the hour turns an 85-point false alarm into a 5-point calm.

Scope: time-slot backgrounds absorb slow daylight drift only. A passing cloud, a switched lamp, or headlights sweeping the frame still break the match, because no stored page shows that exact light.

Beginners tune the threshold endlessly when light is the real problem. A threshold of 20 works for the boat-on-water case and fails for the day-night case at any setting, because the whole frame shifts. When the entire mask lights up, suspect the reference frame, not the threshold.

15.3.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: What is the main challenge with intensity subtraction followed by thresholding? A: The threshold itself is a hyperparameter, so it can be tuned, but the deeper trouble is light. A background shot at daytime against a night frame injects noise straight into the difference, and any intensity change wreaks havoc on the mask. In short: the threshold is tunable, but light shifts inject noise into the difference that no threshold setting removes.

Recap: subtract the matching empty reference, threshold the difference, keep the mask. Bridge: the Q&A already points at the crack — light. The next section breaks subtraction on purpose to map exactly where it fails.

Plain door sensors in paint shops run exactly this logic. Fixed camera, steady light, one reference frame, and the line stops the instant the mask appears.

15.4 Limits of Simple Subtraction

15.4.1 Lighting and intensity noise

Change the light and the whole frame becomes the intruder.

The day-to-night case shows the failure in full. The background model holds daytime values near 100. At night the whole scene drops toward 10 with no boat and no moonlight to lift it. Against a threshold of 20, nearly every pixel now counts as foreground: \(|10 - 100| = 90 > 20\) everywhere. The full frame lights up as one mask and the boat vanishes inside it. Even a slight intensity change between the two frames feeds noise into the difference, so plain subtraction only trusts stable light.

Night frame, no boat, threshold 20. Background holds \(B = 100\) everywhere. Night frame reads \(I = 10\) everywhere with no boat present. Difference \(D = |10 - 100| = 90\) at every pixel, and \(90 > 20\) everywhere, so the full frame lights up as foreground with zero true objects inside. Sense-check: a mask covering 100 percent of the frame carries zero information.

15.4.2 Camera movement

Shift the camera by 20 degrees and the background model no longer lines up with the live frame. Subtraction turns into apples-to-oranges: the door stands at one spot in the model and another in the frame, so their difference reports motion that never happened. Any tilt, pan, or shake injects the same false signal. Action cameras make this worse because the viewpoint never sits still. Simple subtraction needs a fixed camera, a fixed angle, and steady light — a controlled area.

Scope: background subtraction assumes pixel \((x, y)\) in the model views the same world point as pixel \((x, y)\) in the live frame. A 20-degree camera shift breaks that correspondence at every pixel, so the difference measures viewpoint change, not object motion.

Picture two door outlines drawn on tracing paper, shifted by a finger's width. Overlay them and every edge doubles — the doubled edges are exactly the false mask a shifted camera produces.

Beginners blame the threshold when the camera moved. No threshold fixes misalignment: at a small shift the edges still differ by far more than any sane threshold, and raising the threshold high enough to hide the shift also hides the intruder.

15.4.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Can you repeat what breaks when we threshold intensity differences? A daytime background minus a night frame was the example — does the light case flood the whole mask? A: Yes — two things break. First, light: a daytime background near 100 minus a night frame near 10 gives differences near 90 at every pixel, so against a threshold of 20 the whole mask floods and the boat vanishes inside it. Second, viewpoint: move the camera by 20 degrees and the model and the frame stop aligning, so subtraction compares the wrong pixels and reports motion that never happened.

Q: If light shifts between frames, does the difference already carry noise? A: Yes. By the time the difference is taken, the intensity change has already entered it as noise, and thresholding cannot separate that noise from true motion. The corruption happens at subtraction time, so no later threshold choice can undo it.

Q: Since pure intensity comparison confuses objects that share an intensity, should we compare patches of descriptive features instead? A: That points at the real solution. Intensity alone cannot tell two same-grey objects apart, so richer per-patch description is the way forward, and later methods in this session build exactly that way. Note the redirect inside this answer: the 20-degree camera-shift lesson still applies first, because misaligned patches compare the wrong pixels no matter how rich the description.

Recap: light floods the mask and camera shift misaligns it, and both failures enter at subtraction time. Bridge: with the background model broken by any camera motion, the lecture turns to detectors that find objects fresh in each frame and connect the sightings.

15.5 Tracking by Detection

15.5.1 Detect, then connect across frames

When the camera moves too much for subtraction, stop subtracting and start recognizing.

When the camera moves, background models collapse, so detection takes over. A single-shot detector such as YOLO or SSD finds the car in one frame at position \((x, y)\) and finds the same car later at \((x', y')\). The displacement between the two boxes gives motion, and displacement over the known time gap gives velocity. High-speed traffic cameras work this way — two sightings, one time gap, one speed reading.

Tracking by detection connects fresh sightings. Each detector run outputs a box \((x, y)\) with no memory; the tracker links boxes across frames into one trajectory, then reads displacement and velocity off the links.

YOLO and SSD are single-shot detectors (networks that output boxes and labels in one forward pass, built for speed). They trade some accuracy for that speed, which matters twice here: the boxes are noisier than a careful detector's, and the tracker inherits every miss.

15.5.2 Worked example: reading speed from two boxes

Two YOLO boxes over a time gap yield velocity by displacement over time. A detector reports the car at \((x, y) = (100, 200)\) pixels at time \(t_1 = 0.0\) seconds and at \((x', y') = (160, 210)\) pixels at time \(t_n = 0.5\) seconds.

Step 1, displacement: \(\Delta x = 160 - 100 = 60\) pixels, \(\Delta y = 210 - 200 = 10\) pixels. Step 2, time gap: \(\Delta t = t_n - t_1 = 0.5\) seconds. Step 3, image-plane velocity: \(v_x = 60 / 0.5 = \mathbf{120}\) pixels per second, \(v_y = 10 / 0.5 = \mathbf{20}\) pixels per second. Step 4, world speed: converting image-plane shift to world speed needs the camera angle, elevation, and the missing depth \(z\), but that mapping is plain trigonometry once calibration is known. Sense-check: a mostly horizontal 60-pixel shift with a small 10-pixel vertical drift matches a car driving past the camera, not toward it.

Formally, with box centers \((x_1, y_1)\) at \(t_1\) and \((x_n, y_n)\) at \(t_n\):

\[v_x = \frac{x_n - x_1}{t_n - t_1}, \qquad v_y = \frac{y_n - y_1}{t_n - t_1}\]

where \(v_x\) and \(v_y\) are the image-plane velocity components in pixels per second. Two-box displacement over time gives the object velocity vector.

15.5.3 Why detection alone breaks

Four failure modes pile up. Occlusion hides the object: a car slides under a bridge or into a tunnel between sightings, and no box exists to connect — the tracker cannot tell whether the car stopped or kept rolling. Fast objects outrun the frame rate. Camera shake and illumination swings break the detector itself. And running a detector on every frame burns compute while YOLO-style methods trade accuracy for speed. Detection gives boxes, but boxes alone cannot bridge gaps.

Scope: box-connecting assumes the object stays visible, the frame rate stays ahead of the motion, and the detector keeps firing. A bridge, a tunnel, or a dark frame breaks the chain, and no interpolation can tell a stopped car from a moving one inside the gap.

Picture the trajectory as dots joined by arrows, then a bridge drawn across the middle: dots stop at the bridge entrance and resume at the exit, with a dashed question-mark segment underneath. That dashed segment is unknowable from boxes alone.

A common trap is reading world speed straight off pixel shift. Pixels are not metres: without camera angle, elevation, and depth \(z\), a 60-pixel shift could be a slow car nearby or a fast car far away. Calibration maps pixels to metres, and skipping it turns every speed reading into a guess.

15.5.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: If we detect the car at time \(t_1\) and again at \(t_n\), can we subtract the two frames to get how far it travelled? A: Not directly. Image-plane shift is not world distance until calibration enters — camera angle, elevation, and the depth \(z\) component map pixels to metres. Worse, occlusion breaks the chain: if the car passed under a bridge or through a tunnel between sightings, no frame tells whether it stopped or kept moving. Calibration maps pixels to metres, but occlusion breaks the chain entirely.

Recap: detectors give boxes, links give displacement over time, but occlusion leaves gaps no box can bridge. Bridge: before solving motion properly, the lecture surveys where tracking already ships — phones, maps, and faces.

15.6 Where Tracking Shows Up

15.6.1 Stabilization and super resolution

The phone in a pocket already runs this lecture: it steadies shaky video and sharpens blurry text with motion estimation.

Everyday phones lean on motion understanding. Optical and electronic image stabilization (OIS and EIS on common phone brands) detect shake and correct it: gyroscopes and frame-to-frame motion estimates agree on how the hand moved, and each frame is shifted back by that amount. Super resolution stacks an image sequence, estimates the motion between frames with optical flow, and fuses them into one sharp picture — each frame contributes slightly shifted samples of the same page, and aligning them recovers detail no single frame holds.

Low-quality scans of prescriptions defeat text readers, and even human eyes strain at the pixelated strokes — motion blur from a shifting camera or surface causes much of it. Detect the motion, correct it, and the same page turns readable. The moon-zoom feature on modern phones is the same trick pointed at the sky: many shifted moon frames, one aligned stack, one crisp disc.

Think of stacking transparent sheets with the same drawing slightly offset on each. Shuffle them until the lines coincide and the drawing snaps sharp; that alignment step is optical flow, and the sharpened stack is super resolution. The analogy breaks for large shifts: shuffle too far and sheets align to the wrong lines, which is exactly the large-motion failure the pyramid section later fixes.

15.6.2 Mapping, faces, and expressions

Visual SLAM matches features across frames to learn which way a body moves while building its local map — the same detect-motion-then-map loop studied with stereo and single-camera setups, where SIFT features linked by RANSAC across frames mark the frame-to-frame motion. Each matched feature votes on the camera's travel; the surviving consensus becomes both the motion estimate and the map update.

The OpenCV face tracker follows key points on a face and reads their travel. Lips up means a smile, lips down a frown, flaring nostrils anger, dilating pupils drowsiness or intoxication. The face-tracking routine in this OpenCV library runs on the mean-shift family, one of the methods queued for the next session.

Recap: stabilization, super resolution, SLAM with SIFT features linked by RANSAC, and OpenCV face tracking all run on frame-to-frame motion. Bridge: every one of them needs a precise definition of "motion between frames", which the lecture now builds as optical flow.

Traffic surveillance, autonomous vehicles, and augmented-reality waypoint tracking run the same detect-once-then-track bargain from section 15.1, while drones lean on Kalman-style filtering toward full autonomy.

15.7 Optical Flow and Apparent Motion

15.7.1 Brightness patterns in space and time

Forget objects for a moment. Watch each pixel's brightness and ask: which way does the pattern slide?

Optical flow (the per-pixel shift of the brightness pattern between back-to-back frames), describes how things move between back-to-back video frames, pixel by pixel, by watching brightness change across space and time. The heavy phrase "spatiotemporal image brightness variation" means something plain: at each pixel, which way does the light pattern slide.

A hand demo makes it concrete. A person waves a hand before a camera: the hand blocks rays that used to reach the lens, then lets them through as it shifts, and parted fingers bend the pattern further. Each pixel reports light arriving, blocked, arriving again — and that travelling pattern is the apparent motion of the hand. A spinning wheel says the same thing: at time \(t_1\) one set of points blocks the light, the wheel turns by time \(t_2\), those points free up while new ones cover over, and the sweep of the pattern reads as rotation.

Optical flow tracks the brightness pattern, not the object. Its output at each pixel is a small arrow \((u, v)\) saying where that pixel's brightness appears to have moved. Whether the world moved with it is a separate question.

Think of shadows racing across a wall as clouds pass. The shadows sweep, shrink, and merge while nothing on the wall itself moves. Optical flow reports the sweep of the shadows; believing the wall moved would be the beginner's mistake the next subsection warns against.

15.7.2 Apparent motion is not always real motion

Apparent motion can appear with nothing moving at all. Hold the scene still and slide the light source from the bottom to the top: points that blocked light before now pass it, and the brightness pattern sweeps as if something travelled. Move the camera instead of the lamp and the same false sweep appears — the back of a hand hidden at one angle shows at another, yet the hand never moved. Optical flow reports what the brightness pattern seems to do, never a promise about the world. Constraints must fence off these false readings, which is where the next section starts.

Scope: every flow arrow carries an ambiguity — object motion, light motion, and camera motion all paint the same travelling pattern. Flow is evidence of motion, not proof of it, until assumptions fence off the impostors.

Picture two identical arrow fields side by side. On the left a hand waves under a fixed lamp; on the right the hand sits still while the lamp slides. The pixel patterns match arrow for arrow. No flow algorithm can separate the two cases from brightness alone — that separation is the job of the three assumptions coming next.

Recap: optical flow is the apparent sweep of brightness, and light or camera motion can fake it perfectly. Bridge: the lecture now states the three working assumptions that fence off the fakes so the derivation can proceed.

15.8 Three Working Assumptions

Tracking is not fully solved, so optical flow proceeds under three stated limits. Each limit rules out one family of false readings.

15.8.1 Brightness constancy

Follow one physical point across two frames and its brightness comes along unchanged.

A point keeps its brightness as it moves between frames. The palm shows one colour at \((x, y)\); slide it by \((u, v)\) and the same point still shows that colour, provided the lighting holds. In words from the session: "intensity at x, y at time t minus 1 is equal to intensity at x plus u, y plus v at time t". Formally:

\[I(x, y, t-1) = I(x+u, y+v, t)\]

where \(I\) is scalar pixel intensity, \((x, y)\) is the pixel position, \(t-1\) and \(t\) are back-to-back frame indices, and \(u\) and \(v\) are the small horizontal and vertical shifts in pixels. This brightness constancy equation equates the shifted intensities: same point, same brightness, new address. Texts call it the brightness constancy constraint, and it is the standard starting point for translational alignment.

This rules out the sliding-lamp fake: if light changes between frames, the equality breaks and the method must stay silent.

15.8.2 Small motion

Between two back-to-back frames, nothing travels far.

Points move a short distance between frames. A car covers a limited stretch in one frame interval — never tens of kilometres in a single step. Small \((u, v)\) keeps higher-order terms tiny enough to drop, which the derivation in the next section relies on.

Concretely, "small" means small in pixels: shifts of one or two pixels keep the squared terms \(u^2\) and \(v^2\) near zero, while a ten-pixel jump makes them large enough to wreck the linear approximation. This fences off the large-motion case, which the pyramid section later rescues by shrinking the image until big shifts look small.

15.8.3 Spatial coherence

Neighbours on one surface travel together.

Parts of one object travel together. Fingers on one hand share a direction; one finger cannot fly off opposite to its neighbour. An eye cannot drift one way while its face heads the other. Neighbouring pixels on the same surface share nearly the same \((u, v)\), which lets one window vote on a single motion.

This fences off chaos: without coherence, each pixel could claim its own private motion and 25 neighbours would give 25 unrelated answers instead of one shared solution. Coherence is the license for the local window that Lucas-Kanade builds next.

Recap: brightness constancy (a palm keeps its colour, fencing off light changes), small motion (a car covers a limited stretch per frame, fencing off big jumps), and spatial coherence (fingers travel together, fencing off per-pixel chaos). Bridge: with the fakes fenced off, the lecture turns constancy into an equation and derives the flow constraint step by step.

Carry these three forward as the Lucas-Kanade limits: small motion, handled by pyramids except for extreme motion; brightness constancy, the same look every frame; spatial coherence, neighbours moving as one.

15.9 From Brightness Constancy to the Flow Equation

15.9.1 The constancy equation

Name the unknown first: the shift \((u, v)\) is the only thing we do not know.

Start from the first assumption. A point sits at \((x, y)\) at time \(t-1\) with some intensity, then shifts by \(u\) along the horizontal axis and \(v\) along the vertical axis. The spoken form from the session is: "the intensity at x, y, t minus 1 equals the intensity at x plus u, y plus v, t". The reconstruction is:

\[I(x, y, t-1) = I(x+u, y+v, t)\]

Everything here is known except the shift \((u, v)\) — finding those two numbers is the whole task. The left side is measured in the old frame, the right side is measured in the new frame at the shifted address, and the equality ties them together.

15.9.2 Taylor expansion refresher

A shifted function value can be rewritten around its base point. The session states the one-variable form in words as "f of a, plus f derivative of a divided by 1 factorial times x minus a, then second order, third order, onward". Reconstructed as the Taylor series one-variable expansion form:

\[f(x) = f(a) + \frac{f'(a)}{1!}(x-a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2 + \cdots\]

Each term pairs a derivative at the base point \(a\) with a power of the shift \((x-a)\). Here \(a\) is the base point where derivatives are evaluated, \(x - a\) is the shift away from it, \(f'(a)\) is the slope there, and the factorials \(1!, 2!, \ldots\) scale each higher power.

Taylor with numbers. Take \(f(a) = 10\), slope \(f'(a) = 3\), curvature \(f''(a) = 2\), and shift \(x - a = 0.1\). First-order estimate: \(10 + 3 \times 0.1 = \mathbf{10.3}\). Second-order correction: \((2/2) \times 0.01 = 0.01\), giving 10.31. Sense-check: at a small shift of 0.1 the first-order term dominates and the correction is tiny — which is exactly why small motion lets the derivation drop everything past first order.

15.9.3 Expanding in three variables

The shifted intensity \(I(x+u, y+v, t)\) depends on three variables, so the expansion needs three first-order terms. The session builds it in words: "first order derivative along x" times the x-shift, the same along y, the same along time. Here \(I_x\) means \(\partial I / \partial x\) (intensity change along rows), \(I_y\) means \(\partial I / \partial y\) (change along columns), and \(I_t\) means change between frames. The shift \((x+u) - x\) leaves \(u\); likewise \(v\) along y. Dropping second- and higher-order terms (safe because \(u\) and \(v\) are small, so their powers shrink toward zero) gives the three-variable first-order expansion of shifted intensity:

\[I(x+u, y+v, t) \approx I(x, y, t-1) + I_x u + I_y v + I_t\]

Each gradient term is a slope times a step: \(I_x u\) is how much intensity changes sliding \(u\) pixels horizontally, \(I_y v\) the same vertically, and \(I_t\) the frame-to-frame change at a fixed pixel. The approximation sign carries the small-motion assumption on its back.

15.9.4 Cancelling down to one equation

Place this expansion inside the constancy equation from 15.9.1:

\[I(x, y, t-1) = I(x, y, t-1) + I_x u + I_y v + I_t\]

Move the base term left. The two \(I(x, y, t-1)\) terms cancel toward zero — the session checks this aloud twice ("will these two cancel each other"; both sides hold the same base value). What remains is the optical flow constraint equation, where \(I_x u\) plus \(I_y v\) plus \(I_t\) equals zero:

\[I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0\]

It holds approximately, since Taylor expansion approximates and the small-motion limit trims the tail. Plain reading: the spatial gradients weighted by the motion plus the time gradient sum to zero at each pixel. Where the image is flat (\(I_x = I_y = 0\)), the equation says \(I_t = 0\): no change, no motion to find.

15.9.5 Matrix form

Group the gradients into one row and the unknowns into one column. The session phrases it as "delta i equals Ix, Iy multiplied with u, v, plus It, equal to zero". Reconstructed as the gradient dot velocity matrix form of the flow equation:

\[\nabla I \cdot \mathbf{v} + I_t = 0\]

with \(\nabla I = \begin{bmatrix} I_x & I_y \end{bmatrix}\) the spatial gradient row vector (1 by 2) and \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix}\) the velocity column vector (2 by 1). Every pixel yields one such equation, and motion recovery means solving for \(\mathbf{v}\).

Scope: this matrix form is one scalar equation in two unknowns. It constrains the motion to a line, not a point — the aperture problem in miniature. The lecture resolves it next with a window of neighbours, not with more algebra on one pixel.

15.9.6 Student Questions and Answers

Q: When we form x minus a from two pixel positions, what does that difference mean mathematically — is it the distance covered? A: Distance covered is one reading, but the working meaning is the gradient. Both \(x\) and \(x+u\) are pixel positions, and differencing positions is how gradient terms arise — that is the same differencing already used in corner detection. In short: differencing positions yields gradient terms as in corner detection, not a travel distance to stop at.

Q: Are u and v single fixed shifts, and can the full derivation be explained once more? The letters felt distracting. A: Yes — \(u\) and \(v\) are plain shift values along x and y (call them a and b if the letters distract). Rebuilt from the top: a point \((x, y)\) shifts by \((u, v)\) to \((x+u, y+v)\); brightness constancy equates the two intensities; Taylor expansion rewrites the shifted term as base plus \(I_x u + I_y v + I_t\) with higher orders dropped for small motion; moving the base term left cancels it toward zero and leaves \(I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0\). The full constancy to Taylor to cancellation chain is now rebuilt step by step.

Recap: constancy names the unknown shift, Taylor expands it, cancellation leaves one equation per pixel. Bridge: one equation cannot fix two unknowns, so the lecture counts the deficit exactly and then borrows constraints from neighbours.

15.10 One Equation, Two Unknowns

15.10.1 The counting problem

One witness, two suspects. The pixel cannot convict either motion component alone.

Each pixel delivers one constraint equation, yet it carries two unknowns, \(u\) and \(v\). One equation cannot pin down two numbers. The session polls the room on exactly this ("I have two unknowns and only one equation — can I solve it") and the answer is no.

The aperture problem starts here. A single flow equation \(I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0\) admits infinitely many \((u, v)\) pairs. Any solver needs more constraints before it can answer.

Think of one person pulling a rope through a small round hole in a fence. From the hole you see the rope sliding but cannot tell whether the pull is straight back, slanted left, or slanted right — every direction with the same through-the-hole component looks identical. The hole is the pixel, the rope is the moving edge, and the hidden slant is the unsolved component.

15.10.2 Why one equation cannot fix two unknowns

Geometrically, \(I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0\) is one line in \((u, v)\) space — a single flow equation tracing one line in uv space, where every point on that line satisfies the pixel. Pick numbers: if \(I_x = 4\), \(I_y = 2\), \(I_t = -10\), then \(4u + 2v = 10\), satisfied by \((u, v) = (2.5, 0)\), \((0, 5)\), \((1, 3)\), and infinitely many more. No further information separates them. The way out is more equations of the same form, which is where the local-window method of the next section enters: borrow constraints from neighbours that share the motion.

Picture \((u, v)\) space with \(u\) on the horizontal axis and \(v\) on the vertical. One straight line cuts across it; every point on the line is a legal motion. Two unknowns and one equation satisfy a whole line of candidates, and neighbours must lend constraints before the answer collapses to a point.

Beginners try to solve the single equation by setting one unknown to zero. That picks an arbitrary point on the line, not the true motion — it assumes the answer instead of measuring it. The lecture never solves one pixel alone; it stacks a window first.

Recap: one pixel gives one line in \((u, v)\) space, so two unknowns stay unfixed until neighbours lend constraints. Bridge: spatial coherence licenses exactly that borrowing, and the next section stacks 25 neighbour equations into the Lucas-Kanade system.

15.11 Lucas-Kanade in a Local Window

15.11.1 A window full of equations

One pixel begs; twenty-five neighbours vote. Coherence turns one equation into a solvable pile.

Spatial coherence says neighbours on one object share one \((u, v)\). The Lucas-Kanade method (1981, a local method alongside global and dense variants) exploits this: take a 5 by 5 window around the point of interest — the session traces it around a detected person — and assume all 25 pixels moved by the same \((u, v)\). Each pixel contributes its own \(I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0\), so one equation grows into 25. Stack them as the 25 by 2 window system \(A \mathbf{v}\) equals \(\mathbf{b}\):

\[A \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{b}\]

where \(A\) is the 25 by 2 stack of \([I_x, I_y]\) rows (one row per pixel, gradients measured as neighbour differences within the window), \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix}\) is the shared 2 by 1 shift, and \(\mathbf{b}\) is the 25 by 1 column of \(-I_t\) values. Other solvers exist for other settings — the Horn-Schunck method solves globally with smoothness penalties across the whole frame, and the Farneback method estimates dense polynomial-expansion flow, used for jobs such as oil flow in engine systems — but the course focuses on the Lucas-Kanade method.

Lucas-Kanade trades one exact equation for 25 agreeing ones. The shared-motion assumption is its engine; where neighbours truly move together, the stack over-constrains \((u, v)\) enough to solve.

15.11.2 Least-squares solution

Twenty-five equations for two unknowns over-constrain the system, so least squares picks the \((u, v)\) that satisfies the most equations with the least error. In words from the session: "A transpose multiplied with A, with D, equals A transpose B". Reconstructed normal equations, the least-squares normal equations with the 2 by 2 structure tensor:

\[A^\top A \mathbf{v} = A^\top \mathbf{b}\]

Written out, the 2 by 2 structure tensor and its right-hand side are:

\[\begin{bmatrix} \sum I_x^2 & \sum I_x I_y \\ \sum I_x I_y & \sum I_y^2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix} = -\begin{bmatrix} \sum I_x I_t \\ \sum I_y I_t \end{bmatrix}\]

with sums running over the 25 window pixels. Solving gives the single motion that best fits the whole patch.

Least squares with numbers. Suppose a tiny 3-pixel window gives gradient rows \([4, 0]\), \([0, 3]\), \([2, 2]\) with \(-I_t\) values \(8, 6, 10\). Then \(\sum I_x^2 = 16 + 0 + 4 = 20\), \(\sum I_y^2 = 0 + 9 + 4 = 13\), \(\sum I_x I_y = 0 + 0 + 4 = 4\), \(\sum I_x I_t = 32 + 0 + 20 = 52\), \(\sum I_y I_t = 0 + 18 + 20 = 38\). Solving the 2 by 2 system gives \(u = 2.4\), \(v = 2.2\) up to rounding. Sense-check: both gradient directions contribute, so both components come out finite — the signature of a trackable patch.

15.11.3 When the solution exists

\(A^\top A\) must be invertible, and its eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\) decide the case — the same test met earlier in corner detection. Flat patch: both eigenvalues tiny, no intensity variation, nothing to hold onto (a plain sky with drifting cloud gives no reading). Edge: one eigenvalue large and the other small — motion along the edge stays ambiguous, so the patch is unusable. Corner or rich texture: both eigenvalues large, motion along both axes is pinned, and the window tracks. Only high-texture regions with two strong eigenvalues earn tracking.

Eigenvalues grade the patch. Here \(\lambda_1\) is the larger eigenvalue of \(A^\top A\) (motion certainty along the best-constrained direction) and \(\lambda_2\) is the smaller one (certainty along the worst). Both large means solvable; anything else means skip.

15.11.4 Worked example: cloud, edge, and pedestrian

Cloud, edge, and pedestrian windows sort by eigenvalue sizes. Cloud in empty sky: the 5 by 5 window holds near-constant intensity, \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) both come out tiny, \(A^\top A\) is near-singular — verdict: no detectable motion, skip the patch. Roof edge: gradients run strong across the edge and near zero along it, so \(\lambda_1\) is large while \(\lambda_2\) stays small — verdict: ambiguous, skip. Pedestrian or rock or leaf with texture in both directions: \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) both large — verdict: solve the 2 by 2 system and keep the \((u, v)\). Corners win because the eye itself watches outlines and corners move, not blank interiors. Sense-check: flat versus edge versus corner intuition ties each verdict to its eigenvalue sizes exactly as the lecture states.

A frequent mistake is tracking on edges because "something is visible there". Visibility is not solvability: an edge pins only the across-edge component, and the along-edge slide stays free. Track edges and the flow slides phantom-like along them — the aperture problem wearing a local mask.

Recap: coherence stacks 25 equations, least squares solves them, and eigenvalues admit only textured corners. Bridge: the solver still needs its windows placed somewhere cheap, so the lecture turns to corner detectors that flag solvable patches without deep models.

15.12 Picking Points Worth Tracking

15.12.1 Choosing patches without deep learning

Do not track everywhere. Track where the math can win — and find those spots with cheap arithmetic.

Lucas-Kanade still needs its 5 by 5 boxes placed somewhere. A detector could place them, but deep models cost too much for a routine meant to stay light. The standard answer is a corner detector: the Harris corner detector or the Shi-Tomasi feature tracker flags high-texture points where \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) both run large, and tracking starts there. This corner test of both eigenvalues large for solvable patches is the placement rule: both-large means solvable, anything else means skip. Single-camera SLAM pipelines do exactly this — SIFT features plus corners mark where to bank, turn, and advance.

Corners are where the solver is invertible. The corner test and the Lucas-Kanade solvability test are the same eigenvalue check wearing two hats — one for placement, one for solving.

Grading three candidate patches. Patch A (clear sky): \(\lambda_1 \approx \lambda_2 \approx 0.01\) — both tiny, reject. Patch B (window frame edge): \(\lambda_1 \approx 8.5\), \(\lambda_2 \approx 0.05\) — one large, reject as ambiguous. Patch C (window corner): \(\lambda_1 \approx 7.9\), \(\lambda_2 \approx 6.4\) — both large, accept and track. Sense-check: only the corner pins motion along both axes, so only it gets a window.

Scope: corner seeding assumes the scene has corners to find. Point the camera at blank walls, calm water, or fresh snow and the detector returns nothing worth tracking — correctly, because no window there could solve.

15.12.2 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Does Lucas-Kanade run together with corner or object detection, or does it work independently? A: They go hand in hand. \(A^\top A\) is solvable exactly where \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) are both large, and both-large eigenvalues are precisely the definition of a corner. Finding corners and solving Lucas-Kanade are two faces of one test — corner-versus-flow separation resolved as one shared eigenvalue check.

Exam note: expect questions on why corners, not edges or flat patches, seed the tracker — both-large eigenvalues mean solvable, and corner detection plus related numerical topics reward step-by-step working in the open-book exam. Bridge: corner seeding assumes a steady camera, so the lecture now states that limit openly and shows what breaks without it.

15.13 Fixed Camera, Calibration, and the Barber-Pole Illusion

15.13.1 The fixed-camera assumption

Every equation so far quietly assumes the camera sits still.

All three assumptions quietly require a steady camera. Swing the camera and brightness constancy, small motion, and spatial coherence all fail at once — the pattern sweep then reports camera travel as object travel. The session grants the point openly: at this stage the camera stays more or less fixed, and only apparent motion under that limit is claimed.

Scope: with a fixed camera, flow arrows mean object motion. With a moving camera, every arrow mixes object travel and camera travel, and the lecture's equations cannot separate the two without calibration.

15.13.2 Calibration comes first

Because of that limit, the first job in classical vision deployment is camera calibration: build the matrix holding translation and rotation parameters so any camera shift or tilt maps back to world coordinates. With the camera angle and elevation known, pixel shifts convert to real positions through plain trigonometry, and the tracker stops blaming the world for the camera's own motion.

Think of filming from a turning merry-go-round. The whole fairground seems to spin around you; calibration is the accounting step that subtracts your own rotation before accusing any stall of moving. The analogy breaks for non-rigid scenes: calibration removes the camera's share, but independently moving objects still need tracking on top.

15.13.3 The barber-pole illusion

Repeating stripes can march while standing perfectly still.

A striped pole seems to crawl clockwise or anticlockwise as a viewer walks past, yet the pole never moves — pure parallax dressed as motion. The pole carries repeating structure with no true corner to hold, so the eye (like the tracker) reads a sweep that is not there. Whenever high-texture regions exist without genuine displacement, apparent motion deserves suspicion. This parallax pole illusion warns that texture without motion deceives: strong gradients invite solving, but the solved motion is the viewer's own walk, not the pole's.

Picture the pole from above: diagonal stripes wrap a cylinder while the viewer walks past. The stripe crossings slide along the pole's length in the image although each ink mark sits fixed on the glass. The takeaway in one line: periodic structure without a trackable corner manufactures motion.

15.13.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: If the camera itself moves, will the method report the person's motion wrongly? A: Yes. A moving camera paints its own travel onto every pixel as apparent motion, which is exactly why the fixed-camera assumption is stated. Calibration exists to subtract the camera's share before any object claim is made — camera travel paints false motion, so a fixed camera plus calibration is assumed.

Recap: the camera must hold still, calibration subtracts its travel when it cannot, and the barber pole shows texture alone can lie. Bridge: one more assumption still breaks in practice — small motion — and the lecture fixes it optically with image pyramids.

15.14 Large Motion and Image Pyramids

15.14.1 Shrinking the image shrinks the motion

Big motion breaks the math — so shrink the picture until the motion looks small.

Big \((u, v)\) breaks the small-motion limit: second- and third-order Taylor terms stop being negligible and the single clean equation falls apart. Without any deep model to call, the fix is optical — zoom out. Halving each image dimension halves pixel displacements while brightness and neighbourhood layout survive. A pyramid of 1x, 0.5x, 0.25x, 0.125x versions turns an impossible shift into a small one, Lucas-Kanade solves at the coarse tip, and each level down doubles the answer back. Phone maps use the same bargain — kilometres of world compress to centimetres of screen through one scale factor, then expand back on demand. That map-scale analogy makes shrinking motion by zooming out concrete: same world, smaller ruler, smaller numbers.

Coarse-to-fine estimation rescues large motion. Solve the small-looking problem at the pyramid tip, then scale the answer back up level by level. Each doubling is exact because each downsampling halved the shift.

Think of reading a speeding car's plate through the wrong end of binoculars. The car crawls, you read the digits easily, then you flip the binoculars and multiply your reading back to full size. The analogy breaks at fine detail: the tiny view hides small obstacles, so each level must re-check its answer against the sharper image below.

15.14.2 Worked example: ten pixels down to one

Ten-pixel shift halves to 1.25 pixels down the pyramid then scales back. Start from a 128 by 200 image carrying a 10-pixel shift. Downsample to 80 by 100: the shift drops to 5 pixels. Downsample to 40 by 50: 2.5 pixels. Downsample once more toward 20 by 30: about 1.25 pixels. Brightness constancy and spatial coherence still hold because the picture is the same picture, only smaller, and 1.25 pixels obeys the small-motion limit. Solve for \((u, v)\) at the coarse tip, then multiply back up through each doubling — 1.25 to 2.5 to 5 to 10 pixels — to recover the true motion. Sense-check: three halvings divide by 8 (\(10 / 8 = 1.25\)), and three doublings multiply back by 8.

Picture the pyramid as four stacked frames, widest at the bottom. The shift arrow spans 10 pixels on the bottom frame and shrinks to a stub at the tip. The solver stamps its answer at the tip, and the stamp doubles at each step down.

15.14.3 Seeing the fix on a swaying tree

Without pyramids, flow vectors on a swaying tree scatter — large erroneous arrows spray across the crown because the small-motion limit is violated. With the pyramid, the same tree reads correctly: the trunk sits near zero motion while the sides show the sway. Coarse-to-fine estimation removes the violation instead of tolerating it. Swaying tree flow vectors scatter without pyramids and settle with them: crown spray errors collapse, trunk reads near zero, sides keep the sway.

Beginners run more pyramid levels when the answer looks wrong, but extra levels cannot fix broken constancy — if light changed or the branch left the frame, the coarse tip solves the wrong problem confidently. Check brightness and overlap before adding levels.

15.14.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: At the coarse pyramid level, does Lucas-Kanade actually hold? A: Yes. Zooming out changes no brightness and breaks no neighbourhood — it only pulls pixels closer, so constancy and coherence survive while the shift shrinks into the small-motion range. The same picture smaller keeps constancy while the shift enters the small range. Solve there, then scale the answer back up.

15.15 Where the Method Still Fails

15.15.1 Occlusion and fast objects

Some pixels simply have no answer — the object left the frame or hid behind something.

Some gaps no pyramid closes. Occlusion removes the object entirely — a car inside a tunnel offers no pixels to track. Extreme speed defeats fixed scale factors: a sports car launching from 0 to 100 in about 5 seconds crosses too many pixels between frames for coarse-to-fine to catch. Large object movement stays an open challenge for this family of methods.

Scope: every window method assumes the tracked patch stays visible in both frames. Tunnels, bridges, and frame exits void that contract, and no solver can track pixels that are not there.

15.15.2 Light, noise, and uniform regions

Brightness constancy is fragile. Camera motion, sensor noise, and lighting swings each void it, and errors accumulate because least squares keeps adding small residuals across 25 equations. Uniform regions add the aperture problem: where only one eigenvalue is large, or both are small, flow direction stays ambiguous. A car coated in Vantablack — the blackest black paint — shows one flat tone while moving — no gradient, no reading. A tiger merged with foliage, or grass rippling as one sheet, hides the same way. Vantablack car and tiger cases show uniform regions defeat gradients: uniform motion is still motion; the method simply cannot see it.

Do not confuse "no flow found" with "nothing moved". On uniform paint, still water, or camouflaged fur the gradients vanish while the world keeps moving. Report the limit, not a zero-motion verdict.

15.15.3 FlowNet and where accuracy matters most

Deep models fill the accuracy gap where it truly counts. FlowNet-style networks estimate flow for autonomous driving and medical imaging, including image-guided robotic surgery. The heart and lungs never stop moving — every beat and breath shifts the target — so a surgeon robot must re-locate tissue within milliseconds, and only high-accuracy learned flow meets that bar. Everywhere else, edge devices keep the classical bargain: background subtraction, detection plus tracking, and Lucas-Kanade, because the mathematics runs fast. The three Lucas-Kanade limits to carry forward are small motion (handled by pyramids, except extreme motion), brightness constancy (same look every frame), and spatial coherence (neighbours move as one). The next session builds on this with mean-shift tracking and Kalman filters, which tolerate brightness shifts and larger motions better.

Classical Lucas-Kanade Learned FlowNet-grade flow
Accuracy Good on textured, small, steady-light motion High, even with large shifts and noise
Cost Tiny — runs on edge devices Heavy — needs accelerators
When to pick it Real-time tracking, stabilization, SLAM Beating-heart surgery, autonomous driving

When to pick which: ship the classical bargain where speed and power rule, and pay for learned flow where a missed millimetre costs a life.

Recap: tunnels hide, speed outruns, light breaks, flat paint blinds — and learned flow covers the cases where accuracy outweighs speed. The lecture's arc is complete: detect rarely, track cheaply, solve locally, shrink globally, and know exactly where the math stops.

Key Industry Applications

Detect-once-then-track pipelines keep traffic surveillance, autonomous vehicles, and augmented-reality waypoint tracking real time — the detector anchors identity while the tracker carries boxes across interim frames at a fraction of the cost.

Phone stabilization (OIS/EIS) and super-resolution modes (moon zoom, prescription-scan cleanup for text readers) ship optical flow to pockets: shake is estimated frame to frame and subtracted, while shifted frames are aligned and fused into one sharp image.

Visual SLAM guides robots and drones with feature-plus-corner tracking, where SIFT features linked by RANSAC vote on camera travel while the map builds underneath; modern drones lean on Kalman-style filtering toward full autonomy.

Manufacturing safety interlocks (paint-shop door sensors) run on plain motion detection — a fixed camera, a stored empty reference, and a threshold that stops the line the instant the mask appears.

FlowNet-grade learned flow serves autonomous driving and beating-heart robotic surgery where accuracy outweighs speed: the heart and lungs never stop moving, so tissue must be re-located within milliseconds, and only high-accuracy learned flow meets that bar.

Library face trackers follow facial keypoints to read smiles, frowns, anger, and drowsiness from motion alone — lips up a smile, lips down a frown, flaring nostrils anger, dilating pupils fatigue — running on the mean-shift family queued for the next session.
