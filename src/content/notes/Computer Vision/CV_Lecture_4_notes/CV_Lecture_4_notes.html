4.1 Four-Block Structure of Image Understanding

4.1.1 Edges to Textures to Patterns to Objects

Hook: Why does almost every image network look like a stack of four big blocks? The answer is simple. Seeing builds up in layers. Small jumps join into textures. Textures join into patterns. Patterns join into parts.

A grayscale picture is a 2D array of numbers. Each number is brightness at one spot. A common range is 0 for black to 255 for white. Block one scans this array for spots where brightness jumps. Block two looks at many such jumps together. It learns that a certain mix of short slanted jumps means hair or cloth or brick. Those mixes are called textures. Block three learns where such textures sit in relation to each other. Two eye textures side by side plus a nose texture below means a face pattern. Block four learns that eyes plus nose plus mouth form a face part. A last combining step then votes for the full object.

Think of building with bricks. One brick says little. A wall of bricks says texture. Two walls plus a roof says house pattern. Several houses say street part. The network works the same way. Each level reuses the last level. No level skips ahead.

Four blocks: Block 1 finds edges and lines. Block 2 groups edges into textures. Block 3 groups textures into patterns. Block 4 groups patterns into parts of objects. A final layer votes for the full object label.

This same split shows up in well known networks. ResNet has four main blocks. VGG has four main stages. Inception also stacks four levels of feature groups. Each block can hold many convolutions inside. But the job of each block stays fixed. Early blocks keep small sharp detail. Later blocks keep larger shape meaning. The session called this low level vision for block one. It called later steps mid level vision.

Picture the sizes. Block one sees spots a few pixels wide. Block two sees patches tens of pixels wide. Block three sees regions that span a face or a wheel. Block four sees arrangements of those regions. Detail shrinks as meaning grows. That trade is the heart of the design.

Scope: Four blocks are the floor for plain color photos. Color alone needs edge, texture, pattern and part stages. Signals with extra physics need extra branches on top. The first four jobs stay the same in every case.

Recap: Edges join into textures, textures join into patterns, patterns join into parts, and parts vote for objects. Next we see why biology points to the same four steps.

ResNet, VGG and Inception all use this staged idea for image classification tasks. Phone photo apps, factory inspection tools and medical viewers all copy the same order.

4.1.2 Brain Evidence and Why Four Is the Minimum

The four-step idea did not start from code alone. It started from biology. In the late 1950s and early 1960s, David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel ran a cat study on visual brain cells. They showed bars of light at varied angles. Some cells fired only for a near vertical bar. Other cells fired only for a slanted bar. Other cells fired only for a horizontal bar. That result suggested brains hold edge finders first. Later brain areas then join those finds into shapes. The classic cat visual-cortex work gave the staged idea its first hard proof.

Intuition: Think of one brain cell as a matchstick detector. It stays quiet all day. It fires only when its own tilt appears. A vertical lover ignores a flat bar. A slant lover ignores a vertical bar. The brain then listens to which lovers shout at once.

Think of the eye to see why this order helps. An eye rim edge starts with one tilt at the outer corner. It shifts tilt as it curves toward the center. It shifts tilt again toward the inner corner. This eye rim tilt chain shows why linked oriented finders must work in order. No single edge finder can catch that whole curve. Many small oriented finders must fire in turn. A later stage must link their outputs in order. That link is texture and then pattern.

The session stressed four as a floor, not a ceiling. A plain color photo needs at least four blocks. Other signals need more. An ultrasound image needs a depth branch too. An MRI volume needs a 3D branch. A 3D point map may need a splatting or field branch such as Gaussian splatting or neural radiance fields. The extra branch handles the extra physics of depth echoes, tissue density values and point-cloud shape. The first four still do edges, textures, patterns and parts.

Picture a chart of input type against blocks. Color photos stop at four. Ultrasound adds a depth-echo lane. MRI adds a density lane. Point maps add a shape lane. The x-axis is input physics. The y-axis is block count. The takeaway is one line. More physics means more lanes, but the base stays four.

Pitfalls: New learners often think more blocks always mean better sight. More blocks help only when the input holds more physics. Adding a depth branch to plain photos adds cost with no gain. Match the branch to the signal.

Recap: Biology found oriented edge cells first, and geometry shows why curves need chains of them. The base of four holds. Next we trace one face from pixels to parts.

4.1.3 How Parts Build From Edges

A face shows the build in concrete form. Small edge pieces mark the rim of the eye. Their angles move from about 135 degrees at one corner through 180 degrees along the lid to about 270 degrees near the center. A texture step groups those oriented pieces as eye texture. A pattern step notes two such eye textures sit level with each other. A part step labels that pair as eyes. A later step joins eyes with nose and mouth to label a face. Each level reuses the last level. No level skips ahead.

Walk it slowly. Start with a lid pixel. Its neighbors jump in brightness where lid meets white. That jump is an edge. Move a few pixels along the lid. The tilt turns a little. Collect twenty such turns and you hold an eye texture. Find a second eye texture at the same height and you hold a face pattern. Add nose and mouth textures below and you hold face parts. Only then does the label face make sense.

Reuse rule: Each level reads only the output of the level just below it. Edges feed textures. Textures feed patterns. Patterns feed parts. Skipping a level breaks the chain.

A brick wall gives the same lesson without faces. Short mortar jumps group into brick texture. Brick texture repeats into wall pattern. Wall plus window plus door group into house part. Change the domain and the math of grouping stays put.

Recap: One face needs a chain of tilts, then a pair of eyes, then eyes plus nose plus mouth. That chain is the bridge to what an edge is in numbers, which comes next.

4.1.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: If four blocks are the least we need, what are blocks beyond the base four for? Do they add extra physics handling?

A: Extra blocks and branches handle extra input physics beyond color. Color alone needs edge, texture, pattern and part stages. Depth, motion or medical density need added branches. An ultrasound branch tracks depth echoes. A density branch tracks tissue density values. A 3D branch tracks point shape with splatting or radiance fields. Four stays the base. More blocks serve the task at hand.

4.2 What an Edge Means

4.2.1 Abrupt Intensity Change

Hook: How does an artist draw a full statue with just a few pen lines? The artist keeps only the borders where light meets dark. Edge finding copies that trick with numbers.

An edge (a spot where brightness jumps fast) marks a border. The rule used here was plain. Edges are places where intensity changes fast. The math form for a 1D signal says the same thing in symbols. Let \(f(x)\) be brightness at position \(x\) along one row. Let \(x\) be an integer pixel index. Then a large value of edge strength from brightness jump between neighbor pixels marks a possible edge:

\[ G(x) = f(x+1) - f(x) \]

Here \(G(x)\) is the first gap at spot \(x\). Here \(f(x)\) is brightness, often 0 to 255. A large \(|G(x)|\) means a strong jump. The sign tells rise or fall. Neighbor pixels are the two cells being compared.

Look at a drawn sculpture to feel this rule. The source object is 3D. The artist draws a flat 2D view. Curves of the cheek, folds of cloth and rims of the base all survive as lines. Each line sits where black meets white on paper. Each line sits where dark meets light in the source. The sculpture sketch keeps shape by keeping only those jumps. Edge finding tries to do the same job with numbers.

Edge definition: An edge is a pixel where \(|f(x+1) - f(x)|\) is large. Strength is the absolute gap. Place is the gap location between \(x\) and \(x+1\).

Shadows and shine can also cause jumps. A cast shadow makes a dark strip on a bright wall. A shine spot makes a white flash on a dark car. Those jumps are not object borders. They still look like edges to a simple finder. Later sessions handle how to tell good borders from light effects. For now an edge means any fast brightness jump.

Picture a 1D plot. The x-axis is pixel position. The y-axis is brightness 0 to 255. A flat line means no edge. A steep cliff means an edge. The takeaway is direct. Steeper means stronger.

Scope: This gap rule applies when light is even and blur is small. Heavy blur spreads one cliff into a slope. Heavy noise adds small cliffs everywhere. Both cases need smoothing before gaps are read.

Recap: An edge is a fast local jump in brightness, read as a large neighbor gap. Next we see why chains of such jumps trace full shapes.

4.2.2 Why Jumps Describe Shape

A single jump says little alone. A chain of jumps traces a full rim. The direction of each jump matters as much as its size. A near zero-degree jump runs one way. A 90-degree jump runs across it. A 45-degree jump runs slant. A curved rim is just a chain where direction shifts bit by bit. That is why later math keeps both size and angle. Size says how strong the jump is. Angle says which way the border runs at that spot.

Use a hiker picture. The hiker feels slope underfoot. Steepness is size. Facing direction is angle. One steep step means little. A trail of steep steps maps the hill. Edge pixels work the same way. One strong pixel means little. A linked trail maps the rim.

Size plus angle: Size without angle finds dots. Angle without size finds faint lines. Only the pair traces curves, corners and closed rims.

A lens arc shows the need. Spots along the arc read near 10 degrees, then 20 degrees, then 35 degrees. Strength stays high all along. Drop the angle and the arc breaks into loose dots. Keep the angle and the dots join into glass rim.

Pitfalls: Beginners keep only strong pixels and drop angle. That gives starry noise. Others smooth so hard that angle blurs. That gives fat blobs. Keep both channels sharp from the start.

Recap: Chains of sized and angled jumps draw shape. That sets up the three jump shapes: step, ramp and roof.

4.2.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Does every visible border count as an edge, even tiny local ones inside an object?

A: Yes. Any fast local jump counts at this low level. A face holds hundreds of small edges. Skin pores, lashes and lip rims each cause jumps. A tumor mass inside soft tissue also causes a jump. Gray tissue near value 255 next to dark mass near value 50 forms a local edge with gap about 205. Later stages decide which edges belong to the target and which do not.

4.3 Step, Ramp and Roof Edges

4.3.1 Mathematical Shape of Each Type

Hook: Not all borders look the same in numbers. Some snap at once. Some climb slowly. Some spike up and down. Which shape is in your pixel row?

A step edge (a jump from one flat level to a new flat level) is the simplest case. The picture was black to white with no middle. In symbols, let \(f(x)\) be brightness along a row with \(x\) as position. Then a step looks like \(f(x) = 0\) for \(x\) left of the break and \(f(x) = 255\) for \(x\) right of the break. The first difference \(f(x+1) - f(x)\) is 0 away from the break and 255 at the break. This step edge jump from flat zero to flat bright level is the ideal border model.

A ramp edge (a slow climb from one level to a new level) spreads the same rise over many pixels. The picture was 0, then 25, 26, 27, 28, 29 and on up to 100. In symbols, \(f(x)\) climbs by small steps over a run of \(x\) values. The first difference is small but stays non-zero across that run. Many real borders look like ramps because optics blur the jump.

A roof edge (a sharp rise followed at once by a sharp fall) forms a thin ridge. The picture was 0, then 255, then back to 0. In symbols, \(f(x)\) spikes at one \(x\) and drops right after. The first difference shows a large positive spike then a large negative spike. Thin vessels, wires and folds often form roofs.

Three profiles: Step is one lone spike in the gap. Ramp is a run of small non-zero gaps. Roof is a rise spike paired at once with a fall spike. The gap pattern names the type.

Real pictures mix all three. One cross-section through brain tissue may cut a ramp at the soft outer rim. Another cut near the ear may cut a roof at a thin fold. Another cut at the skull may cut a step from dark background to bright bone. The finder must catch all three forms. That mix is why one threshold on lone spikes is not enough.

Picture three 1D plots side by side. The x-axis is position in each. The y-axis is brightness. Step shows a cliff. Ramp shows a slope. Roof shows a tent peak. The takeaway is visual. Gap runs tell them apart faster than raw brightness.

Scope: These three models assume the cut runs across the border. A cut along the border sees flat values and misses the edge. Always scan across the rise, not along it.

Recap: Step, ramp and roof differ in how gaps spread over pixels. Next we work each one with real numbers.

4.3.2 Worked Examples

Worked step from 0 to 255 with rise and fall gaps: Take row values \(0, 255, 0\) at three spots. The rule says subtract next minus current. First gap is \(255 - 0 = 255\). Second gap is \(0 - 255 = -255\). In block form:

\[ G = f(x+1) - f(x) \]

So the two gaps are 255 and minus 255. The size 255 marks a strong edge. The sign tells rise or fall. The absolute value marks edge strength at each gap. Answer: gaps 255 and -255, strength 255. Sense-check: a black-white-black flicker must give two strong opposite jumps.

The ramp case flips the lesson from lone spikes to runs.

Worked ramp from 0 to 100: Take row values 0, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, up to 100. Each gap is 25 for the first jump, then 1, 1, 1, 1 and so on. No single gap is huge. But a run of non-zero gaps marks the ramp zone. Add them: \(25 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 29\) over the first five steps, with more to reach 100. Answer: a long run of small gaps, total rise 100. Sense-check: total rise still matches the end-minus-start brightness, so the run is real.

A roof of 0, 255, 0 gives gaps \(255 - 0 = 255\) then \(0 - 255 = -255\). The pair of opposite spikes marks a thin ridge. A single spike alone would look like a step. The rise-fall pair tells roof apart from step. Answer: paired spikes 255 and -255 mean roof.

A brain cross-section ties them together. A cut through soft tissue climbs bit by bit. That run matches a ramp. A cut across a thin fold spikes up then down. That pair matches a roof. A cut from dark surround to bright skull jumps at once. That lone jump matches a step. The same image holds all three edge types in varied spots.

4.3.3 Local Edges and Tumor Illustration

A tumor case made the local point concrete. Healthy tissue in the example sat near a light gray. The value given was about 255. The mass core sat dark near value 50. This tumor gray dark gap marks a local mass rim even deep inside the organ. The gap is:

\[ G = 50 - 255 = -205 \]

Its sign is negative because brightness drops. Its absolute size 205 marks it as worth keeping. Later steps can then test shape and context to label it as mass rim rather than noise. Answer: gap -205, strength 205. Sense-check: 205 is near a full black-white jump, so it must survive thresholding.

Local rule: Inside or outside does not matter. Only the local jump matters. A deep inner border with strength 205 beats a faint outer border with strength 20.

Picture the scan line through the mass. Bright tissue flat, then a cliff down to dark mass, then flat dark, then a cliff back up. The two cliffs are the rim. The finder keeps both. Shape tests later join them into a closed ring.

Pitfalls: Learners often hunt only outer object rims and miss inner rims. Tumors, vessels and folds live inside. Scan the full row, not just the frame border.

Recap: A 255-to-50 inner drop is a strong local edge. Next we see why noise forces smoothing before any gap is trusted.

4.3.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: What about curved or slanted bevel edges? Does a gradient method still catch the edge type?

A: Yes. A curve is many short straight pieces joined. At one spot the local direction may be 0 degrees. A bit further it may be 45 degrees. Further still it may be 87 degrees or 60 degrees. The kernel moves spot by spot and reports local size and angle each time. Good resolution keeps each piece short. The chain of angles then traces the bevel. Curved bevel edges are caught because each short piece looks straight locally.

The next doubt moves from curves to axes.

Q: Can edges vary along vertical as well as horizontal? Do we need finders for all sides?

A: Yes. Intensity can jump along \(x\), along \(y\) or along both at once. That is why we take partial differences along each axis on its own. Each axis gets its own gap measure. We then join them into one size and one angle. More axes just mean more partial terms. Vertical and horizontal finders run together, not as rivals.

4.4 Noise and the Need for Smoothing

4.4.1 Ideal Signal Against Sensor Signal

Hook: Why does a photo of a flat white wall still show hundreds of tiny edges to a computer? The wall is flat. The numbers are not.

Ideal art uses flat 255 and flat 0. Real sensors do not. Heat, light flicker and hardware gain add jitter. A flat white patch may read 254, 255, 254, 253, 252 in turn. A flat black patch may read 0, 10, 20, 30, 10, 0, 20, 30 in turn. Human eyes still see flat black. Numbers still wobble. Each wobble forms a small gap. A raw difference would call each wobble an edge. That jitter would flood later stages with false finds.

The fix has three linked aims. First, smooth the image to cut noise. Second, mark candidate edge spots with derivatives. Third, pin each edge to its true place with good localization. A fourth aim often joins them. Give only one response per true edge. No thick bands. No double marks for one border.

Three aims plus one: Smooth to cut jitter. Differentiate to mark jumps. Localize to pin true place. Add single response so one border gives one line.

Picture jitter against a true cliff. The x-axis is position. The y-axis is brightness. Jitter looks like small grass along a flat field. A true edge looks like a cliff through the grass. Smoothing mows the grass but keeps the cliff. That is the whole job of the first Canny stage in short.

Phone cameras, scanners and medical sensors all add this jitter, so smoothing is routine before edge finding. A phone shot in low light, a scanned page and an MRI slice all get a blur pass first.

Scope: Smoothing helps when noise is small and local. It cannot fix large stains, motion blur trails or dead pixels. Those need repair or masking, not stronger blur.

Recap: Real numbers wobble, so raw gaps fire everywhere. Smooth first, then read gaps. Next we measure how small that wobble is in numbers.

4.4.2 Worked Examples

Worked jitter on a ramp top with reads 254 and 255: True top should be flat 255. Reads are 254, 255, 254, 253, 252. Gaps are \(255-254 = 1\), \(254-255 = -1\), \(253-254 = -1\), \(252-253 = -1\) and similar. Each gap is tiny next to a true edge gap of 255. But a naive threshold near zero would still fire on each 1 or minus 1. Smoothing pulls 254, 255, 254 toward a shared mean near 254 before gaps are taken. The small gaps then shrink toward zero. Answer: jitter gaps of size 1 fade after smoothing. Sense-check: a mean near 254 leaves almost no slope, so no edge should fire.

Smoothing is a local mean. It pulls each spike toward its neighbors. Lone spikes shrink fast. Long cliffs survive because both sides stay high or low.

Worked black zone jitter with no lone peak: Reads are 0, 10, 20, 30, 10, 0, 20, 30. Gaps are \(10-0 = 10\), \(20-10 = 10\), \(30-20 = 10\), \(10-30 = -20\) and so on. No gap stands out as a lone spike. There is no lone extremum to mark. A finder that seeks a lone peak finds none. That is correct, since no true border sits there. Smoothing makes this still more flat. The gaps then sit near zero. Answer: no edge, gaps near 10 with no peak. Sense-check: random ups and downs with no cliff must not mark an edge.

A mixed roof with noise ties the two lessons together. True shape is 0, 255, 0. Noisy shape may be 0, 254, 255, 254, 0. Gaps mix large true jumps with small noise jumps. Raw gaps show 254, 1, minus 1 and more. Smoothing first brings 254 and 255 close. The true rise still stands out. The small steps fade. Answer: true rise near 254 survives, noise steps near 1 drop.

4.4.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why not skip smoothing and just ignore small gaps by threshold? Why smooth rather than threshold small gaps away?

A: Small gaps can pile up or sit next to true gaps and shift the peak. Smoothing uses neighbor values to pull noise toward the local mean before any gap is taken. Threshold alone keeps the shifted peak place. Smoothing plus threshold keeps both size and place more true. Use blur first, then cut small gaps.

4.5 First-Order and Second-Order Derivatives in One Dimension

4.5.1 Mathematical Formulation

Hook: One subtraction finds a slope. A second subtraction finds where that slope starts and stops. Which one should an edge finder trust?

Let \(f(x)\) be brightness at integer spot \(x\). The rule for a first difference was next minus current. In symbols for the first difference next minus current along a single row:

\[ G(x) = f(x+1) - f(x) \]

Here \(G(x)\) is the first-order gap at \(x\). Here \(f(x)\) is brightness. Here \(x\) is the pixel index. \(G(x)\) tracks slope. A large \(|G(x)|\) marks a candidate edge. The sign tells rise or fall.

The second difference repeats the same idea on \(G\). The rule was difference of differences. In symbols for the second difference of differences tracking slope shifts:

\[ G2(x) = G(x+1) - G(x) \]

Here \(G2(x)\) is the second-order gap at \(x\). It tracks how fast slope itself shifts. Expand it once to see the three-pixel form:

\[ G2(x) = f(x+2) - 2f(x+1) + f(x) \]

It can sharpen faint edges because a small but steady climb yields a steady non-zero \(G\) and a jump in \(G2\) at start and end. The cost is more passes over data and more room for noise to grow.

First vs second: First order asks how steep. Second order asks how fast steepness itself changes. Steepness marks the edge. Change of steepness marks its start and end lines.

A 2D image is \(N\) such 1D rows stacked. Fix \(y\) at one row index to get one 1D array. Fix \(y = 10\) to get the row at 10. Fix \(y = 20\) to get a new row with its own 1D shape. A 3D volume is \(N\) such 2D slices stacked. That is why the 1D study maps straight to full images.

Picture the three curves for a bright bar. Brightness shows a plateau. First order shows a positive extremum at the left rim and a negative extremum at the right rim. Second order shows zero-crossings right at each rim. The x-axis is position in all three. The takeaway is one line. Extremums mark strength, zero-crossings mark place.

Scope: The three-pixel form assumes even spacing of 1 between pixels. It also assumes light smoothing first. Without smoothing, \(G2\) doubles noise because it mixes three noisy reads.

Recap: \(G\) finds slope extremums, \(G2\) finds zero-crossings at rim lines. Next we trace both on real number rows.

4.5.2 Worked Examples

Worked row jump 0 to 6 with minus 6 return gaps: Take a row with values 5, 5, 5, 5, then a jump. Gaps are \(5-5 = 0\) repeatedly. At the jump from 0 to 6 the gap is \(6 - 0 = 6\). After the top the gap back toward base is \(-6\). So the first difference shows 0, then 6, then 0, then minus 6, then 0. The 6 and minus 6 mark two edge sides. This matches the ramp plus step sketch used in class with jumps to 6 and 7. Answer: extremums 6 and -6. Sense-check: up then down must give opposite signs of equal size.

The same row shows why second order lifts faint rims.

Worked second difference lifting the jump zone: Take first gaps \(\dots, -1, -1, 6, -6, 0, \dots\). Next form gaps of gaps. Terms like \(-1 - (-1) = 0\) cancel to zero on flat runs. Near the jump, form each step:

\[ \begin{aligned} G2 &= G(x+1) - G(x) \\ &= 6 - (-1) = 7 \text{ at the rise} \\ &= -6 - 6 = -12 \text{ at the fall} \end{aligned} \]

The jump zone grows larger than its first-order size, with values near 7 and -12 in the illustration mixing 1, 0, 6, 12 and 6. That growth is why second order can lift faint edges above noise. The same growth also lifts noise, so smoothing matters more here. Answer: second-order spikes near 7 and -12 beat first-order 6. Sense-check: start and end of a climb must give the largest change of slope.

An ideal ramp 0 to 255 then back to 0 closes the set. First gap at rise is \(255 - 0 = 255\). Flat top gaps are 0. Fall gap is \(0 - 255 = -255\). The first difference shows extremums of 255 and minus 255. Absolute value gives edge strength 255 at both sides. That pair tells start and end of the bright band.

Exam note: Expect number work of this form where you form gaps step by step and read edge spots from extremums. Show each subtraction, circle the largest \(|G|\), and state rise or fall from the sign.

4.5.3 Why Practice Prefers First Order

Second order gives finer place data. It can mark the exact zero-cross spot of an edge. It can also lift small edges. Yet most edge finders and most deep nets stay with first order. The reason is cost and stability. Second order needs two passes and more memory holds. Training already takes weeks to months on large image sets with first order alone. Second order adds time and space load for small gain on edges. It shows up more in mid level tasks such as corner finding where place needs are stricter. For edges, first order plus smoothing is enough.

Cost rule: One first-order pass holds one gradient field. A second-order pass holds two fields and sweeps twice per update. Time and space both rise for a small edge gain.

A related speed trick replaces a slow Laplacian of Gaussian with a faster Difference of Gaussians for corner work. That trick keeps cost near order \(N\) for one pass rather than letting time and space blow up. Edge work avoids the issue by staying first order from the start.

Picture cost against gain. The x-axis is passes and memory. The y-axis is edge quality. First order sits high on quality at low cost. Second order sits a little higher on quality at twice the cost. The takeaway favors first order for edges.

Pitfalls: Learners often chase zero-crossings for every edge and drown in noise. Zero-crossings fire on every small wobble. Use them only after strong smoothing or for corner pinning.

Recap: First order wins for edges on cost and calm. Second order waits for corners. Next we lift the 1D gap into 2D vectors.

4.5.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Can a single 2D image have many 1D forms? Does each fixed row give one array?

A: Yes. Each fixed \(y\) gives one 1D row array. Row \(y = 10\) is one array. Row \(y = 20\) is a new array with its own jumps. A gray image is \(N\) such rows stacked. A color volume is \(N\) such gray slices. Each row or slice can be studied with the same gap math.

The cost doubt below needs the training view, not just one sweep.

Q: If second order is also order \(N\) per pass, why call it costly? Can we not fold it into the same loop?

A: One forward pass is still linear in pixel count. But learning needs both forward and backward passes plus stored maps. Two derivative stages mean two stored fields and two sweeps per update. Time and space both rise. For edge finding the gain is small, so designers keep first order. For corner tasks they accept the cost or use a faster Difference of Gaussians form.

4.6 Partial Derivatives, Magnitude and Direction

4.6.1 Mathematical Formulation

Hook: A border rarely runs straight along rows or columns. It cuts slantwise. How do two axis-only gaps join into one slant strength?

Let \(f(x,y)\) be brightness at column-row spot \((x,y)\). Let \(x\) run down rows in the book convention used here. Let \(y\) run across columns. The rule for gaps along each axis was next minus current with the other axis fixed. In symbols for partial gaps along x axis and y axis with the other fixed:

\[ G_x(x,y) = f(x+1,y) - f(x,y) \]

\[ G_y(x,y) = f(x,y+1) - f(x,y) \]

Here \(G_x\) is the gap along \(x\). Here \(G_y\) is the gap along \(y\). Here \(f(x,y)\) is image brightness, an integer such as 0 to 255. The phrase next minus current was kept next to each use so later checks can tie symbols back to words.

An edge rarely runs purely along one axis. Both gaps are joined into one strength and one angle. The rule was root of sum of squares for strength and inverse tan for angle. In symbols for magnitude root sum squares with angle inverse tan ratio:

\[ M(x,y) = \sqrt{G_x(x,y)^2 + G_y(x,y)^2} \]

\[ \alpha(x,y) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{G_y(x,y)}{G_x(x,y)}\right) \]

Here \(M\) is edge strength, a non-negative scalar. Here \(\alpha\) is edge direction, in degrees or radians. The pair \((G_x, G_y)\) forms a 2D vector at each pixel. Its length is \(M\). Its tilt from the \(x\) axis is \(\alpha\). The vector sum points across the fastest rise. Its normal runs along the border line.

Vector view: \(G_x\) and \(G_y\) are not two lone edges. They are parts of one gradient vector. Length gives strength. Inverse tan gives tilt. Keep the pair together.

Axis names are free. One may call down-rows \(x\) and across-columns \(y\). One may swap them. One may even use \(z\) for depth in 3D. The gap math stays the same. Only the reference from which \(\alpha\) is read shifts. A tilt read as minus 45 degrees from one origin reads as 135 degrees from the opposite side. Both name the same line direction. The key is to fix one convention for a whole task and keep it.

Padding handles borders. When \(x+1\) or \(y+1\) falls outside the array, add a rim of zeros or repeat edge values. Then the same gap formulas still apply at the rim. Zero padding is simple but darkens rims. Repeat padding copies the rim outward and keeps brightness level.

Scope: The inverse tan form assumes both gaps come from the same smoothing level. Mixed smoothing gives a tilted vector with wrong length. Smooth once, then form both gaps on the smoothed field.

Recap: Two axis gaps form one vector with length \(M\) and tilt \(\alpha\). Next we test the math on a 3-4-5 triangle.

4.6.2 Worked Examples

Worked corner values 50, 250 and 200 giving strength 250: Values are \(f(x,y) = 50\), \(f(x+1,y) = 250\), \(f(x,y+1) = 200\). The step says next minus current per axis. So \(G_x = 250 - 50 = 200\). So \(G_y = 200 - 50 = 150\). Strength is:

\[ M = \sqrt{200^2 + 150^2} \]

Now \(200^2 = 40000\). Now \(150^2 = 22500\). Their sum is 62500. The root is 250. This is the 3-4-5 triangle scaled by 50. Angle is:

\[ \alpha = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{150}{200}\right) = \tan^{-1}(0.75) \]

The ratio 150 over 200 reduces to 3 over 4. The live read stated this tilt as about 60 degrees. The standard table gives \(\tan^{-1}(0.75)\) as about 37 degrees, since \(\tan(37^\circ) \approx 0.75\) while \(\tan(60^\circ) \approx 1.73\). Keep both reads: the session value near 60 degrees and the reconciled value near 37 degrees. Use 37 degrees in later linking, and note the 60-degree call as a live angle-table slip. Answer: \(M = 250\), \(\alpha \approx 37^\circ\). Sense-check: a 3-4-5 triangle must give length 5 scaled, so 250 is exact.

The 3-4-5 link is worth memorizing. Any 150-200 pair must give 250. That spot-check catches square-and-add slips in one glance.

Worked binary border 0 and 2 giving strength 2 and angle 90: Dark cells hold 0. Light cells hold 2. At the border spot, \(f(x,y) = 0\), \(f(x+1,y) = 0\), \(f(x,y+1) = 2\). Then \(G_x = 0 - 0 = 0\). Then \(G_y = 2 - 0 = 2\). Strength is:

\[ M = \sqrt{0^2 + 2^2} = 2 \]

Angle uses \(\tan^{-1}(2/0)\). Division by zero points straight across the zero axis, read as 90 degrees. That matches the visual: brightness jumps straight across the border with no rise along the border itself. In code, guard the zero divide and return 90 degrees directly. Answer: \(M = 2\), \(\alpha = 90^\circ\). Sense-check: a pure \(y\) jump must point along \(y\).

Live annotation adds a warning. During live working the \(x\) and \(y\) arrows on one figure were drawn in swapped spots. That made values read as minus 2 in one pass and 2 in another. Axis arrows swapped, so signs need neighbor re-anchor before trusting them. The fix is to re-anchor which neighbor is \(x+1\) and which is \(y+1\) before subtracting. The gap idea itself did not change. Only the label of which gap is \(G_x\) and which is \(G_y\) needed repair. This moment shows why axis sketches must be checked before trusting signs.

4.6.3 What the Angle Tells About Shape

Angles chain into shape. Eye rims, cup rims and brain folds all curve. At each spot the local \(\alpha\) reports the local run direction. Angles chain into curves tracing shape rims in order. A run that reads 0 degrees, then 45 degrees, then 87 degrees, then 60 degrees is turning. Joined in order, those tilts trace the curve. Strength \(M\) says which links are solid. Angle \(\alpha\) says how they join. That is why finders keep both.

Linking rule: Keep a neighbor when its tilt sits within about 20 degrees of the run. Drop it when it flips by 90 degrees or more. Strength picks candidates, angle joins them.

A common mix-up is to treat \((G_x, G_y)\) as two lone edges. They are not lone. They are parts of one vector. The vector sum points across the fastest rise. Its normal runs along the border line. Keep that pair together when thinning and linking later. Splitting them breaks both thinning and hysteresis.

Picture a curve chart. The x-axis is position along the rim. The y-axis is local tilt. A smooth curve gives a smooth tilt line. A corner gives a sharp jump in the line. The takeaway is direct. Tilt plots show shape even when brightness plots look messy.

Pitfalls: Beginners read 135 degrees and minus 45 degrees as different lines. They name the same line from opposite ends. A 180-degree flip along one curve is normal. A 90-degree jump is not. Learn the 180-degree wrap before linking.

Recap: Strength finds links, angle chains them into rims. Next we turn these per-pixel gaps into fast kernels.

4.6.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why do we need inverse tan of \(G_y\) over \(G_x\)? What does it add beyond the two gaps?

A: The two gaps are axis parts. The vector sum gives the true rise direction beyond axis parts. Inverse tan of \(G_y\) over \(G_x\) recovers its tilt. Tilt tells which way the border runs at that pixel. Without tilt we know strength but not orientation. With tilt we can link border pieces into curves and shapes.

Thinning needs that tilt even more than linking does.

Q: Does the angle matter for finding edges, or only for later shape steps?

A: It matters for both. Thinning uses angle to keep only the peak across the rise and drop side lobes. Linking uses angle to join pieces that point the same way. A lens arc that reads near 10 degrees should link to neighbors near 10 degrees, not to a stray 180-degree flip. Angle guides cleanup as well as shape building.

Grid zero is the next common mix-up.

Q: How is 135 degrees read against the image grid? Where is zero?

A: Zero sits at the chosen \(x\) axis through the pixel origin. Tilt is read from that axis toward \(y\). If down-rows are \(x\) and across is \(y\), zero points down. A tilt of 135 degrees then points down-left. If axes swap, the same physical line gets a new number. Fix one frame for the whole job and read all tilts from it. Minus 45 degrees and 135 degrees name the same line from opposite ends.

The form stays put when tags swap.

Q: Why keep tan inverse of \(y\) over \(x\) even after swapping axis names?

A: The math step stays divide the \(y\) part by the \(x\) part. Names are tags for which gap is which. If tags swap, the numbers fed to the ratio swap too. The form \(\tan^{-1}(G_y/G_x)\) stays. What shifts is which neighbor difference fills \(G_x\) and which fills \(G_y\). That is why the live repair only re-tagged neighbors and kept the same ratio form.

The 3D case extends the same vector idea with one more axis.

Q: How would angle work in 3D with \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) gaps?

A: One tilt is not enough in 3D. A 3D vector needs two tilts or direction cosines against the three axes. The same idea scales up. Form \(G_x\), \(G_y\) and \(G_z\) by next minus current per axis. Join them into length \(\sqrt{G_x^2 + G_y^2 + G_z^2}\). Read orientation against the fixed \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) frame. Just track local rise direction per voxel at this stage.

4.7 Gradient Operators and Kernels

4.7.1 From Pixel Loops to Spatial Filters

Hook: Scanning each pixel twice is slow. What if nine pixels could be read in one step?

Pixel-by-pixel gaps scan each spot twice, once per axis. For an \(M\) by \(N\) image that is about \(M \times N\) steps per axis. Old hardware found that slow. Filters cut the passes by grouping neighbors.

The smallest filter mimics next minus current. The form was multiply by minus 1 and 1. In symbols for the \(x\) step:

\[ G_x \approx [-1 \quad 1] \star f \]

Here \(\star\) means local correlation at each spot. The filter \([-1 \quad 1]\) weights current by minus 1 and next by 1. Their sum is \(f(x+1,y) - f(x,y)\). A vertical \([-1, 1]\) form does the same along \(y\). Roberts filters extend this to 2 by 2 diagonal pairs for fast local response.

Filter view: A kernel is a small weighted window slid over the image. Weights pick the gap direction. One slide gives one gradient per center pixel.

Larger 3 by 3 filters take nine pixels at once. One pass then yields one \(G_x\) and one \(G_y\) per center pixel. That single pair feeds the same \(M\) and \(\alpha\) formulas. The same idea scales to learned kernels in deep nets. Each learned 3 by 3 kernel becomes a tuned edge finder for one tilt such as 45 degrees or 90 degrees or minus 45 degrees.

Picture the slide. The x-axis is window position. The y-axis is output gradient. Each stop multiplies nine numbers and adds them. The takeaway is speed. Nine reads collapse into one write.

Scope: Small 2 by 2 kernels assume clean data and fine detail. Noisy photos need wider 3 by 3 averaging first. Match kernel width to noise level.

Recap: Kernels turn two pixel loops into one window slide. Next we compare the three classic windows.

4.7.2 Prewitt, Sobel and Roberts Compared

Prewitt uses flat weights. The form was top row minus 1, middle row 0, bottom row 1 for the \(x\) pass. Label the 3 by 3 hood as:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} Z_1 & Z_2 & Z_3 \\ Z_4 & Z_5 & Z_6 \\ Z_7 & Z_8 & Z_9 \end{bmatrix} \]

Here each \(Z_i\) is one neighbor brightness with \(Z_5\) at center. Then Prewitt gaps for bottom minus top and right minus left sums are:

\[ G_x \approx (Z_7 + Z_8 + Z_9) - (Z_1 + Z_2 + Z_3) \]

\[ G_y \approx (Z_3 + Z_6 + Z_9) - (Z_1 + Z_4 + Z_7) \]

The first line contrasts bottom against top. The second contrasts right against left. Each sum spans three columns or three rows. That is why one pass covers nine pixels at once.

Prewitt rule: Add one side, add the other side, subtract. Flat 1 weights give wide view with no center stress.

Sobel keeps the same shape but weights the center of each side by 2. In symbols for Sobel center weight lift for faint step detection:

\[ G_x \approx (Z_7 + 2Z_8 + Z_9) - (Z_1 + 2Z_2 + Z_3) \]

The reason was signal lift. This center weight lift helps faint step detection. Fine edges such as 10 against 20 give a small gap of 10. Smoothing may shrink that gap toward 4. A weight of 2 on the middle row lifts the center contribution back up. Detection of faint steps gets better. Noise sensitivity drops a bit compared with flat weights. Place exactness can suffer a bit on very wide kernels. Users can test 5 by 5 or 7 by 7 Sobel forms for wider context.

Work a tiny case. Let top row be 10, 10, 10 and bottom row be 20, 20, 20. Prewitt \(G_x = 60 - 30 = 30\). Sobel \(G_x = (20 + 40 + 20) - (10 + 20 + 10) = 80 - 40 = 40\). The center lift adds 10 of signal. That margin can push a faint rim above threshold.

Roberts stays tiny at 2 by 2. It reacts fast at the exact jump spot. That gives good place data. But it has no averaging across three rows. Noise hits it harder. Detection of faint wide ramps is weaker. In short: Roberts gives good localization but weak noise handling. Prewitt gives wider view but flat weights stay noise prone. Sobel adds center lift for better detection with less noise hit but can blur place a bit.

Sobel and Prewitt finders ship in common vision libraries for quick border sketches before heavier models run. Phone edge previews and factory line checks often start with one Sobel pass.

Pitfalls: Picking Roberts for noisy outdoor shots gives speckle. Picking wide Sobel for thin wire art gives fat lines. Start with Sobel 3 by 3 on smoothed data, then switch only when the task map says so.

Recap: Roberts pins place, Prewitt widens view, Sobel lifts faint centers. Next we run them on a bird outline.

4.7.3 Worked Demo

Worked bird shape blur and Sobel vertical-horizontal outline: The live demo ran four stages on a bird shape. First, a Gaussian blur cut jitter. Pixelation visibly fell. Second, a sharpen pass restored border snap. Third, Sobel \(X\) kept vertical runs. Sobel \(Y\) kept horizontal runs. Fourth, the two maps were joined by size and angle math into one border map with \(M = \sqrt{G_x^2 + G_y^2}\). The final map traced the full outline from intensity jumps alone. That outline then serves as input for texture and pattern stages. Answer: blur, sharpen, Sobel X plus Y, then magnitude join gives the outline. Sense-check: vertical plus horizontal runs must close into a full bird rim.

Kernel size 3 was used in the demo with values of 1 and 0 patterns and center lift of 2 in Sobel form. The same code can swap in 5 by 5 forms. Larger size sees more context per step. It also smooths more. Users should test which size fits their image scale.

Exam note: Be ready to write Prewitt sums from hood labels \(Z_1\) through \(Z_9\) and to state which axis each sum tracks. Bottom minus top tracks \(x\). Right minus left tracks \(y\).

4.7.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: How do we move from 1D gaps to 3 by 3 kernels? Are we not skipping pixels with zeros in the middle row?

A: No pixels are skipped over a full sweep. The middle row of zeros only skips that row at one placement. The filter then slides by one. The prior middle row becomes top or bottom in the next placement. Every row serves as top, middle and bottom across the sweep. Nine-pixel math at each stop covers the whole image. Moving from gaps to kernels with a sliding sweep loses no rows.

Task choice is the next practical doubt.

Q: Is there a guide for which filter fits which task, for project work?

A: Start from goals. Need exact place on clean art-like lines, try Roberts. Need wider context on noisy photos, try Prewitt or Sobel. Need faint step lift, try Sobel with center 2. Always smooth first. Always test thresholds on your own data. Later Canny steps add thinning and linking on top of any of these gradient maps.

Noise lift is the natural follow-up.

Q: Does Sobel not lift noise too along with signal?

A: Yes, any lift can lift noise. That is why smoothing comes first. A Gaussian or median blur pulls lone spikes toward neighbor means before Sobel runs. Lone values such as a stray 10 among zeros shrink. True runs such as long 10 to 20 steps survive as runs. The order smooth then differentiate is the safe order.

The last doubt checks the big picture.

Q: So in short, is edge finding just first differences between neighbor boxes?

A: No, not yet at that point in the build. Gaps give candidate strength only. Full edge finding also needs smoothing, direction grouping, thinning to one-pixel lines and weak-strong linking. Those later Canny steps turn raw gap maps into clean borders. First differences are the start, not the full job.

4.8 Canny Edge Detection in Full

4.8.1 Mathematical Formulation

Hook: Raw gradients give thick messy bands. How do we shave them to one-pixel lines and keep only the true rims?

Canny joins four stages. The list was smooth, gradient, thin and link by double threshold. Each stage has a clear math form. This Canny smooth gradient thin hysteresis linking chain is the core of the lecture.

Stage 1 smooths with a Gaussian. The form was a bell set by spread sigma. In symbols with \(r^2 = u^2 + v^2\) for offset \((u,v)\) from center, for the Gaussian bell spread sigma smoothing weights:

\[ G(u,v) = K \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{r^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]

Here \(G(u,v)\) is the blur weight at offset \((u,v)\). Here \(\sigma\) is spread, a positive scalar in pixels. Here \(K\) is a normalizing scalar so weights sum to one. Here \(r\) is radial distance from the window center. Larger \(\sigma\) spreads weight wider and blurs more. A 3 by 3 example holds center near 0.6 and sides near 0.3 in unnormalized form before dividing by the total sum. For \(\sigma = 1\), raw values are 1 at center, about 0.61 on four sides and about 0.37 at corners. After dividing by the sum near 4.9, the normalized center is about 0.20. Convolve this bell with \(f\) to get smoothed \(f_s\).

Gaussian smoothing: Wide \(\sigma\) kills noise but softens faint rims. Narrow \(\sigma\) keeps detail but keeps speckle. Tune \(\sigma\) on task images.

Stage 2 forms strength and angle on \(f_s\) with any gradient kernel such as Sobel. The same formulas return:

\[ M = \sqrt{G_x^2 + G_y^2} \]

\[ \alpha = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{G_y}{G_x}\right) \]

Here \(G_x\) and \(G_y\) now come from kernel sums on \(f_s\), not lone pixel gaps. Each pixel holds a pair \((M, \alpha)\).

Stage 3 thins thick bands by non-maximum suppression. The rule was keep the peak along the rise and zero the rest. For each pixel, look along its \(\alpha\) line in a small hood. Keep its \(M\) only if it beats both neighbors on that line. Else set it to zero. Also group angles into shared bins. Values near 45 degrees stay together. A stray 145-degree value among 45-degree runs gets dropped. A thick band such as 10, 10, 10, 100, 10, 10 keeps only the 100 spot. The band shrinks to one pixel wide.

Non-maximum work needs angle bins. Common bins are 0, 45, 90 and 135 degrees. Each \(\alpha\) snaps to the nearest bin. The check then runs along that bin direction. That snap is why a 145-degree stray drops from a 45-degree run. Opposite ends wrap, so 0 and 180 share a bin.

Thinning rule: Compare along the gradient, not across it. A pixel survives only when it is the local top of its own slope line.

Stage 4 links by double threshold with hysteresis. Pick a low threshold \(T_L\) and a high threshold \(T_H\). The bands were below 100 drop, 100 to 200 weak, above 200 keep. In symbols, for each thinned \(M\):

\[ \text{strong if } M > T_H \]

\[ \text{weak if } T_L \le M \le T_H \]

\[ \text{drop if } M < T_L \]

Keep all strong spots. Keep weak spots only if joined by a chain of weak spots to a strong spot. Drop lone weak spots and all dropped spots. This hysteresis keeps faint but linked borders while cutting stray texture marks such as path stones.

The two key knobs are \(\sigma\) and the pair \((T_L, T_H)\). Small \(\sigma\) keeps fine detail plus noise. Large \(\sigma\) cuts noise but can lose faint rims. Low thresholds keep more detail plus clutter. High thresholds keep only bold rims. Tune both on sample images from the task at hand.

Scope: Double thresholds assume strong rims exist to anchor weak fills. Images with only faint rims and no strong anchor need lower \(T_H\) or wider \(\sigma\) retuning. Blind defaults break them.

Recap: Smooth with a bell, read \(M\) and \(\alpha\), thin to peaks, link weak to strong. Next we trace each stage on numbers.

4.8.2 Worked Examples

Worked thick band thinning keeping peak 100 at angle 45: After stages 1 and 2 a border reads as magnitudes 10, 10, 10, 100, 10, 10, 10 across seven pixels with angles 45, 45, 45, 45, 45, 145, 45 degrees. Non-maximum suppression scans along 45 degrees. The 100 beats its 10 neighbors so it stays. The 10 spots lose to the 100 or to each other and go to zero. The stray 145-degree spot also loses because its angle bin differs from the run. The seven-wide band becomes one pixel at the 100 spot with angle 45 degrees. Answer: one pixel kept at 100. Sense-check: one true rim must give one line, not seven.

Eyelash grouping shows the same bin logic on curves.

Worked eyelash flipped spot dropped keeping dominant tilt: The lash run reads near 10 degrees at most spots. One spot reads 180 degrees flipped. The eyelash flipped spot is dropped keeping the dominant tilt of 10 degrees. The 10-degree run is kept as dominant. The flipped spot is zeroed. The reason is physical. Neighbor lash pieces cannot flip by 180 degrees in one step. Close spots on one curve must hold close tilts, up to wrap. Answer: keep 10-degree run, drop the flip. Sense-check: hair cannot U-turn in one pixel.

Linking closes gaps that thinning leaves.

Worked threshold linking with weak spots joined to strong 250: Let \(T_L = 90\) and \(T_H = 190\). Spots read 110, 250, 100, 0, 100, 100, 110 in a chain. The 250 exceeds 190 so it is strong. The 110 and 100 values sit between 90 and 190 so they are weak. The 0 sits below 90 so it drops. Weak spots joined to the 250 through weak chains stay. Isolated weak spots with no path to any strong spot go. Final kept chain traces the true rim with gaps closed. Answer: keep 250 plus joined 110 and 100 fills, drop 0 and lone fills. Sense-check: a rim with one bright anchor can bridge small faint gaps.

A path clutter cut shows the other side. A walkway image shows many small stone borders. Their \(M\) values sit low or form lone weak spots with no strong anchor. Double threshold drops them. The main object rim holds strong spots plus joined weak fills. It stays as a clean outline.

4.8.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: How are the low and high thresholds picked for varied image sets? How are low high thresholds picked for varied sets?

A: They are task knobs, not fixed laws. Start with a mid pair such as 100 and 200 on smoothed gradients. View kept strong maps and kept weak-linked maps. If faint rims break, lower \(T_L\) a bit. If clutter floods in, raise \(T_L\) or \(T_H\). If blur wipes detail, lower \(\sigma\). Task knobs are tuned on sample images locking sigma thresholds before final runs.

Cause labeling needs more than edges.

Q: What remains unsolved by edges alone that needs line or corner stages?

A: Edges mark jumps but do not label causes. Light cuts, paint marks, depth gaps and true shape rims all form jumps. Some true rims also break into dashes. Line finding joins dashes into straight runs. Corner finding pins joins where direction snaps. Those mid level steps sit after Canny and use its thinned maps as input.

4.9 Practical Tricks and Limits

4.9.1 Mathematical Formulation

Hook: Smoothing the whole photo and then taking slopes costs two full sweeps. Can one tiny slope-of-blur do both?

Smoothing then differentiating costs two sweeps. The trick folds them into one. The rule was differentiate the bell once, then convolve once. In symbols with \(f\) as image and \(G\) as Gaussian, for the derivative Gaussian trick saving a full image sweep with a single convolution sweep:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(f \star G) = f \star \frac{\partial G}{\partial x} \]

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(f \star G) = f \star \frac{\partial G}{\partial y} \]

Here \(\star\) means convolution. The left side smooths the full image then takes the gap. The right side takes the gap of the small 3 by 3 bell once, then convolves that small result with the image. Both give the same smoothed gradient up to border handling. The right side is much cheaper. A 128 by 128 image needs 16384 gap reads per pass. A 3 by 3 bell needs 9 reads to form its slope once. Modern nets use the same idea when they learn small kernels and slide them.

One-sweep trick: Build slope-of-bell once on 9 pixels. Slide it over \(f\). Skip the full-image smooth pass. Math stays equal because convolution and differentiation commute.

Picture cost bars. Two-sweep bar is tall. One-sweep bar is short. The x-axis is method. The y-axis is pixel reads. The takeaway is savings of roughly image size over kernel size.

Worked cost saving on 128 by 128: Two sweeps read about \(128 \times 128 = 16384\) pixels twice, near 32768 reads plus writes. One-sweep form reads 9 bell pixels once to build \(\partial G / \partial x\), then slides that 3 by 3 over 16384 spots, about 147456 multiplies but only one image pass with no second field held. Memory drops from two full fields to one field plus one 3 by 3. Answer: roughly half the passes and one fewer full map held. Sense-check: tiny-kernel precompute must beat full-image re-smoothing.

Scope: The swap holds for linear shift-even filters with matched borders. Odd borders or non-linear steps like median break the match. Use repeat padding on both paths when comparing.

Recap: Slope the tiny bell, slide once, save a sweep. Next we face what edges still cannot tell.

4.9.2 When Edges Fail

Edges alone cannot tell cause. Four common causes all form jumps. Light cuts form jumps at shadow rims. Paint shifts form jumps at red to blue borders. Depth gaps form jumps at occluding rims. Surface bends form jumps at folds. Some jumps are wanted shape. Some are clutter. Later stages must prune by context, motion, stereo or learned priors. Line and corner detectors help by testing which jumps line up into stable geometry.

Signal chain limits also matter. Heavy blur kills faint roofs. Tight thresholds cut weak but true links. Wide kernels shift place a bit. Always check \(\sigma\) and thresholds together. View strong-only maps against strong-plus-linked-weak maps. The gap between them shows what linking saved.

Cause gap: Jumps mark where, not why. Shadow, paint, depth gap and fold all share the same \(M\) spike. Meaning comes from grouping, motion or learning, not from one pixel.

A red-blue paint border and a depth rim can share the same 200-strength spike. Only context tells them apart. The paint border sits flat in stereo. The depth rim shifts with viewpoint. That extra test is mid level vision.

Pitfalls: Treating every strong rim as object shape floods maps with shadows and paint. Treating every weak rim as noise drops true faint folds. Keep the strong-plus-linked view and prune by shape, not by strength alone.

Recap: \(M\) finds jumps, context names causes. The closing doubt asks why the one-sweep path is safe.

4.9.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why multiply by the derivative of the Gaussian rather than smooth then differentiate in two steps? Why use the derivative Gaussian in one sweep rather than two steps?

A: Both paths give the same smoothed slope. The one-step path builds \(\partial G/\partial x\) and \(\partial G/\partial y\) once on the tiny bell. It then slides those tiny patterns over \(f\) in a single convolution sweep. That saves a full-image second sweep. For large images the saving is large. For learned nets the same saving applies to small kernels.

Key Industry Applications

ResNet, VGG and Inception use staged edge to texture to pattern to part design for classification and detection. Phone galleries, retail search and traffic cameras all copy this order for fast ranking before heavy models run.

Gaussian blur, median blur and Sobel filters in vision libraries give quick smoothing and border maps for phones, scanners and factory cameras. A single Sobel pass sketches weld lines, print defects and lane marks in milliseconds.

Brain MRI rim finding uses step, ramp and roof models to trace skull, folds and mass borders, with local jumps near value gaps such as 255 to 50 marking mass rims for later review. Radiologists keep the strong-plus-linked view and pass kept rims to segmentation tools.
