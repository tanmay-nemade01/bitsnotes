16.1 Recap: Where Optical Flow Tracking Breaks

16.1.1 Methods carried forward

Hook: A camera sees only a grid of brightness values, yet a tracker must say "the same car moved from here to there." How much motion can be read from shifting brightness alone, and where does that reading stop working?

Tracking in this lecture starts from three earlier ideas. Background subtraction separates moving pixels from a fixed backdrop: anything that differs from the learned background counts as foreground. Detection as tracking runs a detector on each frame in turn and links the boxes across time, so tracking is only as fast and as steady as the detector. Optical flow takes a third path: it estimates how brightness patterns shift between frames, giving a motion vector at each pixel without any detector at all.

Two classic optical-flow formulations frame the discussion. The Lucas-Kanade method is a local method: it solves for motion inside a small window around each point, trusting only nearby structure. The Horn-Schunck method is a global method: it adds a smoothness term over the whole image, so motion spreads evenly across regions. The trade-off is worth holding in mind for the rest of the lecture:

Dimension Lucas-Kanade (local) Horn-Schunck (global)
Region used Small window per point Whole image at once
Smooth areas Stays quiet, reports nothing Fills in smooth motion
Edges and texture Follows local structure May smear across boundaries
Cost Small solve per window Large joint solve

Lucas-Kanade in one line: inside each small window, assume every pixel shares one motion vector and brightness stays constant, then solve the resulting small least-squares system for that vector. The small-motion assumption sits underneath: the shift between frames must fit inside the window, or the equations stop matching the right pixels.

A pyramid representation softens the small-motion demand by solving coarse-to-fine: large motion at full resolution looks small at coarse resolution, so the estimate from the top level seeds the search below. Even so, very large jumps between frames still escape it, and the brightness-constancy demand never goes away.

16.1.2 Failure modes that force new tools

Several hard limits of optical flow were listed, and they motivate everything that follows. The aperture problem asks which regions can even be tracked. A corner carries intensity change in two directions, so its motion is pinned down and Lucas-Kanade works. An edge carries intensity change across only one direction, so motion along the edge stays unknown and no local window can resolve it. A flat, uniform region gives no gradient at all, so nothing locks on — a slow cloud drifting against a flat sky is the running image here, all motion and no texture to hold.

Three more weaknesses followed. The method reacts badly to lighting shifts and sensor noise, since every equation leans on constant brightness. It cannot handle occlusion: when an object passes behind a pillar, the brightness match breaks and the track dies instead of waiting. And it cannot follow very large jumps between frames — a drone or a missile closing in fast is the motivating picture, and even a pyramid can land far from the true position. What is missing is a stochastic view of motion, one that admits uncertainty instead of assuming a clean brightness match.

Scope: Optical flow applies when brightness stays nearly constant, motion between frames stays small, the target stays visible, and the window holds enough texture. Break any one of these — relight the scene, jump too far, hide the object, flatten the texture — and the method degrades without warning, because it has no notion of doubt.

Picture the failure as a landscape. Plot image position on the horizontal axis and matching confidence on the vertical axis. A corner gives a sharp peak: one clear best motion. An edge gives a long ridge: every point along the edge scores equally, so the peak never forms. A flat sky gives a tabletop: every motion scores the same and the tracker wanders. The takeaway in one line is that brightness matching can only report motion where texture gives it something to grip.

Recap: Brightness-based flow tracks corners well, guesses along edges, and fails on flat regions, lighting change, occlusion, and large jumps. The next two tools answer those failures directly: mean shift drops the brightness-constancy demand by following color mass, and Kalman filtering replaces blind prediction with a predict-and-repair loop that prices in noise.

In deployed systems the same limits show up daily. Driver-assistance cameras lose lane texture on fresh snow, video stabilization wobbles when a hand crosses the lens, and sports trackers drop sprinters who cross behind hoardings. Each case replays this section's list, which is why production pipelines pair flow with the histogram and filtering tools built next.

16.2 Mean-Shift Tracking with Color Histograms

16.2.1 Mode seeking with a color histogram

Hook: A walking person bends, stretches, and swings their arms, so no fixed template matches them from frame to frame. What property of the person stays put while their shape keeps changing?

Mean shift is a mode-seeking procedure — it climbs toward the densest point of a distribution. A mode (the most common value, the tallest bump of a histogram) is the peak the climber hunts. The same procedure earlier served segmentation as mean-shift clustering; here it serves tracking, and the core idea does not change.

The tracked object gets a color histogram as its signature. Say a detector has boxed a tank in the first frame. Build a histogram of that box: count how many pixels fall in each color bin, so the full color mix becomes one curve. As the tank rolls forward, its paint does not change, so the histogram peak — the mode — travels with it. Each frame, the tracker climbs to the mode and calls that location the new position. Think of it as chasing the tallest bump of color as it slides across the image: the bump keeps its shape while its address changes.

This design drops the brightness-constancy demand of optical flow. Up to a point, lighting drift is tolerated, because the whole color mass is followed rather than exact pixel values. Its stated purpose is non-rigid objects — a walking person, a moving hand, a robot — shapes that bend and stretch so no fixed template fits. A tree that never moves gives nothing to follow, while a walking person constantly reshapes yet keeps a stable color mix. The color spread of a person — head above, torso below, laid out along horizontal and vertical axes — is the attribute under pursuit.

A kernel density estimator (KDE) is the climbing device. Picture scattered intensity points along an axis. Drop a kernel window onto a start point, average the points inside, move the center to that average, and repeat. The window drifts toward the densest cluster the way a marble rolls to the bottom of a bowl — except here the marble rolls uphill toward the densest pile. Where the marble picture breaks: a marble feels the true slope, while mean shift only sees the points inside its window, so a window parked too far from any pile never feels the pull. A Gaussian kernel is the usual pick, though other shapes are allowed. Convergence is declared when the mean stops moving between rounds — the window has settled on the center of mass of the densest region. Several kernels can be started in different spots so that separate modes each get found; each kernel owns an attraction basin that drains toward its peak.

The window rule: with bandwidth \(h\) (the half-width of the window, a fixed number such as \(h = 6\)) and current center \(x_0\) (the window's present address on the axis), the window spans from \(x_0\) minus \(h\) to \(x_0\) plus \(h\):

\[[x_0 - h,\; x_0 + h]\]

Every point inside this span votes in the next average; every point outside is ignored for this round.

Plot the idea as a chart to fix it. Put intensity on the horizontal axis and point density on the vertical axis. Three dots sit at 20, 22, and 27, with a tall bump near 21 and a smaller shoulder near 27. The window is a sliding frame: each round it recenters on the average of the dots it covers, so the frame walks right until the average stops shifting. The takeaway is that the walk always ends at the densest pile the starting frame could reach.

16.2.2 Worked one-dimensional example

Worked example — climbing from 20 to the mode. Three intensity points are given:

\[x \in \{20, 22, 27\}\]

The bandwidth is fixed at \(h = 6\), and the window rule above decides who votes each round.

Round 1. Start at \(x_0 = 20\). The window is \([20 - 6,\; 20 + 6] = [14,\; 26]\). Check each point: 20 lies inside, 22 lies inside, 27 lies outside. Average the captured points:

\[x_1 = \frac{20 + 22}{2} = \frac{42}{2} = 21\]

Round 2. Recenter on \(x_1 = 21\) with the same bandwidth. The window is \([21 - 6,\; 21 + 6] = [15,\; 27]\). Check each point: 20, 22, and 27 all lie inside (27 sits exactly on the rim and counts as captured). Average them:

\[x_2 = \frac{20 + 22 + 27}{3} = \frac{69}{3} = 23\]

Round 3. Recenter on \(x_2 = 23\). The window is \([23 - 6,\; 23 + 6] = [17,\; 29]\). All three points are captured again, so the average is unchanged:

\[x_3 = \frac{20 + 22 + 27}{3} = 23\]

Since \(x_2 = x_3\), the means have converged and the climb stops: the mode estimate is 23.

Sense-check: 23 sits between the pair at 20–22 and the lone point at 27, closer to the pair because two votes beat one — exactly where the densest pile should be. In two dimensions the same rounds run on \(x\) and \(y\) together, with a rectangle or ellipse window instead of an interval.

A second tiny case builds trust in the rule. Start instead at \(x_0 = 27\). The window is \([21, 33]\), which holds only 27 (22 falls just outside), so the average stays 27 and the climb ends at once on the smaller shoulder. Same rule, different basin, different peak — which is why the starting point decides which mode is found.

16.2.3 Frame-to-frame tracking procedure

Purpose: follow a non-rigid target across video frames by chasing its color-histogram mode, without any brightness-constancy assumption and without a detector running on every frame.

Inputs and outputs: the input is a video stream plus one reference box from a detector in a reference frame. The working parameters are the histogram binning, the kernel shape with its bandwidth \(h\), and the neighborhood radius that bounds each search. The output per frame is the new box center — the state carried into the next frame.

Steps — the tracking loop:

  1. Fix a reference frame in which the detector has already boxed the target, such as a hand gripping a paddle.
  2. Build the color histogram of that box and note its mode; this frozen histogram is the signature.
  3. In the next frame, start the kernel at the old position and search only its neighborhood — a hand cannot teleport across the country between frames, so for a 240 by 240 frame the motion range stays small.
  4. Climb to the nearest mode with the mean-shift rounds from the worked example.
  5. Score each candidate window with a similarity function against the reference histogram, and keep the window that matches best.
  6. Store the new position as the state and repeat for every following frame.

Trace — the paddle hand across two frames. Frame one boxes the hand and records its histogram: mostly skin tones with a dark paddle stripe. In frame two the hand has slid ten pixels right. The kernel starts at the old center, its neighborhood covers the shifted hand, and two mean-shift rounds walk the window onto the new histogram peak. The similarity score against the reference histogram confirms the match, and the new center becomes the state for frame three. The mode stays put in histogram space even as the hand moves in image space.

Running the loop costs little: each round averages only the points inside one small window, and the search never scans the whole frame. That is why mean shift fits cheap, fast trackers on modest hardware.

16.2.4 Student questions and answers

Q: We start at 20 with bandwidth 6 over three intensity points. How does the window average move toward the mode, step by step?

A: Fix the bandwidth at 6 and start at 20. The first window is 14 to 26, which holds 20 and 22, so the new average center is 21. The second window is 15 to 27, which holds all three points, so the new average is 23. The third window is 17 to 29, which again holds all three, so the center keeps the value 23. Equal successive centers mean the mode is reached.

16.2.5 Limits of mean shift

Mean shift shares some old frailties, and naming them sets up the Kalman filter. It cannot see through occlusion or survive a distracting look-alike, because the mode it wants is hidden or doubled — two identical tanks give two equal peaks and the climber cannot pick the right one. Fast motion blurs the object, so no sharp mode forms inside the kernel window and the average wanders. A sudden bright light that flattens intensities into a uniform wash also defeats it: a flat histogram has no peak to climb, exactly the tabletop failure from the optical-flow recap.

Scope: Mean shift applies when the target keeps a stable color signature, moves only within the neighborhood between frames, and stays visible against a distinct background. It breaks under occlusion, twin-like distractors, heavy motion blur, and washed-out lighting. These are assumption failures, not coding mistakes: no bandwidth setting rescues a peak that is gone or doubled.

Recap: Mean shift trades pixel-exact matching for color-mass climbing: build a histogram signature, walk each window to its local mode, and carry the peak across frames. It handles bending shapes and mild lighting drift, but blind occlusion and look-alikes still kill it. The bridge to the next tool is uncertainty itself — the Kalman filter keeps a running belief about position and repairs each prediction with fresh sightings instead of trusting any single peak.

Hand-gesture interfaces use exactly this loop to follow a palm across a webcam feed, and sports analysts reuse it to shadow a player's shirt color between detector passes. In each case the signature is cheap, the search stays local, and a detector re-anchors the track before drift builds up.

16.3 Kalman Filtering for Tracking in Noisy Worlds

16.3.1 Predict-then-correct loop

Hook: A delivery drone is told to fly straight at a fixed speed, but wind gusts push it around and its own sensors lie a little. How can a tracker stay locked on when every prediction and every sighting is slightly wrong?

A Kalman filter is a recursive estimator of a system's state. A state (the bundle of physical quantities being tracked — position, speed, direction, and anything else that shapes motion) is revised a little with each new frame, never computed from scratch. The filter works in a two-beat loop: predict where the object should be, then correct the prediction against what is actually seen. Earlier methods only predict; when a prediction misses, nothing repairs it. Kalman filtering repairs it, which is why it survives noisy settings that defeat optical flow and mean shift.

The drone picture makes the need concrete. A delivery drone is modeled as flying straight at some speed, yet headwinds slow it, tailwinds speed it, battery sag shortens its range from 100 kilometers to 80, and evasive turns bend its path. None of the earlier trackers price in wind, drag, air density, stalls, or rolls. Kalman filtering treats every measurement as noisy and keeps updating its parameters, so a miss becomes information instead of failure. An object moving at 100 kilometers per hour is first predicted ahead at that speed; when it actually appears farther along because it sped to 150, the filter absorbs the difference and re-predicts at the new speed.

16.3.2 Linear Gaussian motion model

The motion model: take the position \(x_t\) (the address of the object at time \(t\)), add the distance covered since the last frame — velocity \(\dot{x}_t\) (the speed at time \(t\)) times the time step \(\Delta t\) (the gap between frames) — and add Gaussian noise \(w_t\) (a random push standing in for every unmodeled force: wind shifts, drag, viscosity, and the rest):

\[x_{t+1} = x_t + \dot{x}_t \Delta t + w_t\]

Read it in plain words: next position equals current position plus velocity times time, plus a random nudge. With numbers: at \(x_t = 0\) meters, flying at \(\dot{x}_t = 100\) kilometers per hour with \(\Delta t = 1\) hour and no noise, the prediction is \(x_{t+1} = 0 + 100 \times 1 = 100\) kilometers downrange. When the sighting instead lands at 150, the 50-kilometer gap feeds the correction round.

This scalar form is the constant-velocity special case of the standard textbook model, which stacks position, velocity, and any other quantities into one state vector \(x_k\) and steps it forward through a transition matrix \(A_k\) with process noise \(w_k\), written \(x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + w_k\), while each sighting \(z_k\) reads the state through a measurement matrix \(H_k\) with its own noise \(v_k\), written \(z_k = H_k x_k + v_k\). Both wordings describe the same idea; the session uses the scalar position-plus-velocity wording, and the matrix wording is what larger implementations build on.

The noise term is deliberately vague: it need not be named as wind or drag, only modeled as Gaussian spread. Because the dynamics stay linear — a headwind subtracts speed, a tailwind adds it, passing trains separate faster — the whole system runs on small matrices rather than deep networks. The filter first predicts the next position from current position and velocity, then reads the fresh velocity and measurement, refines its parameters, and predicts again, sharper each round. If a predicted position misses, the update round moves the estimate and the next prediction at \(t + 2\) lands closer.

Imagine the uncertainty as a chart. Put time on the horizontal axis and position on the vertical axis. The prediction is a line marching forward, wrapped in a widening band: the farther ahead, the wider the doubt. Each fresh sighting pinches the band narrow again at the observed point, then it starts widening toward the next step. The takeaway is that the filter never claims certainty — it carries its doubt along and lets each measurement shrink it.

16.3.3 Observe, estimate, predict cycle

The working rhythm has three named beats. Observe: watch the system over some span \(k\) and gather noisy measurements \(z\) with their uncertainty \(R\). Estimate: fit the parameters — the state \(x\), its covariance \(P\) that records how quantities like speed and wind push the outcome around, the state-transition matrix \(A\), and the process-noise matrix \(Q\). Predict: project state and measurement forward to \(k + 1\). Then observe again, compare prediction with sighting, fold the gap back through the Kalman gain into fresh estimates, and predict once more. The loop never stops: observe, estimate, predict, observe again.

The Kalman gain in one line: the gain \(K\) (a weighting number between trusting the prediction and trusting the sighting) blends the two sources by their reliability — steady sensors earn a high gain on sightings, wild sensors keep the weight on the prediction. Each round recomputes it from the current covariances, so the blend adapts as doubt grows or shrinks.

Real-world: bird flight through a forest is occluded by trees and bends into dives; missile blips arrive every few seconds along zigzag paths; planets hide behind solar and lunar phases. All three are tracked this way — infer the state from noisy sightings over time, bridging the blind spans with the motion model.

Real-world: placement of payload release from aircraft was raised through a war film about bombing runs — calibrating release under wind, speed, and drag noise is exactly the multi-parameter problem Kalman machinery addresses, and air forces still drill it as applied estimation.

16.3.4 Student questions and answers

Q: The filter adds Gaussian noise, yet it is called linear. How does it handle truly unpredictable events — a sudden turn, or an occlusion that can start at any moment?

A: Occlusion is handled by coasting: if the object should be behind a tree at time \(t + 1\) and reappear at \(t + 2\) with parameters unchanged, the filter bridges the blind span on prediction alone and picks the object up again. A sudden direction change is handled by fast repair. Tracking along the x-axis, the filter predicts straight ahead; when the drone actually jumps to the y-axis, that sighting is a noisy miss, the update round rotates the displacement vector by 90 degrees, and the next prediction follows the new heading. Each fresh turn triggers the same re-aim, and because displacement math is linear, the repair lands within a few rounds.

Q: Are the several Kalman filters fixed in advance, and how many do we use? Also, what exactly is the difference between observe, estimate, and predict?

A: The count is a design choice tied to compute budget. A common offline setup uses three filters, one per axis: forward motion, lateral yaw, and climb or descent — each run under near-linear models such as constant velocity or constant acceleration. Heavier setups add more filters for more parameters. On the second point: observe means collect noisy measurements over a span; estimate means fit the state, covariances, and matrices from those sightings; predict means project state and measurement forward to the next step. Then the loop compares, corrects through the Kalman gain, and goes again.

Q: Can a Kalman filter sit after deep-network trackers — detect with a network, then filter the boxes? Is that beneficial?

A: It can be built that way, and researchers do try it for exotic targets such as electron tracks in cloud-chamber images, where tiny specialized detections need smoothing. In day-to-day industrial practice the answer was no: conventional trackers already give the needed speed, and a deep tracker only adds latency without a payoff worth paying.

16.3.5 Combined filters and guidance

No single filter covers a maneuvering craft, so practice stacks them. IMM filters (interacting multiple models) run a bank of Kalman filters side by side — some assuming constant velocity, others constant acceleration — and fuse their outputs in one compute unit that steers the craft. Missile guidance literature works the same way: model interceptor velocity, line-of-sight geometry, and lateral acceleration commands per scenario, then combine, because a missile that cruises, then weaves, then dives fits no single straight-line story. The Kalman filter is the building block inside each channel.

Exam note: only the conceptual predict-correct cycle belongs to the syllabus; the matrix derivations do not. Expect questions on the cycle — what is observed, what is estimated, what the gain corrects — never the matrix arithmetic.

16.3.6 Matrix walkthrough beyond the syllabus

For readers who want the full machinery, the session walked through the matrices at whiteboard level: start from measurement \(z_k\) with noise \(R_k\) at time \(t_k\); form state \(x\) and covariance \(P\); propagate through the transition matrix; compute the Kalman gain that weighs prediction against sighting; and land on the updated state-error covariance. The algebra itself was flagged as outside the syllabus and left for self-study with a simplification guide, so no derivation steps are reproduced here.

Recap: The Kalman filter replaces blind prediction with a doubt-carrying loop — model motion as position plus velocity with Gaussian noise, observe noisy sightings, fold each gap back through the gain, and predict again. It coasts through occlusion, re-aims after sharp turns, and stacks into IMM banks for maneuvering craft. The bridge onward is deployment: the next section weighs this classical machinery against deep trackers on speed, and shows the detect-then-track rhythm that keeps real streams in real time.

Scope: The filter applies when motion is near-linear and noise is near-Gaussian — cruising drones, coasting aircraft, orbiting bodies. It degrades under strongly non-linear moves and heavy clutter, where particle filters or deep appearance models take over. A sudden long occlusion with a maneuver hidden inside it is the classic break: the coasted prediction exits the blind span pointing the wrong way.

16.4 Tracking in Practice: Latency, Pipelines, and Edge Devices

16.4.1 Detect-then-track pipeline

Hook: A detector that reads 45 frames per second faces a camera that delivers 90. Half the frames would go uncovered — unless something cheap fills the gaps.

Detectors are slow and trackers are fast, so production systems alternate them. Detect once, track cheaply for several frames, then detect again to re-anchor. The arithmetic from the session: a detector clearing 45 frames per second cannot cover a 90-frame-per-second stream — 45 frames go uncovered. Human persistence of vision sits near 1/16 of a second, so motion barely changes across a few frames. Detect on frame one, track the next five to ten, re-detect around frame ten or twelve, and the stream processes in near real time with only milliseconds spent per tracked frame.

The pipeline rule: an expensive detector sets the anchor, a cheap tracker (mean shift, Kalman, or a sorter) carries the boxes between anchors, and each re-detection resets the drift before it compounds. The re-detection interval is the tuning knob: shorter costs more compute, longer risks more drift.

That alternating rhythm is also how modern YOLO-family detectors fold tracking into sorting across frames: each detection pass re-labels and re-anchors, while the light tracker keeps identities alive in between. The same pattern drives traffic cameras that detect plates every few frames and track them between, and retail counters that detect shoppers at the door and follow them down the aisles.

16.4.2 Deep trackers and the latency objection

DeepSORT and FairMOT are deep-network trackers: they model non-linear appearance well and beat classical methods on raw accuracy. Yet tracking cares about speed first and accuracy second — a box slightly off target still seeds the next detection fine. Deep trackers raise pipeline latency for precision nobody asked for, so working engineers keep classical trackers in the loop.

Dimension Classical trackers (Kalman, mean shift, sorters) Deep trackers (DeepSORT, FairMOT)
Accuracy Good enough on boxes Higher on hard appearance
Latency Milliseconds per frame Heavier pass per frame
Compute Runs on modest boards Wants bigger GPUs
Pick when Real-time streams, re-detection covers drift Appearance is the hard part, budget allows

Real-world: beating-heart surgery uses tracked valve motion under image guidance rather than a heavy network, because the loop must stay fast — a late box is worse than a slightly loose one. Research corners differ — electron tracks in cloud chambers may justify deep smoothing — but that is the exception.

Exam note: deep trackers are outside the syllabus entirely. Know their names and the one-line reason they lose in production (latency), and spend revision time on the classical loop instead.

16.4.3 Student questions and answers

Q: What is the common practice — deep networks for tracking, or Kalman-style classical methods? And with 45 FPS detection against a 90 FPS stream, how does tracking close the gap with a detect pipeline?

A: Classical methods carry production work; deep trackers are known but unused in the speaker's industrial experience because they inflate latency. The gap closes by detecting once and tracking the next stretch: with persistence of vision near 1/16 of a second, the scene barely moves across five to ten frames, so re-detecting every fifth to tenth frame holds the track while the pipeline keeps pace with 90 FPS input.

Q: How does a newcomer start with computer vision on small boards such as Raspberry Pi or a Jetson-style device instead of only running notebook code?

A: Learn deployment formats first: training saves PyTorch weight files, but edge boards want converted formats such as ONNX, so convert and flash. Start with a cheap board such as a Raspberry Pi, plug in a simple CMOS camera over USB, and run a tiny detector such as nano YOLO rather than a large one, trading parameters for memory. Then practice the surrounding stack — memory budgets, calibration, and before building, the ROS robotics operating system with its Gazebo simulator. A dedicated robotics course covering deep reinforcement learning is the recommended next step, and modern code assistants make the bring-up fast, though the theory has to be learned first.

16.4.4 Edge and robotics pointers

Real-world: phone camera teams ship bokeh and semantic-segmentation features built on these vision ideas, and SLAM pipelines reuse the same tracking instincts — anchor with a heavy pass, carry with a light one. Real-world: hobby quadcopters get built from cheap parts and tuned with the same calibration thinking — camera geometry first, then flight control. The through-line is deployment under limits: small memory, small models, simulated testing before real flight.

Scope: Edge deployment applies when the model fits the board's memory and the frame budget survives conversion — nano-scale detectors, converted formats, calibrated cameras. It breaks when a notebook-sized model is flashed as-is: memory overflows, frames crawl, and no tracker tuning recovers the gap. Simulate in Gazebo and measure on the board before trusting any pipeline claim.

Recap: Production tracking is a budget problem first: detect sparsely, track cheaply, re-detect before drift compounds. Classical trackers win the loop on latency while deep models sit out except in research corners. The bridge onward is scoring — the next section builds mean average precision from counts to curves, so detector quality itself can be graded before any tracker is trusted.

16.5 Mean Average Precision: Worked Numericals

16.5.1 Precision, recall, and overlap

Hook: Two detectors both claim "I found the cars." One draws three careful boxes; the other sprays ten loose ones that happen to cover the same cars. Which detector is better — and how can one number say so?

Detection scoring starts from three counts. A true positive (TP) (a predicted box that matches a ground-truth box) is a hit. A false positive (FP) (a predicted box with no match) is a false alarm. A false negative (a ground-truth box nobody predicted) is a miss. Precision — among all predictions, how many were right — is:

\[P = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}\]

Recall — among all ground truths, how many were found — is:

\[R = \frac{TP}{\text{total ground truths}}\]

Say a detector fires 5 boxes, 3 hit and 2 miss, over 4 real objects. Then precision is \(3/5 = 0.6\) and recall is \(3/4 = 0.75\): six in ten shots landed, and three in four targets were found. Spamming boxes raises recall but tanks precision; firing one safe box does the reverse — which is why scoring needs both.

A match is decided by intersection over union (IoU): the overlap area divided by the total area covered by both boxes. With \(A\) the predicted box and \(B\) the ground-truth box:

\[IoU = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}\]

With numbers: a predicted box of area 100 and a truth box of area 100 overlapping over 60 square pixels cover \(100 + 100 - 60 = 140\) between them, so \(IoU = 60/140 \approx 0.43\). The verbal rule from the session: below the threshold it counts as a false positive; above it, a true positive. The working threshold in the examples is 0.5, so 0.43 is an FP and 0.88 is a TP. A confusion matrix tabulates these outcomes, and the session flagged it alongside precision, recall, and IoU as the definitions where small mistakes cost marks.

The scoring contract: sort by confidence first, match each prediction to at most one truth in that order, score TP or FP at the IoU threshold, then accumulate precision and recall down the ranked list. Every number in this section flows from that order.

16.5.2 Worked eleven-point example

Worked example — eleven-point average precision with three truths and six detections. One class, three ground truths, six detections labeled A, C, F, D, B, E. The average-precision walkthrough runs as numbered steps:

  1. Sort every detection by confidence score, highest first: A, then C, F, D, B, E.
  2. Walk down the list and tag each as TP or FP at IoU 0.5. A overlaps strongly at about 88 percent: TP. C matches nothing: FP. F matches nothing with zero overlap: FP. D overlaps well: TP. B overlaps at about 70 percent: TP. E overlaps above half, yet counts as FP — the duplicate rule below explains why.
  3. Accumulate precision down the ranked list. After A: \(1/1 = 1\). After C: \(1/2 = 0.5\). After F: \(1/3 \approx 0.33\). After D: \(2/4 = 0.5\). After B: \(3/5 = 0.6\). After E: \(3/6 = 0.5\).
  4. Accumulate recall against the three ground truths: \(1/3\), \(1/3\), \(1/3\), \(2/3\), \(3/3\), \(3/3\) — about 0.333, 0.333, 0.333, 0.667, 1.0, 1.0.
  5. Interpolate: at each recall level \(r\) in \(\{0, 0.1, \ldots, 1.0\}\), take the highest precision at any recall at or above \(r\). Levels 0 through 0.3 keep precision 1; levels 0.4 through 1.0 keep 0.6.
  6. Average the eleven values: four 1s plus seven 0.6s gives \(4 + 4.2 = 8.2\), so

\[AP = \frac{8.2}{11} \approx 0.745\]

  1. Mean over classes:

\[mAP = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C} AP_c\]

With a single class, \(C = 1\), so average precision equals mean average precision here: mAP is about 0.745.

Sense-check: most of the 0.6-level precision survives across the recall range and the early perfect precision lifts the first four levels, so a score near three-quarters fits a detector that found everything with two false alarms along the way.

The duplicate rule deserves its own breath. Only one prediction can claim each ground truth. D outranks E on confidence, so D takes the match as TP while E, though above the IoU line, is marked FP. Any fourth above-threshold prediction against three ground truths is FP by the same logic.

Exam note: sorting by descending confidence first is the step students most often skip — without it every later number is wrong. Expect the duplicate-detection trap to appear deliberately: rank first, then match, then score.

16.5.3 Larger visual example and the duplicate rule at scale

A second, larger visual case (detections labeled A through Y against many ground truths) was solved live with an explicit warning: the sheet predates the current teaching team and carries errors, and several calls below rest on visual judgment rather than stated IoU numbers. The walkthrough, as presented: A is FP (no matching box); B is TP (strong overlap); C is FP; between D and E, E overlaps more, so E is TP and D is FP; F is FP (no box); G is TP (strongest overlap in its group) while H and I with weaker overlaps are FP; J is TP; K is FP; among L, M, N, M overlaps most yet its TP tag was flagged as debatable and in need of correction, with L false and N false; O is FP; P is TP; Q is FP on weaker overlap; R is TP against S, which loses on lower overlap and a confidence near 23 percent; T, U, V are FP; X is TP on larger overlap while Y is FP on smaller overlap.

Two lessons were drawn. First, the stated-count discipline matters: with the ground-truth count fixed, every extra above-threshold prediction is FP, decided by confidence order — debatable visual calls like M do not change the counting rule, they only show why stated numbers matter. Second, visual overlap calls cannot be graded fairly at scale — every student would assume a different IoU.

Exam note: in the exam both the confidence score and the IoU value will be stated, or students should ask for them rather than guess. Never score a close visual call on gut overlap; rank by the given confidence and threshold on the given IoU.

16.5.4 Why interpolation exists

Raw precision-recall points zigzag: precision jumps up and down as each FP dips the curve and each TP lifts it, so the area under the raw curve cannot be read off fairly. Interpolation replaces each point with the best precision available at or beyond its recall, which smooths the sawtooth into a monotone staircase. Picture recall on the horizontal axis and precision on the vertical axis. The raw curve bounces with every false alarm; the interpolated curve steps down only, never back up, like a staircase seen from the side. The eleven-point version samples recall at 0, 0.1, up to 1.0; the nine-point version samples 0.1 through 0.9. Plotting both the raw and the interpolated curve shows the smoothing directly: the staircase sits on top of the zigzag, touching it at each level's best point and bridging the dips. Alongside mAP, the session named k-means clustering and mean-shift rounds as the other numerical types worth rehearsing.

16.5.5 Student questions and answers

Q: With two ground truths and three detections where the first two are true positives, is the third automatically a false positive, or could it sit on top of an already claimed truth?

A: Decide purely on confidence score and IoU values, nothing else. Rank by confidence; the top claims go first, and once every ground truth is claimed, any further above-threshold detection is a false positive. Overlapping an already claimed truth does not save it.

Q: In the big visual sheet, does the largest covering box always win as the true positive? How do we check without stated numbers?

A: Largest overlap normally wins, but visual judgment alone cannot settle close calls such as R against S. Real scoring needs both the confidence score and the IoU value stated. Between close rivals, the higher-confidence box takes priority, and anything else stays an assumption the grader cannot verify across hundreds of scripts.

Q: Two detections clear the IoU threshold on the same ground truth, with different confidences. Do we keep only the stronger one?

A: Yes. Sort by descending confidence first, then walk down: the stronger detection claims the ground truth as a true positive and the weaker one is marked false positive. The E detection lost to D for exactly this reason — both cleared the overlap bar, but D ranked higher.

Scope: The eleven-point mAP applies when one fixed IoU threshold and one class are in play with a stated ground-truth count. It breaks as a comparison tool across thresholds or across datasets with different truth counts — modern benchmarks average over thresholds and classes instead. For this exam, the fixed-threshold single-class walkthrough is the whole game.

Recap: Precision scores the shots, recall scores the coverage, IoU decides each hit, confidence order decides each duplicate, and interpolation turns the zigzag into a fair staircase before the average. The bridge onward is the exam itself: the next section converts these drills into marks, weightage, and open-book strategy.

Detector leaderboards run on exactly this machinery: a traffic-sign benchmark ranks submissions by mAP at IoU 0.5, and a winning entry is the one whose staircase stays highest longest — few false alarms early, full coverage by the end.

16.6 Exam Pattern and Weightage

16.6.1 Marks split

The comprehensive exam carries 40 percent of the total grade. Expect six to seven questions with uneven marks — some worth four, others six, seven, nine, or ten — tuned to the topic at hand rather than split equally. Of that paper, only 10 to 20 percent revisits pre-midterm material in a single question; 80 to 90 percent tests post-midterm topics. The three evaluation components weigh 30, 30, and 40 percent respectively, with the comprehensive paper as the final 40.

Exam note: the big-mark questions are deliberately mixed in — solving one high-value question correctly lifts the total fast, which rewards genuine understanding over attempted coverage. Spend revision time where the marks live: post-midterm topics first.

16.6.2 Question style

The paper is open-book: manuals and shared PDFs are allowed, so rote recall is pointless and nothing rewards memorization. Every question, numericals included, probes concepts — why one approach was chosen, why another was rejected, how a method behaves under stated conditions. The problems simplify some computations so answers stay reachable, but the grading hunts for reasoning. Reference numericals in the shared material show the shape of what is coming without repeating verbatim.

Exam note: high scores come from knowing how to use the allowed material, not from carrying more of it. Tab the mAP walkthrough, the Kalman cycle, and the mean-shift rounds so each can be found in seconds.

Sample practice questions were promised for upload to the class groups, and the annotated material was discussed with a request to strip repeated slides while restoring any taught core concepts that went missing — students were asked to send pointed notes so the teaching assistants can refine the set.

Key Industry Applications

  • Modern YOLO-family detectors fold track-then-sort logic into their pipelines for real-time throughput, detecting sparsely and tracking between anchors.
  • Phone camera teams ship bokeh and semantic-segmentation features grounded in this vision stack, and SLAM systems reuse the same tracking instincts.
  • Delivery and payload drones navigate wind, battery sag, and evasive turns with banks of Kalman-style filters fused per axis.
  • Missile guidance stacks constant-velocity and constant-acceleration models with line-of-sight geometry and lateral acceleration commands.
  • Beating-heart surgery tracks valve motion under image guidance with fast classical trackers, not deep networks, because the loop must stay fast.
  • Bird, missile-blip, and planet tracking all run predict-correct loops under occlusion and sparse sightings.
  • Edge deployment converts PyTorch weights to formats such as ONNX and runs tiny detectors such as nano YOLO on boards like Raspberry Pi with plug-in CMOS cameras, prototyped first in ROS and Gazebo.
  • DeepSORT and FairMOT show where deep trackers help — specialized research such as electron tracks in cloud chambers — while production stays classical for latency.
