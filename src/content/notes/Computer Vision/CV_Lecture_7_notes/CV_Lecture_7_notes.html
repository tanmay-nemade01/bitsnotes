7.1 SIFT Motivation and Invariance Goals

7.1.1 What SIFT Solves

A single photo of a street corner can return at noon, at dusk, from across the road, or tilted 90 degrees. How does a program still know it is the same corner?

A scale invariant feature transform — a method that finds image points that match across zoom, rotation, and light change — is often called SIFT for short. The 2004 paper by David Lowe introduced the method as a full pipeline from detection to description. The core promise is simple. Take two views of the same place with different zoom, angle, and light. The same physical point must still match.

This brings together all prior work in the course. Edge detection finds intensity jumps. Line detection groups edges. Corner detection finds edge crossings. Histogram of gradients counts edge direction. SIFT uses all of them at once. It builds small image patches that act like fingerprints. Each patch links edges and corners into one pattern. That pattern stays stable when the camera moves.

SIFT fingerprint tuple with position, size, and orientation. Each kept point stores where it is, how large its support is, and which way it points, plus a descriptor vector built from local gradients. Matching then compares fingerprints, not raw pixels, so one fingerprint per point replaces fragile pixel-by-pixel comparison.

A photometric change — a shift in brightness, exposure, or illumination — must not break the match. A geometric change — a shift in position, size, or angle — must not break the match either. The session set three geometric goals: translation invariance, rotation invariance, and scale invariance. Translation here covers shift in position. Scale covers zoom in and zoom out, such as a 4 by 4 patch growing to 16 by 16. Rotation covers turns such as 90 degrees, 100 degrees, or 180 degrees.

The comparison below places SIFT next to its two closest relatives.

Dimension Harris corner SIFT Deep augmentation pipeline
What is stored Corner position only Position, characteristic size, principal orientation, gradient descriptor Learned weights over whole images
Zoom handling None; single scale Built in through scale-space search Learned from zoomed copies of training images
Rotation handling Rotation-free response but no stored angle Stored angle; patch rotated back before matching Learned from rotated copies
Light handling Sensitive to strong gain change Gradient-based plus normalization, so tolerant Learned from exposure jitter
Cost Cheap Moderate, runs on plain compute Heavy training, needs strong hardware

When to pick which: use Harris when the camera barely moves and speed matters most; use SIFT when zoom, turn, and light shift are all present but no training budget exists; use a learned model when wide class spread must be covered and training data plus hardware are available.

The link to deep learning is direct. In a deep learning pipeline, augmentation zooms, rotates, and flips training images. That teaches the network to ignore size and angle. SIFT builds that tolerance into the feature itself, with no training and no graphics hardware. Deep models cost a lot to train and need strong hardware to run. SIFT runs fast on plain compute. That is why it served as a base for driver-assist systems from early versions up to recent ones, and why it still appears in fast mapping work.

Driver-assist stacks used SIFT-family matching for lane and object tracking before large neural models took over, because it runs in real time on modest hardware. Samsung phone highlight search that frames a jacket region and returns shopping matches is textbook image retrieval built on the same local-feature idea. The patented core lived outside the base install, so open tools ship it through OpenCV Contrib while variants such as ORB use the same skeleton with cheaper binary tests.

Recap: SIFT turns each strong patch into one fingerprint that survives light change, zoom, shift, and turn. Bridge: the next step asks what that fingerprint must contain so a zoomed peak top still links to its wide-view twin.

7.1.2 Invariance in Plain Terms

Think of a human face. To find a person across photos, the eyes, nose, and mouth matter more than the torso. The nose stays the nose at many zooms and angles. The eyes stay a distinct pair, and the mouth keeps its place below the nose. Each part is distinct, each has a stable location, and each keeps its size relative to its surround. SIFT looks for the same kind of stable part in any image. It picks a small region, the interest point, and stores an x and y location — the pixel position of the point — plus a size — how large the patch is — plus an orientation angle — which way the patch points. Together these form a signature for that patch.

Everyday picture: think of a nose on a face. The nose is the interest point, the face is the search window, the size of the nose sets the scale, and the tilt of the face sets the orientation. Even when the photo is zoomed or turned, the nose-to-eyes layout points back to the same person. Where the picture breaks: a nose alone cannot name a person the way a full face can, just as one SIFT point alone cannot name an object — many points must agree.

The signature is a tuple \((x, y, \sigma, \theta)\), where \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) is the horizontal pixel position, \(y \in \mathbb{R}\) is the vertical pixel position, \(\sigma \in \mathbb{R}\) with \(\sigma > 0\) is the characteristic size tied to blur width, and \(\theta \in [0, 2\pi)\) is the principal direction in radians. Each symbol is defined at first use here and reused later. In full fingerprint form with a descriptor vector \(v \in \mathbb{R}^{128}\):

\[ p = (x, y, \sigma, \theta, v) \]

where \(p\) is one SIFT keypoint, \((x, y)\) is its image position in pixels, \(\sigma\) is its characteristic size, \(\theta\) is its principal orientation, and \(v\) is the normalized gradient-histogram descriptor around it.

An interest point — a pixel region worth storing because it stands out — must pass three tests. It must have a stable location. It must have a stable size. It must have a stable direction. When all three hold, a match in one image maps to one match in the other. Edges alone fail this test, as the next section shows.

Picture a size-orientation chart to fix the idea. The horizontal axis is patch size \(\sigma\) from fine to coarse. The vertical axis is orientation \(\theta\) from 0 to 360 degrees. A good interest point is a lone spike on this chart: one size towers above the rest and one direction towers above the rest. A flat sheet with no spike means the patch looks the same at every size and angle, so it carries no identity and gets dropped.

Scope: the \((x, y, \sigma, \theta)\) fingerprint covers translation, uniform zoom, in-plane rotation, and steady illumination change. Assumption: the patch is near-planar and the zoom is about uniform in all directions. The model breaks under strong perspective tilt, non-rigid warping, or day-to-night relighting, where one global size plus one angle cannot describe the change.

A frequent trap is to treat any strong edge as a ready fingerprint. A long straight edge fires strongly yet slides along its own length, so its \((x, y)\) never locks. Another trap is to store position without size: two copies of the same corner at 1x and 4x then look like different points. A third trap is to store size without direction: a 90-degree turn then breaks the match even when position and size agree.

Recap: a keepable point needs all three — locked position, locked size, locked direction — or its fingerprint cannot survive zoom plus turn. Bridge: panorama stitching shows why all three are needed at once.

7.2 Panorama Stitching and the Matching Problem

7.2.1 Why Stitching Needs Features

Two photos show the same mountain peak — one wide, one zoomed. A person links the two peak tops at once. What must a program compute to earn the same link?

Before strong stitching tools, joining photos into a wide panorama was hard. Two shots of one mountain peak show the issue. One shot is wide. The other is zoomed into the same peak. A person sees at once that the peak top in shot one is the peak top in shot two. A program must prove that link from pixels.

The task has three steps. First, pull a set of features from each image, such as the peak top. Second, match features across images. Third, align and blend. Alignment may need a shift, a size change, and a turn. The same logic drives retrieval. Given one query image, find its twin in a large set despite zoom and light shifts.

Peak-top zoomed-versus-wide panorama patch alignment intuition. A peak top forms a dark-rock-against-light-sky pattern that keeps its layout under zoom. The descriptor around the peak therefore agrees across views even when raw pixel grids differ in size, so the pair can anchor the shift-scale-turn fit that places one image inside the other.

Intensity change breaks naive edge matching. Two shots of one stone monument in Rome show this. One is zoomed in. One is zoomed out. Angle, light, and intensity all differ. Edge maps from the two shots do not line up. A harder case adds rotation. A side view turned at an angle must still match the front view. SIFT gives a path through both cases.

Imagine the alignment chart for the Rome pair. The horizontal axis lists candidate patch pairs, the vertical axis shows fingerprint distance from 0 (twin) to 1 (stranger). True peak-top pairs form a deep valley near 0 while random pairings sit high near 1. The takeaway: distance between fingerprints separates twins from strangers where raw edge overlap cannot.

Scope: local-feature stitching fits views that share a rigid shift-scale-turn link, such as distant panoramas or flat catalog shots. It breaks when the scene moves between shots, when overlap is near zero, or when repeated texture (rows of identical windows) gives many equal-best matches.

A common pitfall is to compare whole edge maps after a global brightness fix and expect them to line up. Zoom shifts every edge pixel outward, so no single global shift repairs the map. A second pitfall is to tune on the wide pair and then test on a rotated pair without an orientation step; the score drops and the failure gets blamed on bad features rather than the missing rotation fix.

Recap: stitching needs local patches with anchored position, size, and direction, because global pixel comparison cannot survive zoom plus light shift. Bridge: the worked peak-top pairing below shows the three-step pull, match, and align loop in action.

7.2.2 Worked Example: Peak Top Across Zoom

Peak-top panorama zoom stitching alignment blend — fully traced.

Setup: image A shows a full ridge with a peak, about 800 pixels wide. Image B shows a zoomed view of the same peak with extra detail around it, about 400 pixels wide but covering one quarter of the land area, so the effective zoom factor is near \(z = 2\). Goal: place the patch from B inside A.

Step 1 — pick the peak top in A as a candidate patch. Center a 16 by 16 window on the darkest rock pixel just below the sky line, at say \((x_A, y_A) = (400, 200)\).

Step 2 — pick the peak top in B as a candidate patch. The same rock sits at about \((x_B, y_B) = (200, 120)\) in the smaller grid because the framing differs.

Step 3 — compare patch fingerprints rather than raw pixels. Build each 128-length gradient descriptor and score the Euclidean gap. The true pair scores near 0.15 after normalization while a wrong pairing (peak against sky) scores near 0.85.

Step 4 — when fingerprints agree, lock the pair. Accept the pair whose gap is far below the second-best gap, which rejects flat sky patches that match everywhere.

Step 5 — use the pair plus other pairs to shift, scale, and turn B into the frame of A. With three or more locked pairs, solve for shift \((t_x, t_y)\), scale \(z\), and turn \(\phi\), then warp B. Here \(z \approx 2\) maps \((200, 120)\) in B to about \((400, 240)\) in A up to a small shift.

Step 6 — blend the overlap. Feather pixel weights across the seam so the rock edge stays sharp while the sky gradient shows no line.

Sense-check: zoom does not change the fingerprint much because the peak top keeps its local pattern of dark rock against light sky. Rotation needs one extra fix, handled later by orientation alignment, so this trace covers shift plus scale only.

7.2.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why extract features instead of matching whole images pixel by pixel?

A: Whole-image match breaks with zoom, turn, and light shift. Local patches around strong points hold their shape. Matching a few hundred strong patches is faster and far more stable than matching every pixel. A full-frame compare at 1 megapixel touches one million pixels per shift tried, while a 500-point fingerprint set touches a few hundred vectors — faster by orders of magnitude and stable because flat pixels that carry no identity never enter the contest.

7.3 Applications Built on Local Features

7.3.1 Simultaneous Localization and Mapping

A car drives down a road with no satellite fix and one camera. How can it still draw the path it just drove?

Simultaneous localization and mapping — building a map while tracking camera position inside it — is often called SLAM for short. Visual SLAM was shown live with two demos. One used a single camera, called monocular vision. One used a pair of cameras, called stereo vision. In both, the system spots road edges, signs, and building corners frame by frame. It links the same spot across frames. From those links it draws a path.

The striking part is speed with no neural model. Thousands of local points get built per frame. Each new frame matches its points to the last frame. The map grows as the camera moves. The same base method guides indoor robots, cars, and headsets.

Frame-to-frame SLAM tracking loop. Each frame yields a fresh keypoint set, each new set matches against the last frame by descriptor gap, and surviving tracks triangulate into map points while the camera pose updates. No learned weights are needed because geometry plus descriptor identity carries the track.

Picture the road demo as a trail chart. The horizontal plane is the ground map, the vertical trail is time. Road-edge and sign points leave steady trails across frames while the estimated camera path threads between them. The takeaway: many cheap points beat a few costly ones when the camera moves fast.

Scope: visual SLAM holds when the scene is mostly still and textured, with steady light and enough overlap between frames. It breaks in a white corridor, in heavy rain glare, or with fast-moving traffic that fills the frame, because tracks then belong to moving bodies rather than the still world.

A common pitfall is to keep every detected point. Points on car windows and shadows look strong for one frame and then slide, so they poison the pose. Keeping only points with lone-peak scale responses plus consensus filtering avoids this.

Recap: SLAM road mapping demo in one line — match thousands of cheap points frame to frame and the map plus path fall out together. Bridge: the same match-then-lift idea powers retrieval, reconstruction, and tracking next.

Visual SLAM in cars and robots tracks road scenery frame by frame to hold lane position and build a live map.

7.3.2 Retrieval, Reconstruction, and Tracking

Once images can align, many tasks open up. Image alignment means placing two views in one frame. From aligned views comes 3D reconstruction — lifting matched points into depth. From video frames comes motion tracking — linking the same point over time. From templates comes object recognition — asking if this object sits in that photo. From a query comes retrieval — pulling twins from a store.

One matching core, five tasks. Alignment solves the warp; 3D reconstruction lifts matched pairs into depth by triangulation; motion tracking chains matches over time; object recognition tests whether a template set sits inside a photo; retrieval ranks a store by twin count. All five rest on the same local-identity test.

The Mars rover story makes this concrete. A rover sees only a small patch of soil. It holds a wide map of the terrain. It pulls key points from its small view, pulls points from the map, and matches them. The match tells it where it stands. The same match logic powers Google image search for book covers: snap a book cover, pull its key points, and find the same cover in a catalog even when the snap is tilted or dim.

Catalog search for books and discs finds a query cover inside cluttered shelf photos by matching local patches. A truck tire and rim patch was shown as a stable key region that matches across views because the circular rim pattern plus spokes stays distinct — the circle center locks position, the rim radius locks size, and a spoke locks direction, so all three fingerprint parts fire at once.

Imagine a retrieval score chart. The horizontal axis ranks catalog entries, the vertical axis counts agreeing fingerprint pairs. The true book cover forms a tall lone bar (say 40 agreeing pairs) while shelf clutter forms a low flat floor (2 to 3 chance pairs each). The takeaway: counting agreeing pairs separates the twin from the pile.

Scope: template retrieval fits same-object matching — the same tire, the same book cover — across zoom, shift, turn, and light change. It does not cover wide class spread such as telling dog breeds apart; that needs many training samples per class so a learned model can span the variation.

Recap: retrieval, clutter DVD search, reconstruction, and tracking all reuse one skill — proving that two patches show one spot. Bridge: clutter plus occlusion tests whether that skill holds when most of the object hides.

7.3.3 Clutter and Occlusion

One DVD cover hides inside a pile of many discs, with only a corner showing under changed light and angle. Why does matching still find it?

A simple case shows the full object. A hard case hides the object in clutter. One demo asked for one DVD cover inside a pile of many discs. Only part of the target shows. Light differs. Angle differs. The fix is volume. One image can yield hundreds or thousands of local features. Even when most are hidden, a few strong ones peek through. Those few drive the match.

The session stressed efficiency. Every method wants real-time speed. SIFT aims for many distinct points per image, each cheap to compare, so large object stores stay searchable.

Clutter DVD search — traced with numbers. The target cover yields 800 points in the clean template. In the pile photo only about 120 of those 800 are visible; the rest hide under other discs. Of the 120 visible, about 25 survive the lone-peak plus threshold tests as strong. Brute-force matching pairs those 25 against 2000 pile points. About 18 of the 25 land on the true cover corner within a tight geometric fit, while the remaining 7 scatter at random. A consensus fit keeps the agreeing 18 and drops the 7 strays, so the cover is found from under 3 percent of its pixels. Sense-check: a handful of strong survivors beats hundreds of weak ones when most of the object hides.

Scope: volume saves the day only when the visible fragment still holds a few high-contrast, multi-direction patches. A blank spine with one flat color carries no fingerprint, so no count of points can save it.

Recap: hundreds of cheap points per image buy robustness — occlusion eats most, survivors still agree. Bridge: this volume trick only works if each single point is worth keeping, which is why edges alone fall short next.

7.4 Blobs as Interest Points

7.4.1 Why Edges and Lines Fall Short

Two red dots sit on the same straight pillar edge. Their tiny edge windows look twins. Are they the same physical spot?

Edges mark where brightness jumps. Corners mark where two edges cross. Both react to light. Neither stores where along a long edge a point sits. A pillar demo made this sharp. Pick two red dots on the same straight pillar edge. Their local edge shape matches. Their positions differ. Edge match alone links the wrong dots.

Turn and zoom widen the gap. Turn the pillar view a little and zoom it by a factor of two or five. Edge direction shifts. Edge strength shifts. A match score based only on edge pixels drifts. View angle matters here. At 90 degrees of turn or five times zoom, the claim "this edge equals that edge" no longer holds from edge data alone. There is no position lock along the edge.

The textbook reason is the aperture problem. A straight edge viewed through a small window can slide along its own length with no change in the window, so shift along the edge is unknowable. Formally, with a weighted squared-gap score \(E(\Delta u)\) over a patch and auto-correlation matrix \(A\) built from outer products of gradients smoothed by the window, a straight edge gives one large eigenvalue and one near-zero eigenvalue. The near-zero direction is the slide direction. Only patches with gradients in two or more different directions — corners, blobs, textured dots — give two large eigenvalues and a lone sharp minimum.

Pillar straight-edge dots show missing position lock, so a blob patch is needed. Along one straight run every dot shares the same edge direction and strength, which means edge-only distance ties across dots. A richer surround that adds two-direction texture breaks the tie and pins \((x, y)\).

Picture the auto-correlation surface for three patches. A flower-bed patch forms a sharp bowl with one deep minimum. A roof edge forms a long valley that is sharp across the edge but flat along it. A cloud patch forms a flat sheet with no minimum at all. The takeaway: only the bowl localizes in both axes; the valley and the sheet cannot.

Scope: edge and line cues serve motion along the edge normal and group into crossings and blobs. They fail as lone point identities on straight runs, at high turn angles, and under large zoom, where direction plus strength both drift.

A common pitfall is to raise the edge threshold and hope the tie breaks. Stronger edges still share the same direction along the run, so the tie survives. The repair is structural — widen the window into a blob with two-direction content — not a higher cutoff.

Q: In road detection work, crossing lines gave a stable crossing point, so why do coordinates fail on the pillar?

A: A crossing of two lines does give a stable crossing, and that is a good point. The failure is along a single straight edge with no crossing. Any dot on that straight run looks like any other dot. Rotate or zoom the view and the edge pixels still fire, but they do not say which dot is which. That missing position lock is why a richer patch, a blob, is needed. Road crossing blob localization in one line: the crossing supplies two directions, which is exactly the two-eigenvalue condition a lone edge lacks.

Q: Does view angle matter, so an edge at one angle cannot match the same edge turned by 90 degrees or zoomed five times?

A: Yes. Turn and zoom change edge strength and direction. Edges still fire, but sameness is lost. The fix keeps edges inside a larger patch that adds position, size, and direction, so sameness can be tested. In eigenvalue terms, turn plus zoom reshapes the small matrix \(A\) enough that an edge-only score drifts, while a blob score with its size \(\sigma\) and direction \(\theta\) attached stays comparable after the patch is scaled and turned back.

Recap: one straight edge leaves one axis free, so dots along it tie — only a two-direction surround pins the point. Bridge: blobs supply that surround plus stored size and direction.

7.4.2 Blob Structure and Fingerprint

A blob — a compact image region with its own brightness shape, such as a dot, disk, or oval — serves as the interest point. A blob can be round or oval, small or large, even a mix of round shapes. Inside one blob sit many small edges grouped into a pattern. That group is more distinct than one edge.

Everyday picture: return to the nose on a face. The nose is the blob center, the eyes plus mouth form the surround, nose size sets \(\sigma\), and face tilt sets \(\theta\). Zoom out and the whole layout shrinks together; turn the head and the layout turns together. Where the picture breaks: real blobs are simple brightness bumps, not semantic parts, so a plain disk can serve as well as a nose.

The blob fingerprint stores position plus size plus direction. The reconstructed tuple is \((x, y, \sigma, \theta)\) as defined in section 7.1, with \(\sigma\) for size and \(\theta\) for direction. A nose analogy helped: a nose keeps its place relative to eyes and mouth, and it has its own size and tilt. In the same way, a blob keeps size and tilt relative to its patch, so a turned or zoomed copy still links back. With the descriptor vector \(v\) attached, the stored record is:

\[ p = (x, y, \sigma, \theta, v) \]

where \((x, y)\) is pixel position, \(\sigma > 0\) is characteristic size in pixels, \(\theta \in [0, 2\pi)\) is principal direction, and \(v\) holds the normalized gradient pattern that gives light tolerance.

Blob fingerprint with position, size, direction, and gradient pattern. Size ties to blur width \(\sigma\) found by the scale search. Direction ties to the strongest gradient direction inside the patch, covered in section 7.8. Light tolerance comes from using gradient direction and normalized strength rather than raw brightness, with patch mean removed and variance set to one.

Size ties to blur width. Direction ties to the strongest gradient direction inside the patch, covered in section 7.8. Light tolerance comes from using gradient direction and normalized strength rather than raw brightness. Good localization needs all three: without the blob surround, a point slides along the edge and position lock is lost.

Imagine a blob response chart. The horizontal axis sweeps blur width \(\sigma\), the vertical axis shows normalized response. A true disk gives one tall lone peak at the \(\sigma\) that fits it best. A straight edge gives a broad ridge with no lone peak. The takeaway: peak shape itself tells keep from drop before any matching starts.

Scope: the round-blob model fits compact, near-isotropic structure — dots, disks, corners with surround. It stretches poorly to long thin bars and wire edges, where an affine or elongated model fits better. Those cases call for ellipse fitting or line grouping rather than a single \(\sigma\).

Do not mistake a large blur for a large object. Blur width \(\sigma\) is the measuring tool, object size is the thing measured; the peak where tool fits thing marks the size. Confusing the two leads to storing the sweep setting instead of the object width.

Recap: a blob wraps edges into a sized, directed package whose gradient pattern survives light and geometry change. Bridge: Gaussian smoothing plus derivatives supply the ruler that finds that size.

7.5 Gaussian Smoothing, Derivatives, and Characteristic Scale

7.5.1 Mathematical Formulation

Images carry noise, so every slope measured raw jitters. How can smoothing plus slope-taking collapse into one cheap step?

Images carry noise, so smoothing comes first. The 1D Gaussian kernel with width \(\sigma\) is used. The reconstructed kernel is:

\[ G(x; \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]

where \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) is the offset in pixels, \(\sigma \in \mathbb{R}\) with \(\sigma > 0\) is the blur width in pixels, \(G \in \mathbb{R}\) is the filter weight, and the weights sum to one across \(x\). A noisy 1D signal \(f(x)\) is smoothed by convolution \(s = f \ast G\), where \(f\) is the input brightness, \(G\) is the kernel above, \(\ast\) means convolution, and \(s\) is the smoothed signal. Wider \(\sigma\) means a wider, flatter hat that averages over more neighbors; narrow \(\sigma\) keeps fine detail but also keeps noise.

Gaussian kernel blur with width \(\sigma\) for smoothing. Convolution with \(G(\cdot; \sigma)\) trades fine detail for noise resistance. Small \(\sigma\) near 1 keeps edges sharp, large \(\sigma\) washes small bumps away, and the sweep over \(\sigma\) later turns this trade into a size ruler.

Edge finding uses the derivative trick. The reconstructed identity is:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left(f \ast G\right) = f \ast \frac{dG}{dx} \]

where \(d/dx\) is the first derivative with respect to pixel position. In words, smoothing then taking the slope equals taking the slope of the smoother then blending with the image. This saves one pass. Without it, one would smooth and then take slopes of two signals. With it, only the kernel slope is needed. The kernel slope \(dG/dx\) is fixed and known in closed form:

\[ \frac{dG}{dx}(x; \sigma) = -\frac{x}{\sigma^2} G(x; \sigma) \]

so the whole edge pass reduces to one convolution with a fixed odd-symmetric wave.

Zero crossing marks the edge. Where the first slope peaks, the edge sits. The second slope sharpens this. The reconstructed smoothed second slope is \(f \ast d^{2}G/dx^{2}\), where \(d^{2}/dx^{2}\) is the second derivative. Second slopes place edges with more care than first slopes, but they cost more compute, so many pipelines skip them except in this scale search. The kernel second slope is:

\[ \frac{d^{2}G}{dx^{2}}(x; \sigma) = \frac{x^2 - \sigma^2}{\sigma^4} G(x; \sigma) \]

which has the dip-peak Mexican-hat shape: a central dip flanked by raised wings, crossing zero at \(x = \pm \sigma\).

Raw second-slope values run small, so they get a boost by \(\sigma^{2}\). The reconstructed normalized response is:

\[ R(x; \sigma) = \sigma^{2} \cdot \left(f \ast \frac{d^{2}G}{dx^{2}}\right)(x; \sigma) \]

where \(R \in \mathbb{R}\) is the scale-normalized response at position \(x\) and blur \(\sigma\). Sweeping \(\sigma\) through 1, 2, 3, 4, and more gives one response curve per \(\sigma\). The peak across \(\sigma\) marks the characteristic scale — the blur width at which the blob shape best fits the kernel and the response hits a local peak. The \(\sigma^{2}\) factor matters: without it large-\(\sigma\) responses shrink toward zero and small blobs always win; with it responses at different \(\sigma\) sit on one comparable scale, which is the Normalization studied by Lindeberg for true scale invariance.

Picture the response family. The horizontal axis is position \(x\), the vertical axis is boosted response \(R\). Each \(\sigma\) draws one curve. Small \(\sigma\) curves spike on small bumps, large \(\sigma\) curves swell on wide bumps. The takeaway: the envelope peak across curves names both the position and the fitting width.

Scope: the derivative-associative smoothing identity needs a shift-invariant linear blur — Gaussian here — plus enough padding at borders. Assumption: noise is fine-grained relative to the blob, so some \(\sigma\) in the sweep brackets the true width. Breaks arise when noise blobs rival signal blobs in size or when the sweep stops short of the true width.

A common pitfall is to read the tallest raw (unboosted) response as the size. Raw second slopes shrink as \(\sigma\) grows, so the small end always wins without the \(\sigma^{2}\) fix. A second pitfall is to confuse a spatial peak (best \(x\) at fixed \(\sigma\)) with a scale peak (best \(\sigma\) at fixed \(x\)); characteristic scale needs the maximum across \(\sigma\).

Recap: smooth with \(G\), fold the slope into the kernel by associativity, boost the second slope by \(\sigma^{2}\), and the peak across \(\sigma\) names the size. Bridge: the worked triple-blob sweep below runs this ruler on three zooms. Exam note: practice stating the associative smoothing-slope identity plus the \(\sigma^{2}\) boost in one line each.

7.5.2 Worked Example: Blobs at 1x, 2x, and 4x

Blobs at zoom with characteristic \(\sigma\) peaks and fingerprint triples — fully traced.

Setup: three blobs called A, B, and C sit in images at zoom 1x, 2x, and 4x. The kernel width \(\sigma\) is swept over 1, 2, 3, and more. Goal: find one \(\sigma\) per blob where the boosted second slope peaks.

Step 1 — at small \(\sigma\), blob A fits the kernel and gives a peak response. Say \(\sigma = 2\) for blob A with response \(R = 0.90\), while \(\sigma = 1\) gives 0.55 and \(\sigma = 3\) gives 0.60. Keep \((x_A, y_A, 2)\).

Step 2 — blob B is larger, so small \(\sigma\) gives a weak response. Raise \(\sigma\) to about 6 and blob B peaks with \(R = 0.88\), against 0.50 at \(\sigma = 4\) and 0.55 at \(\sigma = 8\). Keep \((x_B, y_B, 6)\).

Step 3 — blob C is still larger. Raise \(\sigma\) to about 10 and blob C peaks with \(R = 0.86\), against 0.52 at \(\sigma = 8\) and 0.58 at \(\sigma = 12\). Keep \((x_C, y_C, 10)\). The values 2, 6, and 10 are illustrative demo settings that show the peak marching right as the blob grows; any real image yields its own fitted widths from the same sweep logic.

Step 4 — read off the triple \((x, y, \sigma)\) at each peak. That triple is the position plus size fingerprint.

Sense-check: peak \(\sigma\) grows with blob width, and only the ratio of widths matters across zooms, so the same physical bump maps to itself at any imaging size.

The ratio link gives zoom tolerance. The reconstructed size link is:

\[ \frac{\text{size}_{B}}{\text{size}_{A}} = \frac{\sigma_{B}}{\sigma_{A}} \]

where \(\text{size}_{A}\) and \(\text{size}_{B}\) are blob diameters in pixels. Because only the ratio matters, units cancel — the link is unit invariant. A point at \((10, 10)\) at 1x moves to \((20, 20)\) at 2x zoom. In symbols, if zoom factor is \(z \in \mathbb{R}\) with \(z > 0\), then \((x', y') = (z \cdot x, z \cdot y)\). At \(z = 2\), \((10, 10)\) maps to \((20, 20)\). At 3x it maps to \((30, 30)\). With the \(\sigma\) ratio known, a point found at one zoom maps up or down to its twin. Numerically, blobs B and A above give \(\sigma_B / \sigma_A = 6 / 2 = 3\), so B is three times the width of A, matching its 3x zoom.

Picture the ratio on a doubling chart. The horizontal axis is zoom \(z\), the vertical axis is pixel position. Points ride straight rays from the origin with slope set by base position: \((10, 10)\) rides the ray to \((20, 20)\) at \(z = 2\) and \((30, 30)\) at \(z = 3\). The takeaway: zoom is multiplication from the origin, and the \(\sigma\) ratio reveals the multiplier.

Scope: the size ratio rule assumes uniform zoom with no stretch or tilt, so one scalar \(z\) maps the whole patch. Under anamorphic stretch the \(x\) and \(y\) ratios split and a single \(\sigma\) ratio under-fits.

Recap: sweep \(\sigma\), keep the tallest boosted peak per spot, read off \((x, y, \sigma)\), and ratios bridge zooms. Bridge: 2D images need the same idea along both axes at once. Exam note: practice the scale-ratio map \((10, 10)\) at 1x to \((20, 20)\) at 2x plus the \(\sigma^{2}\) boost reason.

7.5.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: We get peaks at many zoom levels, but how do we choose which blob fingerprint to keep from the sweep?

A: The choice comes after the sweep. Keep the \((x, y, \sigma)\) with the strongest peak across \(\sigma\). Weak or tied peaks get dropped. A lone spike whose tallest value beats its neighbors by a clear margin stays; a flat row of near-equal values means the patch looks the same at every size and carries no identity. The full selection rule is covered with the 2D method, where flat patches give no single strong peak and edge-like ridges get cut by a curvature test.

7.6 Laplacian of Gaussian and Scale-Space Extrema

7.6.1 Mathematical Formulation

A 1D ruler finds blob width along a line. What operator finds round blobs in a real picture with two axes?

For 2D images, slopes run along both axes. The reconstructed 2D Gaussian is:

\[ G(x, y; \sigma) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right) \]

where \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) is the horizontal offset, \(y \in \mathbb{R}\) is the vertical offset, and \(\sigma\) is the blur width as before. The volume under the hat sums to one, so brightness is preserved. The Laplacian adds the two pure second partials:

\[ \nabla^{2}G = \frac{\partial^{2}G}{\partial x^{2}} + \frac{\partial^{2}G}{\partial y^{2}} \]

where \(\nabla^{2}\) is the Laplacian operator, \(\partial^{2}/\partial x^{2}\) is the second partial along \(x\), and \(\partial^{2}/\partial y^{2}\) is the second partial along \(y\). In closed form:

\[ \nabla^{2}G(x, y; \sigma) = \frac{x^{2} + y^{2} - 2\sigma^{2}}{2\pi\sigma^{6}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right) \]

The shape looks like a central dip with a raised ring, the same dip-peak shape seen in 1D but turned round. The zero ring sits at radius \(r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2} = \sqrt{2}\,\sigma\), which is why the ring grows with \(\sigma\).

Normalized Laplacian of Gaussian partial-derivative octave stack. The 2D kernel above summed over both second partials gives a round blob meter. Boosting by \(\sigma^{2}\) puts all widths on one scale, and stacking the response over blur widths plus image sizes (octaves) turns one picture into a 3D search volume in \((x, y, \sigma)\).

The boosted form is the normalized Laplacian of Gaussian, often shortened to normalized LoG. The reconstructed response for image \(I(x, y)\) is:

\[ R(x, y; \sigma) = \sigma^{2} \cdot \left(\left(\nabla^{2}G(\cdot; \sigma)\right) \ast I\right)(x, y) \]

where \(I\) is input brightness, \(\ast\) is 2D convolution, and \(R\) is the normalized response. Sweeping \(\sigma\) gives a stack of response images. At each pixel, the \(\sigma\) with the largest magnitude response marks its characteristic size, so octave normalized response peaks name both position and size in one 3D search. Maxima mark bright blobs on dark surround, minima mark dark blobs on bright surround; both signs are kept by magnitude.

In practice the image is also swept in size, called octaves. Each octave halves the resolution, illustratively 128 by 128, then 64 by 64, then 32 by 32, then 16 by 16 — a halving stack shown in the demo to picture the idea rather than a fixed image size. Each octave is one image size. Within each octave, \(\sigma\) sweeps 1, 2, 3, 4, and more. That two-way sweep covers both blur width and image size. After each octave the doubled-\(\sigma\) blur image is down-sampled by taking every second pixel in each row and column, which keeps sampling density matched to blur while cutting compute.

Picture the scale-space cube. The base plane is \((x, y)\), the vertical axis is \(\sigma\) (with octave steps stacked above). A round blob lights up as a glowing spot that is brightest at one height — its fitting \(\sigma\) — and dimmer above and below. The takeaway: blob search is peak-hunting in 3D, not edge-tracing in 2D.

Scope: the round LoG meter fits compact near-circular blobs. It under-fits long bars and sharp corners, where the ring straddles the shape unevenly. Dense octave stacks also cost memory, which is why the cheaper difference step arrives next.

A common pitfall is to read \(\sigma\) labels as derivative orders. Labels like sigma 1 or sigma 2 mean blur widths, not first or second derivatives; the derivative order stays at two throughout while \(\sigma\) sweeps. Another pitfall is to keep every spatial peak at every \(\sigma\) — that yields a thicket of overlapping hits instead of one \((x, y, \sigma)\) per blob.

Recap: the normalized LoG turns each pixel into a size curve, and octaves extend the curve across image sizes so big blobs meet big rulers. Bridge: the worked face-versus-wall test below shows the keep-or-drop rule in action.

7.6.2 Worked Example: Strong Peak Versus Flat Patch

Strong face peak kept, flat wall weak tied peaks dropped — fully traced.

Setup: pick two candidate points. Point P sits on a face with strong light-dark structure (eye corner). Point Q sits on a flat wall. For each, plot normalized LoG response across \(\sigma\) and across octaves.

For P, the curve has one tall peak. Say at octave 64 by 64 and \(\sigma = 2\) the response reads 0.92, against 0.55 at \(\sigma = 1\) and 0.58 at \(\sigma = 3\) in the same octave. The fingerprint is \((x, y, \sigma) = (x_{P}, y_{P}, 2)\). Size 2 goes with the point. To map P from octave 2 back to octave 1, halve the position using the scale ratio \(s_{1}/s_{2} = \sigma_{0}/\sigma_{1} = 1/2\). So \((x', y') = (x/2, y/2)\) when stepping down one doubling: a point at \((96, 64)\) in the fine octave sits at \((48, 32)\) in the next coarser one.

For Q, the curve stays flat. Responses read 0.21, 0.23, 0.22, 0.24 across \(\sigma = 1, 2, 3, 4\) — several \(\sigma\) values give about the same weak response and no single peak stands out. That point is dropped. The rule is direct: strong single peak stays, weak tied peaks go.

The selection starts from many random seeds. The session suggested about a thousand random blob points per image, then pruning to the strong ones. A face point with structure survives. A blank wall point does not.

Sense-check: texture plus contrast builds a lone spike; flatness builds a flat line, and flat lines carry no size identity.

Scope: the thousand-seed start assumes the image holds at least a few textured zones. On a near-blank frame even a thousand seeds yield mostly flat curves, so the survivor list stays near empty rather than filling with noise.

Recap: tall lone peak means keep with its \(\sigma\); flat tie means drop. Bridge: this 3D peak test is exact but costly, which motivates the cheap difference shortcut.

7.6.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: The demo images look more blurred than zoomed, so is this blurring rather than zooming?

A: Both happen. Blur comes from the Gaussian kernel at each \(\sigma\). Size change comes from octave resizing, such as 720 by 720 down to 360 by 360. The ratio trick makes blur width and image size work together, so a point found at one size maps to the same point at another size. Blurring versus zooming in one line: kernel blur probes width at fixed grid size while octave resizing replays the same probe at a coarser grid.

Q: In the middle octave the response at one \(\sigma\) topped the rest, but why did that \(\sigma\) win when each \(\sigma\) gave some peak?

A: Each \(\sigma\) gives its own local peak across space, but only one \(\sigma\) gives the tallest peak at that pixel. Light falloff, contrast, and prior smoothing shape the heights. The tallest one marks the best size fit, so that \(\sigma\) becomes the stored size. Think of trying rings over a coin: every ring touches somewhere, but only the fitting ring hugs it all round and scores highest.

Q: If higher-order derivatives give taller peaks, why stop at second order instead of third or fourth order?

A: Order stays at two while \(\sigma\) sweeps; labels like sigma 1 or sigma 2 mean blur widths, not derivative orders. Third or fourth order would shift peaks but add heavy compute cost. First to second order already adds a full extra pass over every scale, so going higher slows the method a lot for little gain. The pipeline stops at second order to bound compute cost — derivative order compute cost in one line: each extra order repeats a full multi-scale pass for shrinking stability returns.

7.7 Difference of Gaussians and Keypoint Localization

7.7.1 Mathematical Formulation

Second partials at every scale give exact peaks but burn compute. What cheaper pair of blurs lands peaks in nearly the same spots?

Second partials at every scale cost a lot. If \(n\) is image width, a second-order pass behaves like order \(n^{2}\) work per scale, times the number of \(\sigma\) steps. The session flagged this as the main block. The fix replaces the normalized LoG with a difference of Gaussians, often shortened to DoG.

Blur the image once per \(\sigma\) to get \(L(x, y; \sigma) = G(\cdot; \sigma) \ast I\). Then subtract neighbors:

\[ D(x, y; \sigma) = L(x, y; k\sigma) - L(x, y; \sigma) \]

where \(L\) is the blurred image, \(k \in \mathbb{R}\) with \(k > 1\) is the blur step factor, and \(D\) is the DoG response. The demo used neighboring blurs four apart (\(k = 4\), as in sigma 2 is four times sigma 1) to make the gap easy to see on screen; the shipped SIFT setting instead divides each octave doubling into \(s = 3\) intervals with \(k = 2^{1/s} \approx 1.26\), stacking \(s + 3\) blurs per octave so extrema search covers a full octave. Both choices share the same shape logic below — the demo value teaches the eye while the shipped value tunes stability.

Difference of Gaussians blur-minus-neighbor threshold step. Neighboring blurs subtract to a hat-shaped response with the same dip-peak form as the normalized LoG, so peaks sit in nearly the same places at far lower cost. No second partial is computed. Only blurs and minus steps run, with the \(\sigma^{2}\) normalization already folded in through the \((k-1)\) factor.

Why the shapes agree follows from the heat equation in \(\sigma\). Since \(\partial G / \partial \sigma = \sigma \nabla^{2}G\), the finite gap gives:

\[ G(x, y; k\sigma) - G(x, y; \sigma) \approx (k-1)\,\sigma^{2}\nabla^{2}G \]

Each line of the move: start from the \(\sigma\)-derivative identity, replace the derivative by the neighbor gap over \((k-1)\sigma\), then multiply through. The factor \((k-1)\) is constant over all scales, so it never shifts peak positions, and the gap error shrinks toward zero as \(k\) approaches 1. Tests show even wide gaps keep peak maps stable, which is why the demo \(k = 4\) still teaches the right picture.

Peak search runs per octave. Take one octave, such as 256 by 256, with blurs at \(\sigma = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\). Form four DoG images: 2 minus 1, 3 minus 2, 4 minus 3, 5 minus 4. At each pixel, compare across the stack. Each sample meets 26 neighbors — 8 in its own DoG image plus 9 in the scale above plus 9 in the scale below — and survives only when larger than all 26 or smaller than all 26. The stored fingerprint at this stage is \((x, y, \sigma_{\text{peak}})\), where \(\sigma_{\text{peak}}\) is the blur step that gave the peak. A quadratic fit then refines position plus scale to sub-pixel precision.

Weak peaks get cut by a threshold like non-maximum suppression in edge work. The spoken rule was "from the maximum, the next maximum should be 20 points behind" as an example cutoff. In practice a fixed gap or fraction drops flat ties and keeps lone spikes. Edge-like ridges get a second cut through the local Hessian of \(D\): with trace \(\text{Tr}(H)\) and determinant \(\text{Det}(H)\), points with \(\text{Tr}(H)^{2} / \text{Det}(H)\) above about 10 are rejected as too edge-like. What stays is a set of blobs per octave, each with its own \((x, y, \sigma)\).

Picture the DoG stack as a sandwich. The horizontal slices are DoG images from fine to coarse, the vertical pin through one \((x, y)\) threads its 26-neighborhood. A true blob is the tallest floor or the deepest cellar along the pin; a ridge ties with its neighbors and drops out. The takeaway: the 26-test plus threshold turns smooth response hills into a sparse point list.

Scope: DoG peak search fits still, textured scenes where a sparse repeatable set exists. Assumption: blur steps tile scale finely enough (\(s = 3\) shipped) that no blob falls fully between sampled levels and contrast exceeds the cutoff. Low-contrast specks and dense repeated grids violate the assumption and either drop or flood the list.

A common pitfall is to difference across octaves instead of inside one octave; grids then mismatch and positions smear. Differences run inside each octave only, with cross-octave ratios handling the jumps. A second pitfall is to keep both signs confused: maxima and minima both count, since bright-on-dark and dark-on-bright blobs are equally valid.

Recap: blur sweep, neighbor minus inside each octave, 26-neighbor peak plus threshold — the DoG shortcut replaces normalized LoG to avoid second partials. Bridge: the octave-stack trace below runs the full pick-and-pool loop. Exam note: state the DoG shortcut in one line and list the three build steps in order.

7.7.2 Worked Example: Octave Stack and Peak Pick

Octave stack blur-minus peak threshold pooling — fully traced.

Setup: original size 250 by 250. Blur with \(\sigma_{1} = 1\) and \(\sigma_{2} = 4\), so \(\sigma_{2} = 4\sigma_{1}\) for screen clarity (shipped code uses \(k \approx 1.26\)). Repeat for more \(\sigma\) values in the same octave, then repeat for smaller octaves such as 180 by 180, 120 by 120, and 100 by 100. Goal: list surviving key points.

Step 1 — build blurred set \(L_{1}, L_{2}, L_{3}, L_{4}\) in one octave at fixed grid size.

Step 2 — form DoG set \(D_{1} = L_{2}-L_{1}\), \(D_{2} = L_{3}-L_{2}\), \(D_{3} = L_{4}-L_{3}\).

Step 3 — at sample pixel \((x, y) = (100, 80)\), read \(D_{1} = 12\), \(D_{2} = 38\), \(D_{3} = 14\). The middle gap towers over its neighbors.

Step 4 — if one value tops its 26 neighbors by the threshold gap (say 20 points clear of the next best), keep \((x, y, \Delta\sigma)\) where \(\Delta\sigma\) marks the winning gap. Here \(38\) beats \(14\) by 24, so keep \((100, 80, \Delta_{2})\). The stored width label \(\Delta_{2}\) names the winning minus, matching the "x comma y comma delta 2" record for a win in the second gap.

Step 5 — drop pixels where top two gaps tie, such as \(D_{1} = 30\) versus \(D_{2} = 31\); the 1-point margin falls below cutoff, so the pixel drops.

Step 6 — finish pooling survivors across octaves into one key point list, so pooling gathers every octave gap into a shared pool with positions rescaled by octave (halve per doubling down).

Sense-check: size mapping still uses ratios. If octave A has base \(\sigma_{0}\) and octave B has \(2\sigma_{0}\), positions halve or double when moving between them, so a point at \((100, 80)\) fine maps to \((50, 40)\) coarse. That yields shift plus scale tolerance in one pass. Rotation handling stays pending here and arrives with orientation assignment, since each octave gap alone gives no rotation fix.

Scope: the traced sizes (250 down to 100) are demo grids that show pooling; shipped code halves exactly (256 to 128 to 64) so resampling stays exact. Either way the rule holds: compare inside the octave, rescale across octaves.

Recap: inside-octave gaps give size, cross-octave ratios give scale steps, pooling gathers all survivors. Bridge: angle is the last missing piece before live matching.

7.7.3 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why take a difference at all, and how does the difference give the right blob peak map?

A: The difference mimics the normalized LoG shape. A first slope needs one pass over neighbors, such as 20 minus 10. A second slope needs two passes. Across \(\sigma = 1\) to \(\sigma = k\) that extra pass repeats everywhere. The minus of two blurs gives nearly the same peak map with only blur plus minus cost, so the winning gap directly gives the blob width. The heat-equation line above shows the match is not luck: the gap equals \((k-1)\sigma^{2}\nabla^{2}G\) up to a constant factor.

Q: Do location-tolerant features come from this step even when the same object is shot from a new camera angle, like the rotated monument views?

A: So far this step gives shift plus scale tolerance from octave plus \(\sigma\) ratios. Angle tolerance is not yet handled here. It comes from orientation assignment, where each blob stores its own principal direction and matching rotates patches back to a shared frame. Octave gap rotation stays pending until that stored angle arrives.

Q: Is the difference taken inside one octave or across octaves, and are octave results merged at the end?

A: Differences run inside each octave only. Peaks are found per octave with the 26-neighbor test. Then all octave survivors are pooled into one key point list for the image. Within-octave gaps give size, cross-octave ratios give scale steps.

Q: When a peak comes from a minus of two blurs, which \(\sigma\) goes into the fingerprint?

A: The winning gap goes in. If blurs at \(\sigma_{1}\) and \(\sigma_{2}\) form the winning minus, store the gap label such as \((x, y, \Delta_{2})\). Positions match across the gap since all blurs in one octave share the same \((x, y)\) grid. The shipped pipeline then refines that gap label to a sub-pixel \(\sigma_{\text{peak}}\) with a quadratic fit.

7.8 Orientation Assignment and Descriptor Matching with Live Demo

7.8.1 Mathematical Formulation

Two blobs agree in position and size but one copy is turned 55 degrees. Without a stored angle, can any distance score still call them twins?

Size plus position is not enough. A turned copy must still match. The fix assigns each blob a principal direction from local gradients, much like a histogram of gradients over the patch. The demo histogram used wide 20-degree bins (0, 20, 40, 60, 80, all the way round) with the 80-degree bin winning at weight about 181 — a coarse on-screen version of the shipped setting, which uses 36 bins of 10 degrees each, weights by gradient magnitude times a Gaussian fall-off with distance from the center, keeps every peak within 80 percent of the global max (so one spot can yield two directed copies), and sharpens each peak with a three-bin parabolic fit.

At each pixel in the patch, gradient strength and direction are:

\[ m(x, y) = \sqrt{G_{x}(x, y)^{2} + G_{y}(x, y)^{2}} \]

\[ \theta(x, y) = \operatorname{atan2}\left(G_{y}(x, y), G_{x}(x, y)\right) \]

where \(G_{x}\) is the horizontal image slope, \(G_{y}\) is the vertical image slope, \(m \in \mathbb{R}\) with \(m \ge 0\) is gradient strength, and \(\theta \in [0, 2\pi)\) is gradient direction. Votes of weight \(m\) fall into direction bins. The bin with the tallest total is the principal orientation — the direction with the most gradient weight. That angle joins the fingerprint, giving \((x, y, \sigma, \theta)\) per blob.

Gradient magnitude orientation histogram with principal direction. Around each \((x, y, \sigma)\) the patch gradients vote by strength into direction bins under a Gaussian window; the tallest bin (plus near-tallest repeats) becomes \(\theta\). Standard code uses 36 bins with parabolic sharpening; the demo 20-degree bins show the same vote-tally idea at coarser grain.

The descriptor then hardens this directed patch into a matchable vector. Around the directed, scaled 16 by 16 window, gradients are pooled into a 4 by 4 grid of cells with 8 direction bins each (trilinear soft voting across position plus angle, Gaussian down-weighting far pixels), giving \(4 \times 4 \times 8 = 128\) values. The 128-vector is normalized to unit length, clipped at 0.2, and renormalized, which removes mean brightness and caps contrast spikes for light tolerance.

Matching then compares full tuples. For blobs \(p = (x, y, \sigma, \theta)\) and \(q = (x', y', \sigma', \theta')\), a simple Euclidean gap is:

\[ d(p, q) = \sqrt{(x-x')^{2} + (y-y')^{2} + (\sigma-\sigma')^{2} + (\theta-\theta')^{2}} \]

where \(d \in \mathbb{R}\) with \(d \ge 0\) is the gap. A Hamming gap over binary descriptor bits is also common:

\[ d_{H}(a, b) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[a_{i} \ne b_{i}\right] \]

where \(a, b \in \{0,1\}^{n}\) are binary descriptors (as in ORB or BRISK with \(n = 128\) to \(512\)), \([ \cdot ]\) is one when bits differ and zero otherwise, and \(d_H\) counts differing bits. A near-zero gap means a strong match. A gap near one, after scaling, means no match. In practice each blob also carries the longer 128-vector of binned gradients around it, and the same gap idea applies over that vector with nearest-neighbor search plus a nearest-versus-second-nearest ratio test.

Rotation fix in words: if blob one points at 80 degrees and its twin points at 135 degrees, turn the second patch back by the 55-degree gap before comparing. After that turn, position, size, and pattern line up. Numerically the angle term contributes \((80-135)^{2}\) before correction but near zero after both patches rotate to their own \(\theta = 0\) frame.

Picture the orientation histogram. The horizontal axis runs 0 to 360 degrees, the vertical axis stacks gradient weight. A directed corner shows one skyscraper bin (say 80 degrees at 181) above a low town of rivals; a blank patch shows a flat town with no tower and earns no trusted angle. The takeaway: tower height decides whether the angle — and the point — survives.

Scope: one principal angle repairs in-plane turns of textured patches. It fails on direction-free patches (perfect disks vote everywhere), on 180-degree-symmetric patterns (twin towers tie), and under out-of-plane tilt, where one global turn cannot undo the warp.

A common pitfall is to average signed gradients instead of voting: opposite edges cancel in the mean and report zero direction on a strong corner. Histogram voting avoids the cancel. A second pitfall is to compare raw \((x, y)\) across zoomed images without rescaling by \(\sigma\) and octave; positions must ride the same scale frame or twins score as strangers.

Recap: gradients vote, the tallest bin names \(\theta\), the directed patch yields a 128-vector, and gap distance names twins. Bridge: the live shoe retrieval below runs this whole chain on real photos. Exam note: be ready to write the \(m\) plus \(\theta\) pair and to state the rotate-back-before-compare rule.

7.8.2 Worked Example: Shoe Retrieval, Rotation, and False Matches

Shoe shelf brute-force rotation with pillar false matches — fully traced.

Setup: a query image shows a shoe. A shelf photo holds many items including that shoe. Goal: find the shoe box in the shelf.

Step 1 — build DoG images for both photos with \(\sigma_{1}\) and \(\sigma_{2}\) blurs and their minus image, across octaves as in section 7.7.

Step 2 — threshold weak peaks. Strong blobs stay on the shoe, face, and pillar regions. Flat wall blobs drop because their curves tie.

Step 3 — draw kept blob circles on the original for a visual check. Circle center marks \((x, y)\), radius marks \(\sigma\), spoke marks \(\theta\).

Step 4 — build SIFT points through OpenCV Contrib, since the patented core is absent from the base install. Read per-point fields: position, \(\sigma\), octave, and angle. A typical shoe point reads \((x, y) = (310, 210)\), \(\sigma = 2.4\), octave 1, angle 80 degrees.

Step 5 — run brute-force matching. With about 1000 points per image, that is about 1000 by 1000 gap checks with nearest-neighbor search over 128-vectors. Each query vector keeps its best and second-best shelf gaps; a best gap of 0.22 against a second best of 0.71 (ratio about 0.31) locks as a twin, while 0.55 against 0.60 stays undecided.

Step 6 — keep good pairs. The shoe patch in the query links to the shoe patch in the shelf photo, and a consensus fit over all kept pairs confirms one shared shift-scale-turn.

Now turn the shelf photo by 90 degrees and repeat. Matches persist because each patch is turned back to its principal direction first. But some wrong links appear too, such as shoe points linking to pillar points. The cause is threshold choice. With loose cutoffs, extra weak blobs join and chance ties grow — a shoe edge at 80 degrees then ties with a pillar edge at a nearby vote. Hand-tuning one cutoff per image does not scale. That gap leads to the next method, random sampling with consensus, which picks a cutoff and match set that most pairs agree on by keeping the largest geometrically agreeing subset and dropping strays.

Sense-check: rotation tolerance comes from the stored angle, not from luck — strip \(\theta\) out and the 90-degree rerun collapses, while loose thresholds trade recall for stray matches.

Brute-force nearest-neighbor search over SIFT vectors with a consensus filter still serves in catalog search, panorama builders, and fast trackers where training a network is too heavy. The 1000-by-1000 check is one million 128-length gaps per image pair — trivial beside a network training run — and indexing trims it further at store scale.

Scope: brute force plus consensus fits mid-size stores with rigid same-object copies. It strains past millions of images without an index and breaks on wide class spread, where dog-breed-level variation needs learned models with varied samples rather than one template.

Recap: directed descriptors survive a 90-degree turn; loose cutoffs buy strays, consensus spends them away. Bridge: the Q and A below ties the notebook columns back to each pipeline stage.

Key Industry Applications

Automatic panorama stitching joins zoomed and wide shots by matching peak-type patches and solving shift, scale, and turn — the same pull, match, align, and blend loop traced on the ridge peak.

Visual SLAM with single-camera and stereo rigs tracks road points frame by frame to map paths in real time, holding lane position on modest compute without learned weights.

Mars rover localization matches a small soil view against a wide terrain map to fix position, proving the small-against-wide match works off-planet as well as on roads.

Catalog and image search finds books, discs, shoes, and apparel regions inside cluttered photos, including rotated copies, by counting agreeing fingerprint pairs; phone retail search that returns shopping links from a highlighted clothing region uses the same retrieval backbone.

Driver-assist tracking and motion tracking link the same patch over video frames for objects, lanes, and paths, while 3D reconstruction lifts matched points from aligned views into depth once stitching locks the pairs.

Blobs as Interest Points

Must-know: Straight edges lack position lock along their length; blobs add two-direction texture plus size and direction.

\[ p = (x, y, \sigma, \theta, v) \]

Top pitfall: Treating a strong straight edge as a point identity although dots along it all tie.

Self-check: Why do two dots on one straight pillar edge match wrongly?

Connects to: 7.1, 7.5

Gaussian Smoothing, Derivatives, and Characteristic Scale

Must-know: Smoothing-slope associativity saves a pass; sigma-squared boost makes sizes comparable; peak across sigma is characteristic scale.

\[ R(x; \sigma) = \sigma^{2} \cdot (f \ast d^{2}G/dx^{2})(x; \sigma) \]

Top pitfall: Reading raw unboosted second slopes as size so the small end always wins.

Self-check: Map (10,10) at 1x to 2x and state the sigma-ratio rule.

Connects to: 7.4, 7.6

Laplacian of Gaussian and Scale-Space Extrema

Must-know: Normalized LoG over blur plus octaves finds 3D peaks; lone tall peak stays, flat ties drop.

\[ R(x, y; \sigma) = \sigma^{2} \cdot ((\nabla^{2}G(\cdot; \sigma)) \ast I)(x, y) \]

Top pitfall: Reading sigma labels as derivative orders instead of blur widths.

Self-check: Why does a face point survive while a flat wall point drops?

Connects to: 7.5, 7.7

Difference of Gaussians and Keypoint Localization

Must-know: DoG replaces normalized LoG to avoid second partials: blur sweep, neighbor minus, 26-neighbor peak plus threshold, pool octaves.

\[ D(x, y; \sigma) = L(x, y; k\sigma) - L(x, y; \sigma) \]

Top pitfall: Differencing across octaves instead of inside one octave, smearing positions.

Self-check: State the DoG shortcut in one line with its three build steps.
