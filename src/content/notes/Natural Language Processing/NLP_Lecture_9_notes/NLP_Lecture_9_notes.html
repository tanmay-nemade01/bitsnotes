Parsing and Syntactic Analysis

This lecture introduces the core machinery of syntactic parsing. We cover how to represent language grammar (Context-Free Grammars), how to build parse trees algorithmically (chart parsing, CKY), how to choose the best tree (Probabilistic CFGs), and how to measure parser quality (labeled precision/recall/F₁). The journey begins with ambiguity — the problem that makes parsing necessary. It ends with the modern insight that transformers implicitly learn the same structural relations these algorithms compute.

9.1 Ambiguity in Natural Language

Hook. A witness runs up to you and says: "I saw the man with the telescope!" Did the witness look through a telescope to see a man? Or did the witness see a man who was carrying a telescope? Both readings are grammatically flawless — and a computer has no common sense to fall back on. This is the problem that parsing exists to solve.

9.1.1 Definition and Explanation

Intuition + Analogy. Think of a sentence as a set of Lego bricks. The bricks (words) are the same, but you can snap them together in different ways. "I saw the man with the telescope" is one pile of bricks. You can attach the "with the telescope" brick to "the man" (the man owns the telescope) or to "saw" (you used the telescope to see). Both structures are physically valid — but only one matches what the speaker meant. Parsing is the rulebook that tells you which attachments are legal, and probabilistic parsing adds the statistics that tell you which one is most likely.

The analogy breaks when sentences grow long: with Lego, you can see the pieces. With language, the attachment choices are invisible — they exist only in the abstract structure.

Ambiguity is one of the central challenges in any natural language. It causes misunderstanding and miscommunication because statements can carry multiple interpretations. In technical terms, a single sentence can have multiple parses — different ways of assigning structure to the same string of words.

Consider the sentence: I saw the man with the telescope. This has two interpretations:

  1. The man has the telescope, and I saw that man.
  2. I used the telescope to see the man.

The phrase with the telescope can attach either to the man (making it a prepositional phrase modifier of the noun) or to the verb saw (making it an adverbial modifier). This is called structural ambiguity or attachment ambiguity.

Formalizing Ambiguity. A sentence has structural ambiguity when a single sequence of words admits more than one valid parse tree under the same grammar. The number of possible parse trees for a sentence with \(n\) prepositional phrases grows according to the Catalan numbers:

\[ C_n = \frac{1}{n+1} \binom{2n}{n} \]

For \(n=1\) (one PP): 2 parses. \(n=2\): 5 parses. \(n=3\): 14 parses. \(n=4\): 132 parses. The growth is exponential — a sentence with five PPs already admits 469 parses.

This is not just a theoretical curiosity. Real sentences in news, legal text, and conversation routinely contain multiple prepositional phrases, coordination, and relative clauses. Each additional structural choice multiplies the ambiguity.

Some further examples that drive the point home:

  • We are looking for a thief with a bicycle. Attach with a bicycle to thief — correct. Attach it to looking — absurd (looking using a bicycle?), but grammatically possible.
  • Why is the teacher wearing sunglasses? Because her students are so bright. A pun exploiting lexical ambiguity — bright meaning both "intelligent" and "emitting light." This is lexical, not structural, ambiguity — but sentences often mix both types.

Assumptions & Scope. Parsing assumes the sentence is grammatical — i.e., that the words can be arranged into a valid tree under the grammar. Real-world text often contains fragments, typos, and ungrammatical constructions. A parser will either fail (no valid tree) or return a low-probability parse. Parsing also assumes the grammar rules are correct and complete for the domain; a grammar trained on Wall Street Journal text may perform poorly on Twitter or medical text. Finally, parsing addresses only syntactic structure — it does not resolve semantic ambiguities like word sense ("bank" = river or financial institution).

Visual Intuition. Picture two trees side by side for "I saw the man with the telescope." Both have S at the root, NP ("I") as the subject, and VP ("saw the man with the telescope") as the predicate. In Tree 1, the PP "with the telescope" branches off NP ("the man") — it is a child of NP. In Tree 2, the same PP branches off VP ("saw...") — it is a child of VP. The two trees have identical leaves (same words, same order) but different internal branch points. The takeaway: ambiguity lives in the structure, not the words. If you drew only the words in order, you would see no difference at all.

Pitfalls.

  1. Confusing lexical and structural ambiguity. Lexical ambiguity is about word meanings ("bank" the river vs. "bank" the institution). Structural ambiguity is about phrase attachment. A sentence can have both types simultaneously.
  2. Thinking more words = more ambiguity. Not always. A highly constrained sentence like "John sleeps" has exactly one parse. Ambiguity comes from optional modifiers (PPs, relative clauses, coordination) that can attach at multiple points in the tree.
  3. Assuming the first parse you think of is the only one. Humans are extremely good at disambiguating using world knowledge and context. Computers are not. When solving parsing problems, always list all grammatically valid parses before choosing one.
  4. Forgetting that ambiguity compounds multiplicatively. Each PP roughly doubles the parse count. A 10-word sentence with 3 PPs can already have 14+ parses. This is exactly why naive parsing without dynamic programming is infeasible — the search space explodes.

Recap. Ambiguity is the exponential explosion of possible syntactic structures for a single sentence — governed by Catalan numbers. Parsing is the systematic way to enumerate and choose among these structures. Next: the formal rulebook that defines which structures are legal — Context-Free Grammars.

9.1.2 Industry Applications

Every NLP application that must understand or generate language deals with ambiguity. Conversational AI systems, machine translation engines, grammar checkers like Grammarly, and question-answering systems all rely on parsing — either explicitly or implicitly — to resolve which meaning is intended.

Real-World & Domain Connection. The attachment ambiguity problem directly impacts machine translation. If the source language uses case markers to disambiguate attachment (e.g., Russian uses instrumental case for "telescope" as instrument vs. genitive for possession), but the target language (English) does not, the translation system must infer the correct attachment from context. Google Translate and DeepL use neural models that implicitly learn these attachment preferences from billions of sentence pairs. The same ambiguity appears in legal document parsing — a contract clause with multiple prepositional phrases can admit readings that differ by millions of dollars in liability. In bioNLP (parsing biomedical literature), prepositional phrase attachment errors can misattribute a drug's effect to the wrong protein — a potentially dangerous mistake.

9.2 Grammar and Context-Free Grammar

Hook. You are building a house. Before you can lay a single brick, you need a blueprint — a set of rules that says walls go here, doors go there, the roof sits on top. A grammar is the blueprint of a language. It tells you which word groups (phrases) are legal and how they combine. Without a grammar, a parser is just guessing.

9.2.1 What Makes a Grammar

Intuition + Analogy. Every language has a "default wiring pattern." In English, you say "I eat mango" (Subject-Verb-Object). In Hindi, you say "mein aam khata hun" (Subject-Object-Verb — the verb goes last). These are not random preferences — they are rules. A grammar is the full set of these rules: word order, phrase structure, tense, number agreement. Just as a C++ compiler rejects int x = "hello" because the types do not match, a syntactic parser rejects word sequences that violate the grammar.

The analogy breaks because natural language grammars are far messier than programming language grammars. English has exceptions, idioms, and constructions that flout the rules ("Book that flight" has no visible subject). A good grammar handles the regular cases systematically and the exceptions with special rules.

Every language — English, Indian languages, French, German — has a specific set of grammatical rules. These rules govern word order and phrase structure.

  • In English: Subject — Verb — Object (e.g., I eat mango)
  • In Indian languages: Subject — Object — Verb (e.g., mein aam khata hun — the verb comes at the end)

A grammar also encodes tense, number agreement, and other morpho-syntactic constraints. Just as programming languages have a formal grammar that a compiler checks, natural languages have grammatical structures that parsing verifies and exploits.

A good grammar must be generic — a standard set of rules applicable across words, domains, and applications. These rules have been developed over time for many languages.

9.2.2 Context-Free Grammar (CFG)

Formal Definition of a CFG. A Context-Free Grammar \(G\) is a 4-tuple \((N, \Sigma, R, S)\):

  • \(N\) — a finite set of non-terminal symbols (phrasal categories: S, NP, VP, PP, etc.). These are the abstract building blocks that get expanded.
  • \(\Sigma\) — a finite set of terminal symbols (the actual words or POS tags: "the", "dog", N, V, Adj). These are the leaves of the parse tree. \(N \cap \Sigma = \emptyset\).
  • \(R\) — a finite set of production rules (or rewrite rules). Each rule has the form \(A \rightarrow \beta\) where \(A \in N\) (a single non-terminal) and \(\beta \in (N \cup \Sigma)^*\) (any sequence of terminals and non-terminals).
  • \(S\) — the start symbol, \(S \in N\). Every valid parse tree must have S as its root.

The term "context-free" means the expansion of a non-terminal \(A\) does not depend on what surrounds it — the rule \(A \rightarrow \beta\) applies regardless of context. This is both the strength (simplicity) and the weakness (no contextual conditioning) of CFGs.

Context-Free Grammar is a concept borrowed from Theory of Computer Science, originally developed for parsing programming languages (ALGOL, in Chomsky's original work). The same formalism applies to natural languages.

A grammar can be represented mathematically using two kinds of symbols:

  • Non-terminals: Phrasal categories like NP, VP, S. These are the building blocks that get expanded.
  • Terminals: The actual words or their part-of-speech tags (lexicons). These are the leaves of the parse tree.

A grammar also contains a set of production rules (or rewrite rules) of the form:

\[ A \rightarrow B \; C \]

meaning "A can be rewritten as B followed by C." The left-hand side is always a single non-terminal. The right-hand side is a sequence of terminals and/or non-terminals.

These rules are called context-free because the expansion of a non-terminal does not depend on what surrounds it — the rule applies regardless of context.

The head is the word that carries the core meaning; the other words in the phrase are modifiers or complements of the head.

9.2.4 Parse Trees

A parse tree (also called a phrase-structure tree or derivation tree) is a rooted, ordered tree that represents the syntactic structure of a sentence according to a CFG:

  • The root is S (the start symbol).
  • Internal nodes are non-terminals (NP, VP, PP, etc.).
  • Leaf nodes are terminals (the actual words, read left to right).
  • Edges represent the "is-composed-of" relation — a parent node is rewritten as its children.

The parse tree is the output of a parser. It captures two kinds of information: (1) constituency — which words group together into which phrases, and (2) hierarchical structure — how phrases nest inside other phrases.

A parse tree is a tree-structured representation of how a sentence breaks into sub-parts and how those parts relate. The root is S (sentence). Internal nodes are non-terminals (NP, VP). Leaf nodes are terminals (words or POS tags). The edges encode which phrase a word belongs to, and in what order.

The parse tree shows relations: NP is related to VP; together they constitute S. This relation information is what parsing algorithms compute.

Visual Intuition. Imagine a family tree turned upside down. The sentence S is the ancestor at the top. Its children are NP and VP. NP's children might be Det ("the") and N ("dog"). VP's children might be V ("chased") and NP (Det "the", N "cat"). The leaves at the bottom, read left to right, spell "the dog chased the cat." Every word is a leaf on exactly one branch, and the branching pattern reveals who is doing what to whom.

9.2.5 How Parsing Works — High-Level Intuition

Given:

  • The words of a sentence
  • Their part-of-speech tags (lexicons)
  • A set of CFG rules

The parser's job is to find the tree structure — which words group into which phrases, and how those phrases combine into the full sentence.

Assumptions & Scope. CFGs assume constituency — that sentences decompose into nested phrases. This works well for English and many European languages but is less natural for free-word-order languages (e.g., Sanskrit, Turkish) where constituency is flatter and dependency relations dominate. CFGs also assume the grammar is known in advance — in practice, grammar rules are either hand-written by linguists or extracted from treebanks. A hand-written grammar is precise but incomplete (does not cover all constructions). A treebank-extracted grammar is broad but noisy (contains rare, possibly erroneous rules). Finally, CFGs are context-free, meaning the rule \(NP \rightarrow Det \; N\) applies identically whether the NP is a subject, object, or inside a PP. This independence assumption is violated in real language — the choice of NP structure can depend on its grammatical role.

Pitfalls.

  1. Confusing terminals and non-terminals. Terminals are words (or POS tags representing word classes). Non-terminals are phrase labels (NP, VP). A rule like \(NP \rightarrow Det \; N\) has only non-terminals on the RHS. A rule like \(Det \rightarrow \textit{the}\) has a terminal on the RHS. Keep the two types separate.
  2. Forgetting the start symbol constraint. Every valid parse MUST have S at the root. If you build a tree whose root is VP, you have a phrase fragment, not a complete sentence parse.
  3. Thinking a grammar can cover everything. No finite CFG covers all of English. There will always be edge cases (poetry, headlines, informal speech). A parser's job is to handle the common cases well, not to be perfect.
  4. Mixing up phrase types. The head determines the phrase type. "Very intelligent" is an AP (head: intelligent, adjective), not an AdvP, even though it starts with the adverb very.

Student Q&A (deduplicated).

Q: Are we expected to determine the part-of-speech tags ourselves, or will they be given?
A: The POS tags and the grammar rules will both be given in exam problems. Your task is to apply the rules to build the parse tree — not to figure out the tags from scratch.

Q: Do different libraries use different tags and rules?
A: Yes. Libraries like the Stanford Parser use slightly different conventions — for example, some use Nominal for what others call Noun. The differences come from training data and annotation scheme. The same algorithms (CKY, chart parsing) work regardless of the tag set.

Q: How is parsing done at web scale on real-world data?
A: Today, parsing is mostly done implicitly by transformer models (BERT, GPT). The attention mechanism computes probabilities over word pairs. It learns which words relate to which — effectively doing parsing inside a black box. The logic matches the explicit algorithms, but it happens inside neural network weights instead of symbolic rules. Understanding the explicit version remains important. It is explainable and forms the foundation. When a transformer makes a parsing error, you debug it by reasoning about attachment and constituency — the same concepts we study here.

Recap. A Context-Free Grammar \((N, \Sigma, R, S)\) is the formal rulebook that defines which phrase structures are legal. Parse trees are the visual output — they show constituency and hierarchy. Next: what can you actually do with a parse tree? The applications.

9.3 Applications of Parsing

Hook. You type "Book a flight to Paris" into a travel chatbot. The chatbot needs to know: is "book" a verb (make a reservation) or a noun (a physical book)? Is "to Paris" the destination of the flight or the recipient of the book? Without parsing, the chatbot is guessing. With parsing, it knows the structure — and acts correctly.

Intuition. Parsing is not an end in itself. It is a scaffolding — a structural representation that other NLP components build on. Sentiment analysis needs to know which adjective modifies which entity. Machine translation needs to know the word order of both languages. Question answering needs to resolve who refers to what. In every case, the parse tree provides the wiring diagram that connects words to their syntactic roles.

Think of parsing as the framing of a house. You do not live in the frame, but you cannot hang drywall, run plumbing, or install windows without it. Similarly, you cannot do deep language understanding without knowing the syntactic structure.

Parsing is required for virtually every interesting NLP application. Even when modern transformers handle it implicitly, the need for structural understanding remains.

  • Sentiment Analysis: Adjectives and adjective phrases carry sentiment. Parsing helps identify which adjective modifies which entity. For example, I like frozen could mean a movie (Frozen, a named entity) or a food dish (frozen yogurt, where frozen is an adjective). The parse determines the interpretation.
  • Relation Extraction: In conversational AI, when a user says "give me topics about a particular NLP area," parsing captures the relation: topic is within NLP area. These relations are critical for understanding user intent and for generating grammatically correct responses.
  • Question Answering: Resolving references like who in Who was the first man in space? requires knowing that who refers to a person (the first man), not to space. Parsing captures these dependency relations.
  • Speech Recognition (Speech-to-Text): Converting speech to text benefits from knowing which word sequences are grammatically plausible. A speech recognizer that knows "the dog barks" is grammatical and "the dog bark" is not can use the parse probability to disambiguate acoustically similar hypotheses.
  • Machine Translation: Translating between languages requires knowing the grammar of both the source language and the target language. For example, English is SVO; Hindi is SOV. Parsing the source helps reorder words for the target. This is a major area of work — projects like AI for Bharat involve extensive machine translation research.
  • Grammar Checking: Tools like Grammarly implicitly use parsing to check whether a sentence follows grammatical rules. The relations among words determine whether the sentence is well-formed.

Worked Example: Sentiment + Attachment.

Consider the sentence: The movie had great visuals but a terrible plot.

A naive word-level sentiment system sees "great" (+1) and "terrible" (−1) and may average them to neutral. But parsing reveals:

  • "great" modifies "visuals" (the NP great visuals)
  • "terrible" modifies "plot" (the NP a terrible plot)
  • The conjunction "but" signals contrast between the two clauses

The parse tree tells the sentiment system: there are two distinct aspects (visuals: positive; plot: negative), not a single averaged score. Aspect-based sentiment analysis fundamentally depends on this parse-level disambiguation.

Sense-check: Without parsing, "great visuals but terrible plot" and "terrible visuals but great plot" look identical to a bag-of-words model. Parsing reveals they are opposites.

Real-World & Domain Connection. The most impactful application of parsing today is conversational AI — chatbots, voice assistants (Alexa, Siri, Google Assistant), and customer service automation. Consider a user saying "Remind me to buy milk when I get to the store." The system must extract the action (remind), the task (buy milk), and the trigger (when I get to the store). Each is a different constituent with a different semantic role. Modern systems use transformers (BERT, GPT) where attention implicitly captures what parsing once did. The key-query-value mechanism computes pairwise word affinities — learning syntactic relations without building an explicit tree. When these models fail on complex sentences, debugging still needs reasoning about constituency and attachment. Those are the concepts explicit parsing teaches.

Real-world: All these are covered by modern transformer-based systems. The attention mechanism (key, query, value) that powers GPT, BERT, and other models implicitly captures the word relations that parsing makes explicit. The upcoming sessions on contextual word embeddings will discuss this internals.

Recap. Parsing is the structural scaffolding for virtually all downstream NLP: sentiment, relation extraction, QA, speech recognition, translation, and grammar checking. Modern transformers learn these structures implicitly, but the concepts remain essential for debugging and explanation. Next: how do we actually build a parse tree? 9.4 Parsing Algorithms — Top-Down and Bottom-Up

Hook. You have the grammar rulebook and a sentence. Now what? You need an algorithm — a systematic procedure that applies the rules to find the tree. There are exactly two directions you can build from: start at the root and work down, or start at the leaves and work up. Both work. Both have the same fatal flaw — and understanding that flaw is what motivates the smarter algorithms that follow.

9.4.1 Top-Down Parsing

Purpose. A top-down parser starts from the start symbol S and recursively expands non-terminals using grammar rules until the leaf sequence matches the input words. It is goal-directed — it asks "what could this sentence be?" and tests hypotheses against the data.

Inputs & Outputs.

  • Input: A sequence of words \(w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n\), their POS tags, and a CFG \((N, \Sigma, R, S)\).
  • Output: A parse tree rooted at S whose leaves (left-to-right) are exactly \(w_1 \ldots w_n\), or failure if no such tree exists.

Steps (simplified top-down, leftmost expansion):

  1. Initialize the tree with root S and a pointer at the first word \(w_1\).
  2. Pick the leftmost unexpanded non-terminal in the current tree.
  3. Choose a grammar rule \(A \rightarrow \beta\) that expands it.
  4. Replace the non-terminal with the RHS symbols \(\beta\).
  5. If a terminal is generated, match it against the current input word. If it matches, advance the word pointer. If not, backtrack — undo the last rule choice and try another.
  6. Repeat from step 2 until all words are consumed and no non-terminals remain (success) or all rule choices are exhausted (failure).

Trace: Top-Down on "Book that flight."

Grammar: S → NP VP | VP. VP → V NP. NP → Det N. Det → that. N → flight. V → book.

  1. Start: root = S. Input: [book, that, flight].
  2. Expand S → NP VP. Tree: S(NP, VP). Leftmost non-terminal: NP.
  3. Expand NP → Det N. Tree: S(NP(Det, N), VP). Leftmost: Det.
  4. Expand Det → that. Terminal "that" ≠ "book" → mismatch. Backtrack.
  5. Undo Det expansion. Try another NP rule... None left for this path. Backtrack further.
  6. Undo NP expansion. Try S → VP. Tree: S(VP). Leftmost: VP.
  7. Expand VP → V NP. Tree: S(VP(V, NP)). Leftmost: V.
  8. Expand V → book. Terminal "book" = "book" → match! Word pointer advances.
  9. Leftmost: NP. Expand NP → Det N. Leftmost: Det.
  10. Expand Det → that. Terminal "that" = "that" → match! Advance.
  11. Expand N → flight. Terminal "flight" = "flight" → match! All words consumed. Success.

Sense-check: The parser explored a dead-end path (S → NP VP) before finding the correct one (S → VP). This backtracking is exactly what makes naive parsing slow on ambiguous sentences.

9.4.2 Bottom-Up Parsing

Purpose. A bottom-up parser starts from the input words and combines them into increasingly larger constituents using grammar rules in reverse, until the entire sequence reduces to S. It is data-driven — it asks "what can I build from these words?" and assembles upward.

Inputs & Outputs. Same as top-down: words + POS tags + CFG → parse tree or failure.

Steps (simplified bottom-up, shift-reduce style):

  1. Start with an empty stack and the input words as a queue.
  2. Shift: Move the next input word onto the stack.
  3. Reduce: If the top \(k\) symbols on the stack match the RHS of some rule \(A \rightarrow X_1 \ldots X_k\), replace them with \(A\).
  4. Repeat shift and reduce until the input is empty and the stack contains only S (success), or no more moves are possible (failure).

Trace: Bottom-Up on "Book that flight."

Same grammar. Input: [book, that, flight]. Stack: [].

  1. Shift "book" (POS: V). Stack: [V].
  2. No rule RHS = "V" alone. Shift "that" (POS: Det). Stack: [V, Det].
  3. No rule RHS = "V Det". Shift "flight" (POS: N). Stack: [V, Det, N].
  4. Reduce Det N → NP. Stack: [V, NP].
  5. Reduce V NP → VP. Stack: [VP].
  6. Reduce VP → S. Stack: [S]. Input empty. Success.

Sense-check: Bottom-up never explored the dead-end S → NP VP path because it only builds what the words support. However, on ambiguous sentences, bottom-up can build many partial constituents that never connect to S — wasting work the other way.

When to Use / Alternatives. Both top-down and bottom-up are pedagogical algorithms — they teach the concepts but are not used in practice for real NLP. Their fatal flaw is backtracking: when a choice fails, the parser undoes work and tries alternatives, often re-deriving the same sub-constituents many times. On ambiguous sentences, this causes exponential runtime. The solution — dynamic programming (chart parsing, CKY, Earley) — stores and reuses intermediate results so that no sub-tree is ever built twice. These are covered in the next two sections.

These basic algorithms (top-down and bottom-up) are no longer used in practice, but understanding them is essential for understanding the more advanced algorithms — dependency parsing and neural parsing — that are used today.

9.5 Chart Parsing

Hook. Imagine solving a jigsaw puzzle by trying every piece in every position, and every time you get stuck, you sweep all the pieces off the table and start over. That is naive top-down/bottom-up parsing. Chart parsing is the smarter approach: once you confirm two pieces fit together, you glue them and never re-examine that connection. The "chart" is your table of glued-together sub-puzzles.

9.5.1 Core Idea

Intuition + Analogy. Chart parsing is dynamic programming applied to syntax. You compute and store every valid phrase (constituent) for every substring of the input exactly once. When a later step needs that same substring's analysis, you look it up in O(1) instead of recomputing it. This is the same idea as memoizing Fibonacci — compute fib(5) once, store it, reuse it when computing fib(6) and fib(7).

The analogy: imagine you are parsing "The large can hold the water." When you prove that "the" (positions 1-2) is an Article, you write it on a sticky note and put it on the chart. When "the" appears again at positions 6-7, you do not re-prove it — you just look at the existing sticky note. The chart is the collection of all sticky notes, organized by their position spans.

Purpose. Chart parsing solves the redundant-work problem of naive parsers. It uses a chart data structure to store every completed constituent along with its span (start and end positions). No substring is ever parsed twice.

Inputs & Outputs.

  • Input: Words \(w_1 \ldots w_n\), POS tags for each word (possibly ambiguous), and a CFG \((N, \Sigma, R, S)\).
  • Output: All possible parse trees (or a compact representation of them) for the input sentence.

Key terminology:

TermMeaning
ChartThe data structure storing all processed (completed) constituents, indexed by span
KeyThe current word or constituent being processed
Active ArcA grammar rule currently being applied, with a dot (·) indicating how much has been processed
AgendaConstituents still to be processed (the to-do list)

The dot notation is central: in the rule NP → Art · Adj N, the dot means "we have processed an Article; we still need an Adjective followed by a Noun to complete this NP."

Steps (bottom-up chart parsing):

  1. Initialize: Mark positions 0 through \(n\) between words. Set the agenda to contain all individual word POS tags at their spans.
  2. Process agenda: Pop the next constituent from the agenda. Add it to the chart at its span.
  3. Activate rules: For each grammar rule whose RHS starts with this constituent's category, create an active arc starting at this constituent's position, with the dot after the first RHS symbol.
  4. Advance arcs: For each existing active arc that expects the current constituent's category as its next symbol, advance the dot. If the dot reaches the end of the RHS, the rule is complete → create a new constituent and add it to the agenda.
  5. Repeat from step 2 until the agenda is empty.
  6. Success if S spans [0, n] is in the chart. The chart now contains all possible constituents.

9.5.2 Bottom-Up Chart Parsing — Worked Example

Trace: "The large can hold the water."

Sentence: The large can hold the water. (7 words, positions 1-8)

Given POS tags:

Given Grammar Rules:

  1. S → NP VP
  2. NP → Art N
  3. NP → Art Adj N
  4. NP → Adj N
  5. VP → Aux VP
  6. VP → V NP

Step-by-step processing:

First, mark positions between words. Positions 1 through 8 separate the 7 words. The article the is at position 1-2, large at 2-3, can at 3-4, hold at 4-5, the at 5-6, water at 6-7, with a final position 7-8 for sentence end.

Position 1–2 (word: the, POS: Art):
Look at rules starting with Art → Rules 2 and 3 both begin with Art. Activate both:

  • NP → Art · N (Rule 2, dot after Art)
  • NP → Art · Adj N (Rule 3, dot after Art)

Rule 4 starts with Adj, not Art → discard for now.

Position 2–3 (word: large, POS: Adj):
From Rule 3 (NP → Art · Adj N), the next needed symbol after the dot is Adj. large is Adj → advance the dot:

  • NP → Art Adj · N (Rule 3, dot after Adj)

Rule 2 (NP → Art · N) expects N, but large is Adj → this arc cannot advance; Rule 2 is discarded for this path.

Position 3–4 (word: can, POS: N/Aux/V):
From Rule 3 (NP → Art Adj · N), the next needed symbol is N. can can be N → advance:

  • NP → Art Adj N · (Rule 3 complete — NP spans positions 1–4)

Now, having completed an NP covering positions 1–4, the S rule (S → NP VP) is partially satisfied. S needs a VP starting at position 4.

Position 4 onward — building the VP:

Word can at positions 4–5: It has three possible POS tags (N, Aux, V). For VP rules:

  • Rule 5: VP → Aux VP (starts with Aux) — applicable since can can be Aux
  • Rule 6: VP → V NP (starts with V) — applicable since can can be V
  • Rule with N is not applicable for VP (no VP rule starts with N)

Both Rules 5 and 6 are activated:

  • VP → Aux · VP
  • VP → V · NP

Position 5–6 (word: hold, POS: N/V):
For the active Rule 6 (VP → V · NP), we need an NP. NP rules start with Art or Adj. hold is N or V, neither Art nor Adj → Rule 6 cannot advance using hold as the start of NP. Rule 6 is discarded.

For Rule 5 (VP → Aux · VP), we need a VP. A VP can start with V (Rule 6). hold can be V → so we apply Rule 6 under Rule 5:

  • can is treated as Aux, and VP → V NP starts with hold as V.
  • VP → V · NP (active, with hold as V at positions 5–6)

Position 6–7 (word: the, POS: Art):
NP rules start with Art. Activate NP rules for positions 6–8:

  • NP → Art · N
  • NP → Art · Adj N

Position 7–8 (word: water, POS: N/V):
From NP → Art · N: water as N completes the NP → Art N (positions 6–8).
From NP → Art · Adj N: water is not Adj → discard.

Now we have NP at positions 6–8. Going back to VP → V · NP (with hold as V at 5–6): this NP at 6–8 completes Rule 6 → VP (V NP) spanning positions 5–8.

Finally, going back to Rule 5 (VP → Aux · VP): we have can as Aux at 4–5 and VP at 5–8 → VP (Aux VP) spanning positions 4–8.

Now S → NP VP is satisfied: NP at 1–4, VP at 4–8 → S spanning 1–8. Parse complete.

NP(1-4) → Art(1-2) Adj(2-3) N(3-4) [using Rule 3]
VP(4-8) → Aux(4-5) VP(5-8) [using Rule 5]
VP(5-8) → V(5-6) NP(6-8) [using Rule 6]
NP(6-8) → Art(6-7) N(7-8) [using Rule 2]

Words: the=Art, large=Adj, can=Aux, hold=V, the=Art, water=N.

                     S
                   /   \
                 NP     VP
               /  |  \    \
            Art  Adj  N    VP
             |    |   |   /  \
            the  large can Aux  VP
                          |   /  \
                         can  V   NP
                              |   /  \
                            hold Art  N
                                  |   |
                                 the water

Conceptually: the large can is the subject NP (a large container), and can hold the water is the VP (is able to hold the water). The parsing resolves that can here is an auxiliary verb, not a noun, because the NP already has its head noun satisfied.

Key takeaway: Chart parsing avoids re-computing: once the is recognized as Art at positions 1-2, that fact is stored in the chart. When the appears again at positions 6-7, the same rules are retrieved from the chart rather than recomputed.

Assumptions & Scope. Chart parsing assumes you have both a CFG and POS tags for every word (possibly ambiguous). In practice, a POS tagger runs first, and the parser works with the tagger's output. The chart can grow large — for an \(n\)-word sentence, there are \(O(n^2)\) possible spans, and each span can hold multiple constituents (due to ambiguity). In the worst case (highly ambiguous grammar + ambiguous POS tags), the chart can contain exponentially many entries. In practice, pruning heuristics (beam search, probability thresholds) keep the chart manageable.

Pitfalls.

  1. Forgetting to add completed constituents to the agenda. When an active arc completes (dot at end), the new constituent MUST go into the agenda so it can trigger further rule activations. Missing this step breaks the whole algorithm.
  2. Confusing the key with the chart. The key is the current item being processed. The chart is the permanent storage. The agenda is the queue of items waiting to be processed. Keep these three data structures distinct in your mind.
  3. Misreading the dot. The dot always sits between symbols on the RHS. NP → Art · Adj N means "Art found, need Adj then N." NP → Art Adj N · means "all three found, rule complete." The dot is never at the very beginning in an active arc (that is just an unstarted rule).
  4. Losing track of positions. Every constituent in the chart has a span [start, end]. The span is as important as the category. NP[1,4] is not the same as NP[5,8]. When advancing arcs, always check that spans are adjacent (end of first = start of second).

9.5.3 Student Questions on Chart Parsing

Student Q&A (deduplicated).

Q: Can you summarize the whole chart parsing process?
A: We have grammar rules and POS tags (lexicons). Using both, we build a parse tree showing relations among words. The "chart" is like a cache — it stores whatever we have already processed so we can reuse it later (like dynamic programming, e.g., Fibonacci with memoization). We process words left to right (bottom-up). For each word, we look at its POS tag and activate only the grammar rules that start with that POS tag. We advance the dot as each expected symbol is found. When a rule is complete (dot at the end), that constituent goes into the chart. We continue until S spans the entire sentence.

Q: Do we always need to check every combination?
A: The chart reduces duplication, but when there is ambiguity (like can having three POS tags), multiple rules are activated and some paths are later discarded when they fail to match the input. The process explores possibilities but stores intermediate results so no sub-problem is solved twice. This is the core of dynamic programming.

Recap. Chart parsing is dynamic programming for syntax — store every constituent exactly once, reuse via O(1) lookup. The chart eliminates the exponential backtracking of naive parsers. Next: adding probabilities to choose the best tree among many — Probabilistic CFGs.

9.6 Probabilistic Context-Free Grammar (PCFG)

Hook. Chart parsing solved the efficiency problem — it finds ALL valid parse trees without redundant work. But for "I saw the man on the hill with the telescope," that is 5 trees. Which one is correct? Without probabilities, a parser is like a librarian who, when asked for a book, hands you every book in the library and says "one of these." A PCFG is the librarian who knows which books are most popular.

9.6.1 Motivation

Intuition + Analogy. Imagine you are a detective investigating a crime scene. There are five different ways the events could have unfolded (five parse trees). Without additional evidence, you cannot choose among them. But if you have historical data — "in 80% of similar cases, the weapon was found near the victim, not carried by the suspect" — you can assign probabilities to each scenario. A PCFG does exactly this: it learns, from a large corpus of correctly parsed sentences (a treebank), which grammar rules are used how often, and in which contexts. The most probable parse tree in that domain is the best guess.

The analogy breaks because the PCFG does not condition on specific words or context — it only uses raw rule frequencies. A bigram model uses local word context; a PCFG does not. This is the main weakness, and we will address it.

Chart parsing (and basic CFG parsing) can produce multiple valid parse trees for an ambiguous sentence. Without additional information, there is no way to choose the best one. Probabilistic CFG solves this by associating a probability with each grammar rule. The best parse tree is the one with the highest overall probability.

The intuition: probability values come from the training corpus of a specific domain or application. The parse tree that is more probable in that domain is the more correct interpretation for that domain.

9.6.2 Computing Rule Probabilities

A PCFG augments each CFG rule \(A \rightarrow \beta\) with a conditional probability \(P(A \rightarrow \beta)\), which is the probability that non-terminal \(A\) expands to the sequence \(\beta\), given that we are expanding \(A\):

\[ P(A \rightarrow \beta) = P(\beta \mid A) \]

Probabilities are computed offline from a labeled training corpus — a treebank. The most famous is the Penn Treebank, containing about 50,000 manually labeled sentences from the Wall Street Journal.

From the treebank, for each rule \(A \rightarrow \beta\):

\[ P(A \rightarrow \beta) = \frac{\text{count}(A \rightarrow \beta)}{\sum_\gamma \text{count}(A \rightarrow \gamma)} \]

That is: the number of times this specific rule appears, divided by the total number of times any rule with the same left-hand side appears. This is maximum likelihood estimation (MLE).

Constraint: For all rules sharing the same left-hand-side symbol, the probabilities must sum to 1:

\[ \sum_\beta P(A \rightarrow \beta) = 1 \]

This is the same normalization constraint as any probability distribution: all possible outcomes for a given event must sum to 1.

Example:
If a training corpus has 300 sentences, all following S → NP VP, then \(P(S \rightarrow NP \; VP) = 1.0\).
If among those, NP → Det N appears 116 times out of 300 NP expansions, then \(P(NP \rightarrow Det \; N) = 116/300 \approx 0.387\).

POS tag probabilities work the same way. If the word flies appears 44 times — 21 as a noun, 19 as a verb, 4 as something else — then:

  • \(P(\text{flies} \mid N) = 21/44 \approx 0.477\)
  • \(P(\text{flies} \mid V) = 19/44 \approx 0.432\)

9.6.3 Probability of a Parse Tree

Given a parse tree \(T\) composed of rules \(r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n\), and assuming independence between rules (each rule application does not depend on the others), the joint probability of the tree and the sentence is:

\[ P(T, S) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(r_i) \]

This is the product of all individual rule probabilities used in the tree. The independence assumption means \(P(r_i \mid r_1, \ldots, r_{i-1}) = P(r_i)\) — each rule choice is treated as independent of all others. This is the PCFG's key simplifying assumption (and its main weakness).

Worked Example: Computing P(T) for "The flight includes a meal."

Given these rules with probabilities (all in CNF):

RuleProbability
S → NP VP1.0
NP → Det N0.3
VP → V NP0.8
Det → the0.4
N → flight0.02
V → includes0.05

For Parse Tree T₁ covering "The flight includes a meal":

\[ \begin{aligned} P(T_1) &= P(S \rightarrow NP \; VP) \times P(NP \rightarrow Det \; N)_{[0,2]} \times P(VP \rightarrow V \; NP)_{[2,5]} \\ &\quad \times P(Det \rightarrow \textit{the}) \times P(N \rightarrow \textit{flight}) \times P(V \rightarrow \textit{includes}) \\ &\quad \times P(NP \rightarrow Det \; N)_{[3,5]} \times P(Det \rightarrow \textit{a}) \times P(N \rightarrow \textit{meal}) &= 1.0 \times 0.3 \times 0.8 \times 0.4 \times 0.02 \times 0.05 \times 0.3 \times 0.3 \times 0.01 &= 1.0 \times 0.3 \times 0.8 \times 0.4 \times 0.02 \times 0.05 \times 0.3 \times 0.3 \times 0.01 \end{aligned} \]

Let us multiply step by step:

  • \(1.0 \times 0.3 = 0.3\)
  • \(0.3 \times 0.8 = 0.24\)
  • \(0.24 \times 0.4 = 0.096\)
  • \(0.096 \times 0.02 = 0.00192\)
  • \(0.00192 \times 0.05 = 0.000096\)
  • \(0.000096 \times 0.3 = 0.0000288\)
  • \(0.0000288 \times 0.3 = 0.00000864\)
  • \(0.00000864 \times 0.01 = 0.0000000864\)
\[ P(T_1) = 8.64 \times 10^{-8} \]

Sense-check: The probability is tiny because we are multiplying many numbers less than 1. In practice, log probabilities are used to avoid underflow: \(\log P(T) = \sum_i \log P(r_i)\). Relative comparison still works — if T₂ has \(P = 1.2 \times 10^{-9}\), then T₁ is about 72× more likely.

9.6.4 Choosing the Best Parse Tree

Given multiple possible parse trees \(T_1, T_2, \ldots, T_k\) for sentence \(S\):

\[ \hat{T} = \arg\max_{T} P(T, S) \]

The tree with the maximum probability is selected. This is the same argmax concept used in softmax classification in neural networks — pick the highest-scoring candidate.

9.6.5 Probability of a Sentence

The probability of a sentence is the sum of the probabilities of all its possible parse trees:

\[ P(S) = \sum_{T} P(T, S) \]

This is less commonly needed for parsing itself, but it is useful for applications like speech recognition (to judge whether a recognized sequence of words forms a plausible sentence). A very low sentence probability (below a threshold) can signal that the input is invalid and should be ignored.

For language modeling, \(P(S)\) tells you how "grammatical" a sentence is according to the PCFG. However, because PCFGs lack lexical conditioning, \(P(S)\) from a PCFG is generally a weaker language model than an n-gram model for speech recognition.

Assumptions & Scope. PCFG makes three strong independence assumptions (from Manning & Schütze, 1999):

  1. Place invariance: The probability of a subtree does not depend on where in the string the words it dominates are located. \(P(NP \rightarrow Det \; N)\) is the same whether the NP is at position [0,2] or [5,7].
  2. Context-free: The probability of a subtree does not depend on words not dominated by the subtree. The choice of NP expansion does not depend on the verb in the VP.
  3. Ancestor-free: The probability of a subtree does not depend on nodes outside the subtree in the derivation. The choice inside NP does not depend on whether NP is a subject or object.

All three are violated in real language. For instance, in English, subject NPs are more likely to be pronouns (NP → Pronoun) than object NPs. A PCFG cannot capture this because it treats all NP expansions identically regardless of grammatical role. These limitations motivate lexicalized PCFGs and dependency parsing.

Pitfalls.

  1. Forgetting the summation constraint. For every non-terminal A, the probabilities of all its rules MUST sum to 1. If your exam problem gives rules for NP that sum to 0.9, something is missing or probabilities need to be re-normalized.
  2. Multiplying when you should add. P(T) is a PRODUCT of rule probabilities. P(S) is a SUM of tree probabilities. Do not confuse the two. The probability of a specific tree is the product. The probability of the sentence is the sum over all trees.
  3. Using raw probabilities instead of logs. \(P(T)\) becomes extremely small (10⁻⁸ or smaller for even short sentences). On a computer, this underflows to zero. Always use \(\log P(T) = \sum \log P(r_i)\) in implementation. For exam hand calculations, raw probabilities are fine because examples are short.
  4. Thinking PCFG probabilities are "true" probabilities. They are estimates from a finite corpus. A rule that never appeared in the treebank gets zero probability — which means any parse using it gets zero probability, even if it is the correct parse. Smoothing techniques address this but are beyond this lecture.

9.6.6 Student Q&A on PCFG

Student Q&A (deduplicated).

Q: What is the advantage of PCFG over plain chart parsing?
A: In chart parsing without probabilities, if there are multiple valid parse trees, you cannot choose among them. PCFG gives each tree a probability score, so you can pick the best one — this is critical for ambiguous sentences. If the sentence has only one valid parse, PCFG is not needed for disambiguation (though it can still provide a confidence score).

Q: How is PCFG "context-free" compared to a bigram model? In bigrams, probability depends on the previous word — that is context. Here, the words seem less relevant; only the phrase-level rules matter.
A: Excellent observation. PCFG makes a strong independence assumption — every rule probability is treated as independent of every other rule. There is no conditioning on surrounding words or neighboring rules. A bigram model does capture local context (the previous word). In fact, this independence assumption is the main weakness of PCFG. Incorporating bigram/trigram context would improve it — and that is exactly what dependency parsing and neural parsers do. But PCFG is the simplest probabilistic formulation and the foundation.

Several students asked this — it is the most important conceptual limitation to understand.

Q: Where do the probability values come from? Are they part of a corpus?
A: They are pre-computed offline from a labeled training corpus — the Penn Treebank or similar. Along with the grammar rules, the probabilities are stored. During parsing (inference), you just look them up. You do not compute them on the fly.

Recap. PCFG adds probabilities to CFG rules, enabling disambiguation: choose the tree with maximum \(P(T) = \prod P(r_i)\). Probabilities come from treebanks via MLE. The price is a strong independence assumption — every rule choice is independent of context. Next: CKY parsing, which combines PCFG probabilities with dynamic programming in Chomsky Normal Form.

9.7 CKY (CYK) Parsing and Chomsky Normal Form

Hook. Chart parsing is powerful but messy — rules can have any number of symbols on the right-hand side, making the combination logic complex. What if we could constrain ALL rules to have exactly two non-terminals (or one terminal) on the right? Then combining constituents becomes trivial: pick two adjacent cells, check if a rule matches, fill the cell. This is CKY — the cleanest, most exam-friendly parsing algorithm.

9.7.1 Motivation for CNF

Intuition + Analogy. CKY is to chart parsing what a standardized shipping container is to cargo. Without containers, you have boxes of all shapes and sizes — loading a ship is chaotic. With standardized containers (CNF — every rule has exactly two non-terminals), loading is systematic: you always combine exactly two adjacent units. The algorithm becomes a simple table-filling exercise.

The CKY algorithm (Cocke-Younger-Kasami) is a bottom-up dynamic programming parser that requires all grammar rules to be in Chomsky Normal Form (CNF).

CKY parsing (also written CYK — named after Cocke, Younger, and Kasami, the researchers who developed it independently in the 1960s) is a bottom-up dynamic programming algorithm. It is the same chart parsing algorithm with one extra requirement: all grammar rules must be in Chomsky Normal Form (CNF).

Chomsky Normal Form (CNF). A CFG is in CNF if every production rule has one of exactly two forms:

  1. \(A \rightarrow B \; C\) — exactly two non-terminals on the RHS
  2. \(A \rightarrow a\) — exactly one terminal on the RHS

The following are NOT allowed in CNF:

  • A single non-terminal on the RHS (\(A \rightarrow B\)) — called a unit production
  • A mix of terminal and non-terminal on the RHS (\(A \rightarrow B \; c\))
  • More than two non-terminals on the RHS (\(A \rightarrow B \; C \; D\))
  • The empty string \(\epsilon\) on the RHS (except possibly \(S \rightarrow \epsilon\) in some formulations)

Why CNF? It simplifies and speeds up the parsing algorithm. With at most two non-terminals per rule, the parser only needs to check pairs of adjacent constituents, making the table-filling process straightforward. The time complexity drops to \(O(n^3 \cdot |R|)\) where \(n\) is the sentence length and \(|R|\) is the number of rules.

9.7.2 Converting Rules to CNF

Steps for CNF Conversion. Any CFG can be converted to an equivalent CNF grammar that generates the same language (minus the empty string).

Case 1 — Rule contains a terminal mixed with non-terminals:

Original: NP → the Adj N

Introduce a new non-terminal and a terminal rule:

  • Det → the
  • Then: NP → Det Adj N (now all RHS symbols are non-terminals)

Case 2 — More than two non-terminals on RHS:

Original: A → B C D

Introduce a new non-terminal X:

  • A → B X
  • X → C D (now each rule has exactly two non-terminals)

Case 3 — Single non-terminal on RHS (unit production):

Original: NP → Nominal

If Nominal → Noun | Nominal Noun | Nominal PP, then replace with:

  • NP → Noun | Nominal Noun | Nominal PP (copy the expansions, then handle any remaining binary/length issues)

Complexity & Cost. CKY runs in \(O(n^3 \cdot |R|)\) time and \(O(n^2)\) space for a sentence of length \(n\) and grammar with \(|R|\) rules.

  • The \(n^3\) comes from the triple loop: for each span width (O(n)), for each start position (O(n)), for each split point (O(n)) — hence \(O(n^3)\).
  • The \(|R|\) factor comes from checking which grammar rules match each pair of constituents.

For a 20-word sentence with 100 rules, CKY does about \(20^3 \times 100 = 800,000\) operations — easily handled. For a 100-word sentence, it is about \(100^3 \times 100 = 100\) million — still feasible. For very long sentences (200+ words), pruning or beam search is needed.

9.7.4 Student Q&A on CKY

Student Q&A (deduplicated).

Q: How do we get the value at row 3, column 5 (NP with probability 0.3)?
A: That NP comes from combining Det at [3,4] and N at [4,5] using the rule NP → Det N. These two adjacent constituents match the RHS of that rule — and since CNF requires exactly two non-terminals, the combination is valid. The order matters: Det must come first, N second. You cannot apply it if N comes before Det (that would be [4,5] + [3,4], which is not a valid span because start > end).

Q: Is it better to fill the diagonal (individual POS tags) first and then combine?
A: Yes — that is the recommended approach and what the algorithm specifies. Fill all diagonal cells [i, i+1] with the POS tags and their probabilities first. Then process widths 2, 3, ..., n in order. For each cell [i, j], try all split points k between i and j. For CNF, you only ever combine exactly two adjacent cells, which makes the process systematic and easy to grade on exams.

Recap. CKY is chart parsing with the CNF constraint — all rules have exactly two non-terminals (or one terminal) on the RHS. The algorithm fills an upper-triangular table in \(O(n^3)\) time by combining pairs of adjacent constituents. It is the cleanest exam-relevant parsing algorithm. Next: why PCFG — even with CKY — is still limited, and what comes after.

9.8 Problems with PCFG and Looking Ahead

Hook. You have built a PCFG parser. It ran CKY. It produced the most probable parse tree for "I saw the man with the telescope." And it picked the wrong one. In the Wall Street Journal, "with"-PPs attach to NPs 60% of the time. But in this specific sentence, with this specific verb (saw) and noun (telescope), the correct attachment is to the VP. Your PCFG does not know that. It only knows raw rule frequencies. This is the independence assumption — the reason PCFGs alone are not enough.

PCFG has a fundamental limitation: it makes a strong independence assumption. Every rule probability is independent of every other rule. There is no conditioning on context — no bigram or trigram information about which words or rules appear nearby.

Three specific failures of the PCFG independence assumption:

  1. No lexical conditioning. The rule \(NP \rightarrow Det \; N\) has the same probability whether the head noun is "flight" or "meal." But in reality, "flight" is more likely to be followed by a PP ("flight to Paris") than "meal." A PCFG cannot capture this.
  2. No structural context. The rule \(NP \rightarrow Pronoun\) has the same probability whether the NP is a subject or an object. But in English, subject NPs are more likely to be pronouns ("She ate") while object NPs are more likely to be full NPs ("ate the sandwich").
  3. No adjacency effects. The choice of VP expansion does not depend on the choice of NP expansion. But in reality, certain verb-subcategorization patterns are strongly correlated — "put" almost always takes an NP and a locative PP ("put the book on the table").

Assumptions & Scope. PCFG works reasonably well when:

  • The domain of the test sentences matches the domain of the training treebank (same genre, same register).
  • The grammar is large enough that most constructions are covered.
  • Disambiguation decisions are coarse (e.g., choosing between two very different structures).

PCFG fails when:

  • The test domain differs from training (WSJ → Twitter).
  • Fine-grained lexical preferences dominate the disambiguation (PP attachment, coordination scope).
  • The sentence contains rare or unseen constructions (zero probability for any parse using a rare rule).
  • The grammar needs to capture long-distance dependencies ("Which book did you say John thought Mary wrote?" — the gap between "which book" and "wrote" is not captured by CFG rules alone).

Pitfalls.

  1. Thinking CFGs/PCFGs are the final answer. They are the starting point. The field has moved through lexicalized PCFGs → dependency parsing → neural parsers. CFG/PCFG is the foundation that makes the advanced models understandable.
  2. Assuming independence when debugging. If a PCFG parser makes a mistake, ask: "would conditioning on a specific word or grammatical role change this probability?" The answer is usually yes — and that tells you which independence assumption was violated.
  3. Ignoring the treebank's domain. A PCFG trained on the Penn Treebank (Wall Street Journal, 1989) will not parse Shakespeare, Twitter, or medical text well. Domain mismatch is a common source of poor performance.

This is why dependency parsing — which captures pairwise relations between words with context — has largely replaced PCFG in practice. Dependency parsing directly models which word depends on which other word, capturing both structure and local context. Neural parsers go further, learning representations that encode rich contextual information.

9.9 Evaluating Parsers

Hook. You have spent weeks building a parser. It produces beautiful parse trees. But is it correct? "Looks right to me" is not a metric. You need a number — a score that tells you, and everyone else, exactly how good your parser is. In NLP, that number is F₁ — the harmonic mean of labeled precision and labeled recall.

9.9.1 Why Evaluation Matters

Intuition + Analogy. Evaluating a parser is like grading a student's math exam. You have an answer key (the gold-standard parse tree, created by human annotators). The student (your parser) produces an answer (a predicted parse tree). You compare them, point by point. Each correct constituent is a point earned. Each missed constituent or extra constituent is a point lost. Precision is "of the points you claimed, how many were right?" Recall is "of the points available, how many did you get?" F₁ balances both.

The analogy breaks because a parse tree is not a single answer — it is a set of constituents (phrase labels + spans). A parser can get some constituents right and others wrong. The evaluation must account for partial credit.

Evaluation is critical for every NLP application. Unless performance is good — accuracy is high, quality is acceptable — nobody will use the system. Parser evaluation tells you how well your parser captures the correct syntactic structure.

9.9.2 Labeled Precision and Labeled Recall

PARSEVAL Metrics. The standard evaluation framework, introduced by the PARSEVAL project (Black et al., 1991), uses labeled precision and labeled recall, comparing the parser's output against a gold-standard (human-annotated) parse tree.

A constituent is a tuple (label, start, end). For a constituent to count as correct, BOTH must match:

  • Label match: The non-terminal category (S, NP, VP, PP, etc.) must be identical.
  • Span match: The start and end positions must be identical. An NP spanning [0,2] is different from an NP spanning [0,3].

Symbol Registry — Parser Evaluation:

SymbolMeaningFormula
LPLabeled Precision\(\frac{\text{# correct constituents in parser output}}{\text{# total constituents in parser output}}\)
LRLabeled Recall\(\frac{\text{# correct constituents in parser output}}{\text{# total constituents in gold standard}}\)
F₁Harmonic mean of LP and LR\(2 \times \frac{LP \times LR}{LP + LR}\)
\[ LP = \frac{\text{Number of correctly labeled constituents}}{\text{Total number of constituents in parser's output}} \] \[ LR = \frac{\text{Number of correctly labeled constituents}}{\text{Total number of constituents in gold standard}} \] \[ F_1 = 2 \times \frac{LP \times LR}{LP + LR} \]

The harmonic mean (F₁) is used instead of the arithmetic mean because it penalizes extreme imbalance. A parser with LP = 1.0 and LR = 0.0 (finds no constituents but everything it says is right — vacuously) should score 0, not 0.5.

9.9.3 Worked Evaluation Example

Worked Example: Computing LP, LR, F₁.

Sentence: Sales executives were examining the figures with great care yesterday.

Gold Standard Parse (8 constituents):

#ConstituentSpan
1S[0, 11]
2NP[0, 2]
3VP[2, 9]
4VP[3, 9]
5NP[5, 9]
6NP[5, 7]
7PP[7, 9]
8NP[9, 10]

Parser Output (7 constituents):

#ConstituentSpanMatch?Reason
1S[0, 11]✓Label and span both match gold #1
2NP[0, 2]✓Label and span both match gold #2
3VP[2, 10]✗Span [2,10] ≠ gold [2,9] (includes yesterday)
4VP[3, 10]✗Span [3,10] ≠ gold [3,9]
5NP[5, 10]✗Span [5,10] ≠ gold [5,9] (includes yesterday)

Three constituents match exactly (label + span). The parser incorrectly attached yesterday — in the gold standard, it is a separate NP at [9,10]; the parser attached it inside the VP.

Calculations:

\[ LP = \frac{3}{7} = 0.429 = 42.9\% \] \[ LR = \frac{3}{8} = 0.375 = 37.5\% \] \[ F_1 = 2 \times \frac{0.429 \times 0.375}{0.429 + 0.375} = 2 \times \frac{0.1609}{0.804} = 2 \times 0.2001 = 0.400 = 40.0\% \]

Sense-check: 40% F₁ is poor by modern standards. State-of-the-art constituency parsers achieve F₁ > 90% on the Penn Treebank test set. Dependency parsers routinely exceed 95% unlabeled attachment score (UAS). This example was deliberately constructed to show a parser that makes a systematic attachment error with yesterday.

9.9.4 Key Insight

The positions of constituents matter as much as the rule labels. A parser that identifies the same rules but with different spans is making attachment errors — the same ambiguity problem that motivated parsing in the first place (e.g., attaching yesterday to the VP vs. leaving it as a separate NP).

Pitfalls.

  1. Counting only label matches. A constituent must match on BOTH label and span. NP[0,2] ≠ NP[0,3]. Two NPs with different spans are two different constituents.
  2. Double-counting. Each gold constituent can match at most one parser constituent. If your parser outputs two NPs spanning [0,2], only one can match the gold NP[0,2]. The other is a false positive.
  3. Forgetting the harmonic mean. Do not use the arithmetic mean \((LP + LR)/2\). The harmonic mean penalizes imbalance — a parser with LP = 0.1 and LR = 0.9 gets arithmetic mean 0.5 but harmonic mean F₁ = 0.18. F₁ is always ≤ the arithmetic mean.
  4. Ignoring punctuation and traces. In standard PARSEVAL, punctuation and null elements (traces, empty categories) are typically excluded from the constituent count. Check your evaluation setup — including or excluding them changes the score.

9.9.5 Evaluation in Practice

The same precision/recall/F₁ framework is used for dependency parsers and neural parsers. This evaluation method is universal for syntactic parsing, whether the parser is rule-based, statistical, or neural.

Real-World & Domain Connection. Parser evaluation drives progress in the field. The Penn Treebank, with its standardized train/dev/test split (sections 02-21 for training, 22 for development, 23 for testing), enabled fair comparison across decades of parsing research. The CoNLL shared tasks (Conference on Computational Natural Language Learning) extended this to dependency parsing across multiple languages, establishing the Universal Dependencies (UD) framework. Today, when you use spaCy or Stanza to parse text, the F₁ scores reported in their documentation come from this exact evaluation methodology — comparing predicted trees against gold-standard annotations. In industry, parser evaluation is less formal but equally important: if your chatbot's intent parser gets the syntactic structure wrong 10% of the time, 10% of user requests will be mishandled.

Recap. Parser evaluation uses labeled precision (how many of your constituents are correct), labeled recall (how many of the gold constituents you found), and F₁ (their harmonic mean). Both label AND span must match. This framework is universal across all syntactic parsing paradigms.

Structural Ambiguity and Catalan Numbers

Must-know: The number of parse trees for a sentence with \(n\) prepositional phrases follows Catalan numbers: \(C_n = \frac{1}{n+1} \binom{2n}{n}\). Each additional PP roughly doubles the parse count, causing exponential ambiguity that parsing must resolve.

\[ C_n = \frac{1}{n+1} \binom{2n}{n} \]

Top pitfall: Confusing structural ambiguity (attachment choices) with lexical ambiguity (word meaning). A sentence can have both simultaneously.

Self-check: For a sentence with 3 prepositional phrases, how many possible parse trees exist?

Connects to: Context-Free Grammar, Probabilistic CFG, Parser Evaluation

Context-Free Grammar (CFG)

Must-know: A CFG is a 4-tuple \((N, \Sigma, R, S)\): \(N\) = non-terminals (S, NP, VP), \(\Sigma\) = terminals (words), \(R\) = production rules, \(S\) = start symbol. Every valid parse tree MUST have S at the root.

\[ G = (N, \Sigma, R, S), \quad A \rightarrow \beta \text{ where } A \in N \]

Top pitfall: Forgetting the start symbol constraint: every valid parse MUST have S at the root. A tree rooted at VP is a phrase fragment.

Self-check: Name the four components of a CFG and explain what each one represents.

Connects to: Parse Trees, Chart Parsing, CKY Algorithm

Parse Trees and Phrase Structure

Must-know: A parse tree has S as root, non-terminals as internal nodes (NP, VP, PP), and terminals (words) as leaves. Each phrase has a head word that determines its category. The head of NP is a noun, VP is a verb.

\[ S \rightarrow NP \; VP \]

Top pitfall: Mixing up phrase types. The head determines the category. "Very intelligent" is an AP (adjective phrase), not an AdvP, even though it starts with an adverb.

Self-check: In "The small boy kicked the red ball," identify the head of each phrase.

Connects to: Context-Free Grammar, Chart Parsing, Ambiguity

Chart Parsing (Dynamic Programming)

Must-know: Chart parsing avoids exponential backtracking by storing every constituent exactly once. Key components: chart (storage), key (current item), active arc (rule in progress with dot), agenda (to-do queue). Complexity: \(O(n^3)\).

\[ \text{NP} \rightarrow \text{Art} \cdot \text{Adj N} \quad \text{(dot notation: Art found, need Adj then N)} \]

Top pitfall: Forgetting to add completed constituents to the agenda. When the dot reaches the end, the constituent MUST go into the agenda to trigger further rule activations.

Self-check: In chart parsing, what does the dot in "VP \(\rightarrow\) V \(\cdot\) NP" mean?

Connects to: CKY Algorithm, PCFG, Top-Down and Bottom-Up Parsing

Probabilistic CFG (PCFG)

Must-know: PCFG augments each CFG rule with a probability: \(P(A \rightarrow \beta) = \text{count}(A \rightarrow \beta) / \sum_\gamma \text{count}(A \rightarrow \gamma)\). Tree probability \(P(T) = \prod P(r_i)\). Best parse: \(\hat{T} = \arg\max_T P(T, S)\). Probabilities come from treebanks (Penn Treebank) via MLE.

\[ P(T, S) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(r_i), \quad \hat{T} = \arg\max_{T} P(T, S) \]

Top pitfall: Multiplying when you should add. \(P(T)\) is a PRODUCT of rule probabilities. \(P(S)\) is a SUM of tree probabilities. Do not confuse the two.

Self-check: Given \(P(T_1) = 3.2 \times 10^{-8}\) and \(P(T_2) = 1.7 \times 10^{-9}\), which tree wins?

Connects to: CKY Algorithm, Chart Parsing, PCFG Limitations

CKY Parsing and Chomsky Normal Form

Must-know: CKY requires all rules in CNF: \(A \rightarrow B \; C\) (two non-terminals) OR \(A \rightarrow a\) (one terminal). Fill upper-triangular table: \(p_A = p_{\text{rule}} \times p_B \times p_C\). Complexity: \(O(n^3 \cdot |R|)\). Fill diagonal first, then increasing widths.

\[ p_A = p_{\text{rule}} \times p_B \times p_C \quad \text{(CKY cell computation)} \]

Top pitfall: Forgetting to multiply rule probability. Cell value = \(p_{\text{rule}} \times p_{\text{left}} \times p_{\text{right}}\), not just \(p_{\text{rule}}\). For span \([i,j]\), \(k\) must satisfy \(i \lt k \lt j\).

Self-check: For a 5-word sentence parsed with CKY, how many cells are in the upper-triangular table?

Connects to: Chart Parsing, PCFG, CNF Conversion

Limitations of PCFG

Must-know: PCFG makes three independence assumptions: place invariance, context-free, and ancestor-free. All three are violated in real language. This motivates dependency parsing and neural parsers which condition on words and context.

\[ P(NP \rightarrow Det \; N) \text{ is identical at all positions — place invariance assumption} \]

Top pitfall: Thinking PCFGs are the final answer. They are the foundation. The field has moved to dependency parsing and neural parsers. PCFG is exam-critical but not state-of-the-art.

Self-check: Why should "NP \(\rightarrow\) Pronoun" have different probabilities for subject vs. object positions?

Connects to: PCFG, CKY Algorithm, Parser Evaluation

Parser Evaluation — LP, LR, F₁

Must-know: Labeled Precision (LP) = correct / parser total. Labeled Recall (LR) = correct / gold total. F₁ = \(2 \times LP \times LR / (LP + LR)\). A constituent is correct ONLY if both label AND span match. Always use the harmonic mean.

\[ F_1 = 2 \times \frac{LP \times LR}{LP + LR}, \quad LP = \frac{\text{#correct}}{\text{#parser}}, \quad LR = \frac{\text{#correct}}{\text{#gold}} \]

Top pitfall: Using arithmetic mean instead of harmonic. LP = 0.1, LR = 0.9 gives arithmetic mean 0.5 but F₁ = 0.18. Also: counting label matches without checking span — both must match.

Self-check: Parser outputs 10 constituents, 7 match gold (label + span). Gold has 14. Compute LP, LR, F₁.

Connects to: Ambiguity, PCFG, Chart Parsing
