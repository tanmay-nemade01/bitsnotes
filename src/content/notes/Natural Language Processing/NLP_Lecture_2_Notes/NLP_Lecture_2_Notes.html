Vector Semantics and Word Embeddings

2.1 Foundations of Lexical Semantics and Vector Representations

Hook: How can a digital computer—which processes only binary zeros and ones—understand that "cat" and "feline" are nearly identical in meaning, while "cat" and "automobile" are fundamentally distinct? Traditional string comparisons fail completely because they treat language as arbitrary sequences of characters rather than geometric structures of meaning.

2.1.1 Symbol Registry

Symbol Registry & Variable Definitions:

  • \(V\) — The complete vocabulary lexicon of unique word tokens — Set of strings
  • \(|V|\) — Vocabulary size (total number of unique words in lexicon) — Positive integer \(\mathbb{N}^+\)
  • \(E\) — Word embedding mapping function \(E: V \to \mathbb{R}^N\) — Vector function
  • \(\mathbf{e}_i\) — One-hot vector representation of the \(i\)-th word token — Vector in \(\mathbb{R}^{|V|}\)
  • \(\mathbb{R}^N\) — Continuous \(N\)-dimensional vector space — Vector space
  • \(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}\) — Dense or sparse feature vectors — Vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^N\)

2.1.2 Lexical Semantics and Computational Challenges

Lexical Semantics & Word Senses:

Lexical semantics is the subfield of computational linguistics concerned with the study of word meanings and the systematic semantic relations among words in a language.

  • Lexicon: The complete inventory of words (vocabulary) in a language or domain, denoted as \(V\).
  • Word Sense: A specific, distinct conceptual meaning expressed by a word token. For instance, the word bank possesses distinct word senses: a financial institution (Sense 1) versus the sloping land along a river (Sense 2).
  • Polysemy: The phenomenon where a single word form carries multiple distinct but related or unrelated senses.
  • Synonymy: The relation between distinct word forms that express nearly identical underlying senses (e.g., big and large).

To process natural language effectively, computational systems must overcome three fundamental structural challenges inherent in naive text representations:

  1. Character-String Limitations & Orthogonal Isolation:

Treating words as arbitrary character strings (such as "dog" vs. "cat") or assigning arbitrary integer identifiers (e.g., \(\text{dog} \to 1\), \(\text{cat} \to 2\), \(\text{automobile} \to 3\)) creates an orthogonal representation space. In a standard one-hot encoding over a vocabulary \(V\), every word vector \(\mathbf{e}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{|V|}\) is a sparse vector with a single \(1\) at index \(i\) and \(0\)s elsewhere. The dot product between any two distinct one-hot word vectors is exactly zero:

\[ \mathbf{e}_{\text{dog}} \cdot \mathbf{e}_{\text{cat}} = 0, \quad \mathbf{e}_{\text{dog}} \cdot \mathbf{e}_{\text{automobile}} = 0 \]

This mathematical formulation implies that "dog" is equally as unrelated to "cat" as it is to "automobile," failing to capture any intrinsic semantic overlap or shared conceptual properties.

  1. Exact String Matching Failures:

Information retrieval or question-answering systems relying solely on exact keyword matching fail when queries use alternative phrasing. Searching a database for documents containing the query term "terrible" will omit documents containing "bad," "awful," or "dreadful," even when those documents address the exact query intent.

  1. Symbolic Dictionary & Taxonomy Constraints:

Hand-crafted lexical databases (such as WordNet) attempt to solve string matching by enumerating formal synsets (synonym sets) and hypernym/hyponym hierarchies. However, symbolic taxonomies suffer from three critical bottlenecks:

  • Coverage Gaps: Language evolves rapidly, leaving curated dictionaries lacking slang, modern technical jargon, and domain-specific terminology.
  • Lack of Fine-Grained Quantitative Metrics: A thesaurus provides binary relationships (words are either listed as synonyms or not), but cannot quantify how similar two terms are on a continuous scale (e.g., scoring similarity between "car" and "truck" as \(0.85\) vs. "car" and "bicycle" as \(0.42\)).
  • Failure to Capture Contextual Nuances: Symbolic resources struggle with pragmatic shifts; for example, while "H2O" and "water" share identical chemical denotations, using "H2O" in a casual conversation ("May I have a glass of H2O?") alters the social register and stylistic context.

Modern Natural Language Processing resolves these challenges by projecting words into a continuous vector space where geometric distance directly models semantic similarity.

2.1.3 The Distributional Hypothesis and Semantic Space

Intuition & Everyday Analogy — The Distributional Hypothesis:

The foundational paradigm powering vector semantics is the Distributional Hypothesis, articulated by linguist John Rupert Firth (1957): "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." Words that occur in similar context environments across large bodies of text tend to share similar semantic meanings.

The "On Choy" Analogy:

Imagine reading an article containing a word you have never encountered before: on choy.

  • "On choy is delicious when sautéed with fresh garlic."
  • "The green leaves of on choy taste wonderful when served over steamed rice."
  • "On choy is a fresh vegetable that can be prepared much like spinach or kale."

Even if you have no prior knowledge of Cantonese cuisine, your brain effortlessly synthesizes the context clues: "delicious," "sautéed," "garlic," "green leaves," "rice," "vegetable," and "spinach." From these surrounding co-occurring words, you immediately infer that on choy refers to an edible, leafy green vegetable. The surrounding words filter out non-food concepts (like electronics or sports) and position on choy right next to spinach and kale in your mental conceptual space.

Semantic Space (Semantic Field):

A semantic space is a geometric model where words are mapped to points or vectors in an \(N\)-dimensional space. Words belonging to the same semantic domain (such as medical terms: surgeon, nurse, anesthetic, hospital) form dense clusters with small spatial distances between them. Words from disparate domains (such as waiter, laptop, or mountain) lie far apart in the vector space.

Visual Intuition of Semantic Space:

Imagine plotting words on a two-dimensional map where the x-axis represents "degree of technical/medical context" and the y-axis represents "degree of culinary context":

  • Cluster A (Culinary): spinach, kale, garlic, and on choy cluster closely in the high-culinary, low-medical quadrant.
  • Cluster B (Medical): doctor, surgeon, hospital, and anesthetic cluster in the high-medical, low-culinary quadrant.
  • Landmark Observation: Distance in vector space directly mirrors conceptual dissimilarity. Moving from spinach to on choy requires traversing a tiny spatial distance, whereas moving from spinach to surgeon requires crossing a large vector distance.

Assumptions & Scope:

  • Corpus Dependence: The Distributional Hypothesis assumes that the underlying text corpus is sufficiently large, representative, and unbiased. If a corpus only mentions "apple" in the context of computer hardware, its vector representation will fail to capture its primary fruit sense.
  • Co-occurrence Scope: Distributional methods capture associative and topical relationships (e.g., "doctor" and "hospital") as well as paradigmatic relationships (e.g., "cat" and "dog"), but may occasionally assign high similarity to antonyms (e.g., "hot" and "cold") because they share identical surrounding contexts ("The weather today is exceptionally ___").

2.1.4 Word Embeddings and Representation Paradigms

Word Embedding:

A word embedding is a mapping function \(E: V \to \mathbb{R}^N\) that projects words from a discrete vocabulary \(V\) into an \(N\)-dimensional continuous or discrete vector space.

Vector representations fall into two primary paradigm classes:

Feature / Dimension Sparse Vector Paradigm (Frequency-Based) Dense Vector Paradigm (Prediction/Learning-Based)
:--- :--- :---
Dimensionality (\(N\)) Extremely high (\(N = \|V\|\) or \(N = \|D_c\|\), typically \(10,000\) to \(1,000,000+\)) Low to moderate (\(N \in [50, 1024]\), e.g., 300 for GloVe, 768 for BERT)
Value Type Non-negative counts or statistical weights (\(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\)) Continuous real-valued floating point numbers (\(\mathbb{R}\))
Sparsity Highly sparse (99%+ of entries are zero) Fully dense (almost all entries are non-zero)
Interpretability High: Each dimension corresponds directly to a specific word or document ID Low (Latent): Dimensions represent abstract, unaligned latent semantic features
Core Algorithms Term-Document Matrix, Co-occurrence Matrix, TF-IDF, BM25 Skip-gram, CBOW (Word2Vec), GloVe, FastText, BERT, Transformer Embeddings

Sparse and dense word embeddings serve as the foundational input layer (the "ABCD" building blocks) for all modern NLP pipelines. They feed continuous numerical representations into search indexing engines, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) vector stores, Large Language Models (LLMs), and autonomous agent systems.

Common Pitfalls:

  1. Confusing String Distance with Semantic Distance: Beginner practitioners often confuse orthographic edit distance (e.g., Levenshtein distance between "bad" and "bed", which is 1 character) with semantic vector similarity. "Bad" and "awful" have zero orthographic similarity but high semantic vector similarity.
  2. Static Vector Polysemy Trap: In traditional static vector models (TF-IDF, Word2Vec), a word has a single static vector regardless of context. Consequently, the vector for "bank" is forced to average the financial sense and riverbank sense together. Resolving polysemy requires contextual embeddings (like BERT or Transformers) that dynamically generate vectors based on surrounding text.

2.1.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: How do vector representations detect correct sentence meaning when sentences share common words with different meanings, such as polysemy?

A: Sparse frequency vectors and traditional static word embeddings assign a single fixed vector per unique vocabulary token, which averages its multiple senses across the training corpus. Modern neural NLP overcomes this limitation through contextualized word embeddings (such as BERT or Transformer encoders). Contextual models process the entire surrounding sequence, dynamically altering the hidden state vector of a polysemous word (like "bank") based on whether neighboring tokens reference "river, water, mud" versus "money, interest, deposit."

Q: Do software engineers or computational linguists manually assign numeric vector values to each word in the vocabulary?

A: No. Vector creation is entirely automated. Algorithms process large text corpora and compute feature representations automatically. Sparse methods calculate vector entries using deterministic counting and statistical formulas (such as co-occurrence counts and TF-IDF equations). Dense methods learn continuous vector weights automatically via neural network optimization tasks (e.g., predicting context words given a target word).

Section Recap & Bridge:

Representing words as numeric vectors in a geometric semantic space overcomes the catastrophic failures of string matching and symbolic dictionaries. By exploiting the Distributional Hypothesis, words with similar surrounding contexts are placed close together in vector space.

Bridge to Next Section: Next, in Section 2.2, we formalize the first major sparse vector representations: Term-Document Frequency Matrices and Word-Word Co-occurrence Matrices.

Real-World & Domain Connection:

Vector representations constitute the core retrieval layer of modern Information Retrieval (IR) and Enterprise Search systems. Vector databases such as Milvus, Pinecone, and Qdrant store high-dimensional word and passage embeddings to perform approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) search. In Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) architectures, domain documents are embedded into semantic vector spaces, enabling AI agents to search and retrieve contextually relevant knowledge chunks within milliseconds to answer user queries.

2.2 Term Frequency and Co-occurrence Matrices

Hook: How can we take an entire library of unstructured texts—such as the complete works of William Shakespeare—and organize it into a structured numerical grid where a computer can instantly determine which plays are comedies and which words share thematic roots?

2.2.1 Symbol Registry

Symbol Registry & Variable Definitions:

  • \(D_j\) — The \(j\)-th document in a corpus collection \(D = \{D_1, D_2, \dots, D_M\}\) — Document object
  • \(w_i\) — The \(i\)-th unique term/word in the vocabulary \(V = \{w_1, w_2, \dots, w_N\}\) — Token string
  • \(f_{i,j}\) — Raw term frequency count of word \(w_i\) inside document \(D_j\) — Non-negative integer \(\mathbb{N}_{\ge 0}\)
  • \(k\) — Symmetric context window radius — Positive integer \(\mathbb{N}^+\)
  • \(\mathbf{d}_j\) — Column vector representation of document \(D_j\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{|V|}\) — Column vector
  • \(\mathbf{w}_i\) — Row vector representation of term \(w_i\) across documents in \(\mathbb{R}^{|D|}\) — Row vector

2.2.2 Term-Document Frequency Matrices

Term-Document Frequency Matrix:

A term-document matrix is a 2D mathematical array of size \(|V| \times |D|\), where rows correspond to unique vocabulary terms \(w_i \in V\) and columns correspond to individual documents \(D_j \in D\). Each cell entry \(M_{i,j} = f_{i,j}\) represents the raw frequency count of word \(w_i\) appearing within document \(D_j\).

Intuition & Grid Analogy:

Imagine a massive accounting ledger. Every column header represents a specific book or file folder (document), and every row label represents a word from the dictionary. By placing a tally mark in a cell whenever a word appears in a folder, we transform raw unstructured text into a spatial grid of numerical coordinates.

Worked Example 2.2.1 — Term-Document Frequency Matrix for Shakespeare Plays:

Consider a sample corpus consisting of four plays by William Shakespeare:

  • \(D_1\): As You Like It (Comedy)
  • \(D_2\): Twelfth Night (Comedy)
  • \(D_3\): Julius Caesar (Tragedy/History)
  • \(D_4\): Henry V (History)

We track the raw occurrences of four representative terms (battle, good, fool, wit):

Vocabulary Term (\(w_i\)) As You Like It (\(D_1\)) Twelfth Night (\(D_2\)) Julius Caesar (\(D_3\)) Henry V (\(D_4\))
:--- :--- :--- :--- :---
battle 1 0 7 13
good 114 80 62 89
fool 36 58 1 4
wit 20 15 2 3

Dual Vector Interpretation:

  1. Document Representations (Column Vectors \(\mathbf{d}_j \in \mathbb{R}^4\)):

Each column forms a 4-dimensional feature vector representing a document's overall topic profile:

\[ \mathbf{d}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 114 \\ 36 \\ 20 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{d}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 80 \\ 58 \\ 15 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{d}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 62 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{d}_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 13 \\ 89 \\ 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \]
  • Observation: The comedies As You Like It (\(\mathbf{d}_1\)) and Twelfth Night (\(\mathbf{d}_2\)) feature high frequencies for fool (\(36, 58\)) and wit (\(20, 15\)) but minimal counts for battle (\(1, 0\)). Conversely, Julius Caesar (\(\mathbf{d}_3\)) and Henry V (\(\mathbf{d}_4\)) exhibit high counts for battle (\(7, 13\)) and very low counts for fool (\(1, 4\)).
  • Result: Plotted in 4D space, \(\mathbf{d}_1\) and \(\mathbf{d}_2\) point in nearly the same spatial direction, forming a tight "Comedy Cluster."
  1. Word Representations (Row Vectors \(\mathbf{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^4\)):

Each row forms a feature vector representing a word's distribution across the corpus:

\[ \mathbf{w}_{\text{fool}} = \begin{bmatrix} 36 & 58 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{w}_{\text{wit}} = \begin{bmatrix} 20 & 15 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]
  • Observation: The row vectors for fool and wit display a strong parallel distribution—both have large values in \(D_1\) and \(D_2\), and tiny values in \(D_3\) and \(D_4\).
  • Sense-Check: Because fool and wit share nearly identical row vector patterns across documents, a computer infers that fool and wit are semantically related in Shakespearean language.

2.2.3 Word-Word Co-occurrence Matrices and Context Windows

While term-document matrices capture broad document-level topics, they do not capture fine-grained word meanings. To model word semantics directly, we construct a word-word co-occurrence matrix (also called a term-context matrix).

Word-Word Co-occurrence Matrix & Context Window:

A word-word co-occurrence matrix is a square matrix of size \(|V| \times |V|\), where both rows and columns represent words in the vocabulary. Cell entry \(M_{i,c}\) records how many times target word \(w_i\) appears within a local context window of radius \(\pm k\) around context word \(w_c\) across the corpus.

  • Context Window Radius (\(k\)): A sliding window parameter specifying the number of tokens to the left (\(-k\)) and right (\(+k\)) of a target word.
  • Window Size Rationale: Standard English sentences average 8 to 10 words in length. A symmetric window of radius \(k = 4\) or \(k = 5\) spans an 8 to 10 word window, perfectly encapsulating local syntactic and semantic relationships within a single sentence while excluding unrelated text from surrounding sentences.

Procedural Spine — Constructing a Word-Word Co-occurrence Matrix:

  1. Purpose: Map fine-grained word semantics by measuring local context associations.
  2. Inputs: Raw corpus text, Vocabulary \(V\), Window radius \(k\) (e.g., \(k=4\)).
  3. Execution Steps:
  • Step A: Initialize a zero-filled square matrix \(M\) of shape \(|V| \times |V|\).
  • Step B: Iterate sequentially through every token position \(p\) in the corpus, setting the token at \(p\) as the target word \(w_{\text{target}}\).
  • Step C: Extract surrounding context tokens within range \([p - k, p + k]\), excluding position \(p\) itself.
  • Step D: For every context token \(w_{\text{context}}\) in the window, increment cell \(M[w_{\text{target}}, w_{\text{context}}] += 1\).
  • Step E: If a context word appears on both the left and right sides of the target word within the same window, count each occurrence separately as a distinct co-occurrence event.
  1. Output: A symmetric (or asymmetric) co-occurrence count matrix \(M \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{|V| \times |V|}\).

Worked Example 2.2.2 — Word-Word Co-occurrence Matrix:

Consider four target words (cherry, strawberry, digital, information) and four context words (computer, data, pie, sugar), extracted using a context window of radius \(k = \pm 4\) across a large text corpus:

Target Word (\(w_i\)) computer data pie sugar
:--- :--- :--- :--- :---
cherry 2 8 442 25
strawberry 0 1 310 180
digital 1683 1670 5 0
information 3982 3325 8 5

Semantic Interpretation:

  • cherry and strawberry produce large co-occurrence counts with pie (\(442, 310\)) and sugar (\(25, 180\)), but near-zero counts with computer and data.
  • digital and information produce massive co-occurrence counts with computer (\(1683, 3982\)) and data (\(1670, 3325\)), but near-zero counts with pie and sugar.
  • Sense-Check: The row vectors map cherry and strawberry into a culinary/fruit cluster, while mapping digital and information into a technology/data cluster.

Assumptions & Scope:

  • Bag-of-Words within Window: The sliding window model ignores the exact relative order or syntactic parse structure of words within the window \(\pm k\); it treats all tokens inside the radius as equal context.
  • Memory & Sparsity Costs: For a vocabulary of size \(|V| = 100,000\), a dense co-occurrence matrix requires \(100,000 \times 100,000 = 10^{10}\) cells (over 40 GB of RAM if stored naively). Consequently, production systems implement sparse Compressed Sparse Row (CSR) matrix formats.

Common Pitfalls:

  1. Confusing Document vs. Local Window Matrices: A term-document matrix counts co-occurrence across entire files (capturing broad topic/genre), whereas a word-word matrix counts co-occurrence within local sentence windows (capturing word meaning and syntactic role).
  2. Raw Frequency Bias: Unnormalized co-occurrence counts are heavily biased by overall word frequency. Ubiquitous stop words (e.g., "the", "and") appear thousands of times in every context window, distorting row vector similarity unless weighting techniques (like TF-IDF or PPMI) are applied.

2.2.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: How do occurrences in Shakespeare plays show the semantic relationship between terms like wit and fool?

A: Term frequency occurrences across documents form row vector distributions. In the term-document matrix, both wit and fool exhibit high frequency counts in comedy plays (As You Like It, Twelfth Night) and low counts in historical/tragedy plays (Julius Caesar, Henry V). Because their row vectors share matching distribution patterns across all documents, their spatial angle in vector space is small, yielding a high cosine similarity score.

Q: How is the context window size chosen, and are documents chunked into fixed non-overlapping windows?

A: Documents are not chunked into fixed non-overlapping blocks. Instead, a continuous sliding window sweeps token-by-token across the text. The window radius \(k\) is selected based on linguistic properties: because average English sentences contain 8 to 10 words, a symmetric radius of \(k = \pm 4\) or \(\pm 5\) tokens around the target word ensures that the window captures within-sentence context without crossing into unrelated adjacent sentences.

Q: If a context word appears on both the left and right sides of the target word within the same window, how is it counted?

A: Each context word appearance within the window is counted as an independent co-occurrence event. If a target word "pie" is surrounded by "sweet cherry pie cherry cake" where "cherry" appears both 1 token to the left and 1 token to the right, the co-occurrence count \(M[\text{pie}, \text{cherry}]\) is incremented by \(2\).

Section Recap & Bridge:

Term-document matrices model document topics via column vectors, while word-word co-occurrence matrices model word meanings via local context row vectors.

Bridge to Next Section: Raw frequency vectors provide coordinates in space, but to compare them quantitatively, we require a formal vector similarity metric. In Section 2.3, we formulate Dot Product, Euclidean Norm, and Cosine Similarity.

Real-World & Domain Connection:

Co-occurrence matrices form the foundational input data for modern topic modeling (such as Latent Semantic Analysis / LSA via Singular Value Decomposition) and GloVe (Global Vectors for Word Representation). In domain-specific search engines (such as medical PubMed or legal research tools), co-occurrence statistics are used to automatically generate domain thesauri, expand query terms, and build entity interaction graphs.

2.3 Mathematical Formulation of Vector Similarity

Hook: If two documents or words are represented by numerical vectors in a multi-dimensional space, how do we measure the exact "degree of similarity" between their meanings? Why can we not simply sum up matching feature counts or calculate Euclidean distance?

2.3.1 Symbol Registry

Symbol Registry & Variable Definitions:

  • \(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}\) — Feature vectors representing words or documents — Vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^N\)
  • \(v_i, w_i\) — The \(i\)-th scalar component/count of vector \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\) — Real scalar \(\mathbb{R}\)
  • \(N\) — Vector dimensionality (number of features, context words, or documents) — Positive integer \(\mathbb{N}^+\)
  • \(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}\) — Dot product (inner product) of vectors \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\) — Real scalar \(\mathbb{R}\)
  • \(\|\mathbf{v}\|_2\) — Euclidean norm (\(L_2\)-norm, length, or magnitude) of vector \(\mathbf{v}\) — Non-negative real scalar \(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\)
  • \(\theta\) — Spatial angle between vectors \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\) — Angle in radians \([0, \pi]\)
  • \(\text{CosineSimilarity}(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w})\) — Cosine of the angle \(\theta\) between vectors — Scalar in \([-1, 1]\)

2.3.2 Dot Product and Vector Magnitude

Dot Product (Inner Product):

The dot product between two \(N\)-dimensional vectors \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\) is the sum of the element-wise products of their corresponding components:

\[ \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} v_i w_i = v_1 w_1 + v_2 w_2 + \dots + v_N w_N \]

Intuition & The Length Flaw of Dot Product:

The dot product measures the total overlap of non-zero features between two vectors. However, using raw dot product as a similarity metric presents a severe mathematical flaw: it is unnormalized and scales directly with vector magnitude.

The Long Document Trap:

Suppose Document A is a 100-word blog post about computer science, and Document B is a 50,000-word textbook about organic chemistry that happens to mention the word "computer" 10 times in an appendix. If we compute the dot product between a user query vector \(\mathbf{q} = [\text{computer}]\) and both documents, Document B will produce a far higher dot product simply because Document B contains more total words. Raw dot product confuses document length with topical relevance.

To eliminate the artifact of vector length, we must normalize vectors using their Euclidean Norm (\(L_2\)-norm).

Euclidean Norm (\(L_2\)-Magnitude):

The Euclidean norm \(\|\mathbf{v}\|_2\) measures the geometric length of a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) from the origin in \(N\)-dimensional space, calculated as the square root of the sum of squared component values:

\[ \|\mathbf{v}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_i^2} = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \dots + v_N^2} \]

2.3.3 Cosine Similarity

Cosine Similarity Formulation:

Cosine similarity divides the dot product of two vectors by the product of their individual Euclidean magnitudes. Mathematically, this equals the cosine of the spatial angle \(\theta\) between the two vectors:

\[ \text{CosineSimilarity}(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}) = \cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_2 \|\mathbf{w}\|_2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_i w_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i^2}} \]

Intuition & The Sunlight/Shadow Analogy:

Imagine two arrows starting from the origin on a sheet of graph paper. The dot product measures how long a shadow one arrow casts along the direction of the other arrow. However, if one arrow is 10 miles long, its shadow will be huge even if it points in a completely different direction!

Cosine similarity shrinks both arrows down to a standardized unit length of 1 meter (\(\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}\|_2 = 1\)). By forcing both arrows to unit length, cosine similarity evaluates only the direction (angle \(\theta\)) in which they point, completely ignoring how long the original vectors were.

Mathematical Properties & Boundary Bounds of Cosine Similarity:

  1. Non-Negative Frequency Vectors (\(v_i, w_i \ge 0\)):

When feature vectors consist of term counts or frequency weights, all entries are non-negative. Consequently, the angle \(\theta\) is restricted to the first quadrant \([0^\circ, 90^\circ]\), bounding cosine similarity between \(0\) and \(1\):

  • \(\cos(\theta) = 1.0\) (\(\theta = 0^\circ\)): The vectors point in exactly identical directions in feature space. The word or document profiles are perfectly proportional.
  • \(\cos(\theta) = 0.0\) (\(\theta = 90^\circ\)): The vectors are orthogonal. They share zero co-occurring features.
  1. Continuous Dense Embeddings (\(v_i, w_i \in \mathbb{R}\)):

In learned neural embedding spaces (such as Skip-gram, GloVe, or BERT) where vector weights can take negative real values, the angle \(\theta\) ranges across \([0^\circ, 180^\circ]\), bounding cosine similarity between \(-1\) and \(+1\):

  • \(\cos(\theta) = +1.0\): Identical semantic meaning.
  • \(\cos(\theta) = 0.0\): Unrelated semantic concepts.
  • \(\cos(\theta) = -1.0\) (\(\theta = 180^\circ\)): Diametrically opposed semantic meanings.

2.3.4 Worked Examples of Cosine Similarity Calculations

Worked Example 2.3.1 — Cosine Similarity between cherry and information:

We calculate the cosine similarity between the target word cherry (\(\mathbf{v}\)) and information (\(\mathbf{w}\)) evaluated over three context features (pie, computer, data):

  • \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{cherry}} = [442, 8, 2]^\top\)
  • \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{info}} = [5, 3982, 3325]^\top\)

Step 1: Compute the Dot Product \(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} &= (442 \times 5) + (8 \times 3982) + (2 \times 3325) \\ &= 2210 + 31856 + 6650 \\ &= 40716 \end{aligned} \]

Step 2: Compute Euclidean Magnitude \(\|\mathbf{v}_{\text{cherry}}\|_2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf{v}_{\text{cherry}}\|_2 &= \sqrt{442^2 + 8^2 + 2^2} \\ &= \sqrt{195364 + 64 + 4} \\ &= \sqrt{195432} \approx 442.0769 \end{aligned} \]

Step 3: Compute Euclidean Magnitude \(\|\mathbf{w}_{\text{info}}\|_2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf{w}_{\text{info}}\|_2 &= \sqrt{5^2 + 3982^2 + 3325^2} \\ &= \sqrt{25 + 15856324 + 11055625} \\ &= \sqrt{26911974} \approx 5187.6752 \end{aligned} \]

Step 4: Compute Product of Magnitudes:

\[ \|\mathbf{v}\|_2 \|\mathbf{w}\|_2 = 442.0769 \times 5187.6752 \approx 2293351.67 \]

Step 5: Compute Cosine Similarity:

\[ \text{CosineSimilarity}(\mathbf{v}_{\text{cherry}}, \mathbf{w}_{\text{info}}) = \frac{40716}{2293351.67} \approx \mathbf{0.01775} \]

Sense-Check: The resulting score (\(\approx 0.0178\)) is near zero, confirming mathematically that cherry (fruit domain) and information (tech domain) share virtually no semantic similarity.

Worked Example 2.3.2 — Cosine Similarity between digital and information:

We calculate the cosine similarity between digital (\(\mathbf{v}\)) and information (\(\mathbf{w}\)) over the same three context features (pie, computer, data):

  • \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{digital}} = [5, 1683, 1670]^\top\)
  • \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{info}} = [5, 3982, 3325]^\top\)

Step 1: Compute the Dot Product \(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} &= (5 \times 5) + (1683 \times 3982) + (1670 \times 3325) \\ &= 25 + 6701706 + 5552750 \\ &= 12254481 \end{aligned} \]

Step 2: Compute Euclidean Magnitude \(\|\mathbf{v}_{\text{digital}}\|_2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf{v}_{\text{digital}}\|_2 &= \sqrt{5^2 + 1683^2 + 1670^2} \\ &= \sqrt{25 + 2832489 + 2788900} \\ &= \sqrt{5621414} \approx 2370.9521 \end{aligned} \]

Step 3: Compute Euclidean Magnitude \(\|\mathbf{w}_{\text{info}}\|_2\):

\[ \|\mathbf{w}_{\text{info}}\|_2 = \sqrt{26911974} \approx 5187.6752 \]

Step 4: Compute Product of Magnitudes:

\[ \|\mathbf{v}\|_2 \|\mathbf{w}\|_2 = 2370.9521 \times 5187.6752 \approx 12299679.29 \]

Step 5: Compute Cosine Similarity:

\[ \text{CosineSimilarity}(\mathbf{v}_{\text{digital}}, \mathbf{w}_{\text{info}}) = \frac{12254481}{12299679.29} \approx \mathbf{0.99632} \]

Sense-Check: The resulting score (\(\approx 0.9963\)) is extremely close to \(1.0\), proving mathematically that digital and information share almost identical contextual usage and strong semantic alignment.

Assumptions & Scope:

  • Invariance to Length, Sensitivity to Angle: Cosine similarity assumes that topic similarity is purely a function of vector direction. If a task requires distinguishing document depth or total word volume, length-neutral cosine similarity must be augmented with length metadata.
  • Euclidean Distance vs. Cosine Distance: Euclidean distance \(d(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}) = \|\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{w}\|_2\) is sensitive to length. For raw count vectors, Euclidean distance between two long documents on the same topic can be massive, whereas Cosine Distance (\(1 - \cos(\theta)\)) remains zero.

Common Pitfalls:

  1. Forgetting to Square Magnitudes Before Summing: Students often incorrectly compute \(\sum v_i\) and then square the total, rather than summing individual squared terms \(\sum (v_i^2)\).
  2. Dividing by Unsquared Sums: Ensure that the denominator contains the square root of the sum of squares \(\sqrt{\sum v_i^2}\), not the sum of absolute values.

2.3.5 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Can cosine similarity ever yield a negative output value?

A: For non-negative frequency count vectors (such as Term-Document or Co-occurrence matrices), all component values are \(\ge 0\), so cosine similarity is bounded between \(0.0\) and \(+1.0\). However, in dense predictive embedding spaces (like Word2Vec, GloVe, or BERT) where neural network weights can take negative real numbers, cosine similarity ranges from \(-1.0\) to \(+1.0\), where negative values indicate opposing semantic directions.

Exam note: Exam problems frequently require step-by-step hand calculations of cosine similarity between two feature vectors. Always calculate: (1) dot product in the numerator, (2) Euclidean magnitude of vector 1, (3) Euclidean magnitude of vector 2, (4) multiply magnitudes in the denominator, and (5) divide numerator by denominator to obtain the final similarity score bounded in \([0, 1]\).

Section Recap & Bridge:

Cosine similarity normalizes for document length by dividing the dot product by Euclidean vector magnitudes, measuring vector angle rather than magnitude.

Bridge to Next Section: While cosine similarity solves the vector length problem, raw count vectors still suffer from frequency distortion caused by ubiquitous stop words. In Section 2.4, we study Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) to re-weight feature counts.

Real-World & Domain Connection:

Cosine similarity is the primary distance metric used in vector databases (e.g., Faiss, Pinecone, Weaviate) to perform K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) semantic retrieval. In semantic search, user queries and candidate documents are embedded into vector spaces, and cosine similarity ranks the most contextually relevant documents in real time.

2.4 Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF)

Hook: If the word "good" or "the" appears 500 times in a novel, does that make "good" 100 times more important than a unique keyword like "cybersecurity" that appears 5 times? How can a mathematical formula penalize ubiquitous stop words while amplifying rare, highly informative terms?

2.4.1 Symbol Registry

Symbol Registry & Variable Definitions:

  • \(t\) — Target term/word token — String element in vocabulary \(V\)
  • \(d\) — Target document — Document element in corpus \(D\)
  • \(N\) — Total number of documents in the corpus collection — Positive integer \(\mathbb{N}^+\)
  • \(\text{count}(t, d)\) — Raw frequency count of term \(t\) inside document \(d\) — Non-negative integer \(\mathbb{N}_{\ge 0}\)
  • \(\text{TF}(t, d)\) — Logarithmically scaled term frequency of term \(t\) in document \(d\) — Real scalar \(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\)
  • \(\text{DF}(t)\) — Document frequency of term \(t\) across the corpus — Integer \(1 \le \text{DF}(t) \le N\)
  • \(\text{CF}(t)\) — Collection frequency of term \(t\) summed over all documents — Non-negative integer \(\mathbb{N}_{\ge 0}\)
  • \(\text{IDF}(t)\) — Inverse document frequency of term \(t\) — Real scalar \(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\)
  • \(w_{t, d}\) — Combined TF-IDF statistical weight of term \(t\) in document \(d\) — Real scalar \(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\)

2.4.2 Term Frequency (TF) with Logarithmic Scaling

Raw count frequency presents a fundamental problem in text analysis: diminishing marginal relevance. A word appearing 10 times in a document is informative, but it is certainly not 10 times more important than a word appearing once. Furthermore, raw count vectors are overwhelmed by ubiquitous stop words.

To suppress linear raw count scaling, we apply a logarithmic transformation with Laplace-style smoothing (\(+1\)).

Logarithmically Scaled Term Frequency:

The logarithmically scaled term frequency \(\text{TF}(t, d)\) compresses raw word counts to reflect diminishing marginal relevance:

\[ \text{TF}(t, d) = \log_{10}(\text{count}(t, d) + 1) \]

Intuition & Laplace Smoothing (\(+1\)) Rationale:

  • Diminishing Returns: Taking the logarithm compresses count ranges. For instance, a count of \(1\) yields \(\log_{10}(2) \approx 0.301\), a count of \(10\) yields \(\log_{10}(11) \approx 1.041\), and a count of \(100\) yields \(\log_{10}(101) \approx 2.004\). The score increases smoothly rather than blowing up linearly.
  • Handling Zero Occurrences: If a word does not appear in a document (\(\text{count}(t, d) = 0\)), taking a raw logarithm \(\log_{10}(0)\) would evaluate to \(-\infty\) (undefined). Adding \(+1\) inside the logarithm ensures that zero occurrences evaluate cleanly to \(\log_{10}(0 + 1) = \log_{10}(1) = 0\).

2.4.3 Document Frequency (DF) vs. Collection Frequency (CF)

Before formulating global word weighting, a critical distinction must be drawn between Document Frequency and Collection Frequency.

Document Frequency (\(\text{DF}\)) vs. Collection Frequency (\(\text{CF}\)):

  • Document Frequency \(\text{DF}(t)\): The number of individual documents in the corpus collection that contain term \(t\) at least once. It measures how broadly distributed a term is across documents (\(1 \le \text{DF}(t) \le N\)).
  • Collection Frequency \(\text{CF}(t)\): The total number of times term \(t\) appears across the entire corpus, summed over all documents (\(\text{CF}(t) = \sum_{d \in D} \text{count}(t, d)\)). It measures total word volume.

Intuition & The Romeo vs. Action Comparison:

Consider a corpus of \(N = 100\) documents (plays and historical records):

  1. Term A (Romeo): Appears a total of 113 times in the corpus (\(\text{CF}(\text{Romeo}) = 113\)). However, all 113 occurrences reside inside a single document (Romeo and Juliet). Thus, \(\text{DF}(\text{Romeo}) = 1\).
  2. Term B (action): Appears a total of 113 times in the corpus (\(\text{CF}(\text{action}) = 113\)). However, it is spread thinly across 31 separate documents. Thus, \(\text{DF}(\text{action}) = 31\).

Linguistic Deduction:

Collection frequency \(\text{CF}\) sees both terms as identical (\(113\)). However, Document Frequency \(\text{DF}\) reveals that Romeo is a highly specific, informative keyword concentrated in one document (\(\text{DF} = 1\)), whereas action is a generic term scattered across many documents (\(\text{DF} = 31\)). TF-IDF relies strictly on Document Frequency \(\text{DF}(t)\).

2.4.4 Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) Formulation

Inverse Document Frequency (IDF):

Inverse Document Frequency measures how much information a word provides—specifically whether the term is rare or ubiquitous across the corpus collection. IDF is defined as the logarithm of the ratio of total documents \(N\) to document frequency \(\text{DF}(t)\):

\[ \text{IDF}(t) = \log_{10}\left(\frac{N}{\text{DF}(t)}\right) \]

Mathematical Behavior of IDF:

  • Rare Terms (\(\text{DF}(t) \ll N\)): If term \(t\) appears in very few documents, \(\frac{N}{\text{DF}(t)}\) is large, yielding a high \(\text{IDF}(t)\) score.
  • Ubiquitous Terms (\(\text{DF}(t) = N\)): If term \(t\) appears in every single document in the corpus collection, \(\frac{N}{\text{DF}(t)} = \frac{N}{N} = 1\). Taking \(\log_{10}(1) = 0\) produces an IDF score of zero, completely wiping out the word's impact.

2.4.5 Combined TF-IDF Weight Formulation

Combined TF-IDF Weight:

The final TF-IDF weight \(w_{t, d}\) combines local log term frequency \(\text{TF}(t, d)\) with global inverse document frequency \(\text{IDF}(t)\):

\[ w_{t, d} = \text{TF-IDF}(t, d) = \text{TF}(t, d) \times \text{IDF}(t) \] \[ w_{t, d} = \log_{10}(\text{count}(t, d) + 1) \times \log_{10}\left(\frac{N}{\text{DF}(t)}\right) \]

Properties of High TF-IDF Weights:

A word achieves a maximum TF-IDF weight when it occurs frequently within a specific target document (high local \(\text{TF}\)) but appears rarely across the general corpus collection (high global \(\text{IDF}\)).

2.4.6 Worked Example of TF-IDF Computation

Worked Example 2.4.1 — TF-IDF Weight for wit in As You Like It (\(D_1\)):

Consider a Shakespeare corpus consisting of \(N = 37\) play documents. We compute the TF-IDF weight for wit in As You Like It (\(D_1\)).

  • Total corpus documents: \(N = 37\)
  • Raw count of wit in As You Like It (\(D_1\)): \(\text{count}(\text{wit}, D_1) = 20\)
  • Document frequency of wit across corpus: \(\text{DF}(\text{wit}) = 34\)

Step 1: Calculate Log-Scaled Term Frequency \(\text{TF}(\text{wit}, D_1)\):

\[ \text{TF}(\text{wit}, D_1) = \log_{10}(20 + 1) = \log_{10}(21) \approx 1.32222 \]

Step 2: Calculate Inverse Document Frequency \(\text{IDF}(\text{wit})\):

\[ \text{IDF}(\text{wit}) = \log_{10}\left(\frac{37}{34}\right) = \log_{10}(1.088235) \approx 0.03672 \]

Step 3: Compute Combined TF-IDF Weight \(w_{\text{wit}, D_1}\):

\[ w_{\text{wit}, D_1} = 1.32222 \times 0.03672 \approx \mathbf{0.04855} \]

Worked Example 2.4.2 — TF-IDF Weight for Ubiquitous Term good in As You Like It (\(D_1\)):

Now consider the term good in the exact same play document As You Like It (\(D_1\)):

  • Raw count of good in As You Like It: \(\text{count}(\text{good}, D_1) = 114\)
  • Document frequency of good across corpus: \(\text{DF}(\text{good}) = 37\) (appears in all 37 plays)

Step 1: Calculate Term Frequency \(\text{TF}(\text{good}, D_1)\):

\[ \text{TF}(\text{good}, D_1) = \log_{10}(114 + 1) = \log_{10}(115) \approx 2.06070 \]

Step 2: Calculate Inverse Document Frequency \(\text{IDF}(\text{good})\):

\[ \text{IDF}(\text{good}) = \log_{10}\left(\frac{37}{37}\right) = \log_{10}(1.0) = 0.0 \]

Step 3: Compute Combined TF-IDF Weight \(w_{\text{good}, D_1}\):

\[ w_{\text{good}, D_1} = 2.06070 \times 0.0 = \mathbf{0.0} \]

Sense-Check: Despite occurring 114 times in the document, good receives a final TF-IDF weight of zero. Because it appears across all 37 plays, its IDF multiplier zeroes out its score, successfully preventing ubiquitous words from distorting document representations.

Assumptions & Scope:

  • Independent Term Weighting: TF-IDF evaluates terms independently, assuming no interaction or syntactic dependencies between co-occurring words.
  • Corpus Sizing Dependence: IDF scores are sensitive to corpus boundaries. A term that is rare in general English (high IDF) might be common in a specialized medical corpus (low IDF).

Common Pitfalls:

  1. Confusing DF and CF: The most frequent exam mistake is using Collection Frequency (\(\text{CF}\)) instead of Document Frequency (\(\text{DF}\)) inside the IDF denominator \(\log_{10}(N / \text{DF})\).
  2. Missing the Log Base: Standard TF-IDF hand calculations use base-10 logarithms (\(\log_{10}\)). Using natural \(\ln\) changes the numerical scale.

2.4.7 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why is Laplace smoothing (\(+1\)) added inside the log term frequency formula \(\log_{10}(\text{count} + 1)\)?

A: Adding \(+1\) serves two critical mathematical purposes: (1) it prevents taking the logarithm of zero (\(\log_{10}(0) = -\infty\)) for words that do not appear in a document, ensuring \(\text{count} = 0 \implies \log_{10}(1) = 0\); and (2) it ensures that a count of 1 yields a non-negative TF score (\(\log_{10}(2) > 0\)).

Q: Why does the term good receive a final TF-IDF weight of zero even though its raw count in the play is 114?

A: Because good appears across all \(N = 37\) play documents in the corpus collection (\(\text{DF}(\text{good}) = 37\)). Its inverse document frequency evaluates to \(\text{IDF}(\text{good}) = \log_{10}(37 / 37) = \log_{10}(1) = 0\). Multiplying any log TF value by zero yields a final TF-IDF weight of zero.

Exam note: Exam questions routinely test step-by-step hand calculations for TF, IDF, and combined TF-IDF weights, as well as conceptual distinctions between Document Frequency (\(\text{DF}\)) and Collection Frequency (\(\text{CF}\)). Always show intermediate \(\log_{10}\) values explicitly.

Section Recap & Bridge:

TF-IDF balances local term frequency (log TF) against global term rarity (IDF), zeroing out ubiquitous words while boosting discriminative keywords.

Real-World & Domain Connection:

TF-IDF and its probabilistic extension BM25 (Best Matching 25) power production search engines like Apache Lucene, Elasticsearch, and Solr. BM25 modifies TF-IDF by adding non-linear saturation to term frequency and document-length normalization, serving as the industry standard keyword retrieval algorithm.

2.5 Implementation of Vectorization in Python (scikit-learn)

Hook: How do software engineers move from theoretical math equations to production Python code? How can we convert millions of raw text documents into compressed, high-performance sparse matrices in just three lines of code?

2.5.1 Symbol Registry

Symbol Registry & Scikit-Learn Class Definitions:

  • CountVectorizer — Scikit-learn feature extraction class that converts raw text documents into a matrix of token counts — Python class
  • TfidfVectorizer — Scikit-learn class that computes TF-IDF weighted feature matrices directly from raw text — Python class
  • fit_transform() — Core vectorizer method that constructs the vocabulary index and transforms text into a sparse matrix — Python method
  • get_feature_names_out() — Method returning the ordered array of vocabulary feature strings — Python method
  • CSR Matrix — Compressed Sparse Row matrix format from scipy.sparse used to store sparse data efficiently — Data structure

2.5.2 CountVectorizer Implementation

Procedural Spine — CountVectorizer Pipeline:

  1. Purpose: Convert a collection of raw text documents into a 2D matrix of raw token counts.
  2. Inputs & Parameters:
  • corpus: List of string documents.
  • stop_words='english': Automatically filters out common English stop words (e.g., "the", "and", "is").
  • lowercase=True: Converts all characters to lowercase before tokenizing.
  1. Execution Steps:
  • Step A: Tokenize raw text strings using regex matching on word boundaries (\b\w\w+\b).
  • Step B: Apply lowercasing and stop-word filtering.
  • Step C: Build vocabulary index mapping each unique token string to an integer column index \(0, 1, \dots, |V|-1\).
  • Step D: Construct and return a Scipy Compressed Sparse Row (CSR) matrix of shape (n_samples, n_features).

```python

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer

Sample corpus containing 3 documents

corpus = [

"The movie was terrible and bad.",

"The weather is bad and terrible today.",

"Digital information and computer data processing.",

]

Initialize CountVectorizer filtering standard English stop words

vectorizer = CountVectorizer(stop_words="english")

Build vocabulary and transform text into count matrix

X_counts = vectorizer.fit_transform(corpus)

Retrieve learned vocabulary feature names

feature_names = vectorizer.get_feature_names_out()

print("Learned Vocabulary Features:\n", feature_names)

print("\nSparse Count Matrix Shape:", X_counts.shape)

print("\nDense Array Matrix View:\n", X_counts.toarray())

```

Code Trace & Expected Output:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]
  • Vocabulary Features (9 unique tokens): ['bad', 'computer', 'data', 'digital', 'information', 'movie', 'processing', 'terrible', 'today']
  • Matrix Dimensions: (3, 9) — 3 documents, 9 vocabulary features.
  • Dense Array View:
  • Document 1 contains bad (1), movie (1), terrible (1).
  • Document 3 contains computer (1), data (1), digital (1), information (1), processing (1). Notice how documents 1 & 2 share zero feature overlap with document 3.

2.5.3 TfidfVectorizer Implementation

TfidfVectorizer Pipeline & Parameter Formulation:

TfidfVectorizer combines raw count extraction, smooth inverse document frequency calculation, and row-wise vector normalization in a single execution step.

```python

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

Sample corpus

corpus = [

"The movie was terrible and bad.",

"The weather is bad and terrible today.",

"Digital information and computer data processing.",

]

Initialize TfidfVectorizer with smooth_idf and L2 vector normalization

tfidf_vectorizer = TfidfVectorizer(

stop_words="english",

smooth_idf=True,

norm="l2"

)

Build vocabulary and transform text directly into TF-IDF matrix

X_tfidf = tfidf_vectorizer.fit_transform(corpus)

Output learned features and normalized TF-IDF matrix

print("TF-IDF Feature Names:\n", tfidf_vectorizer.get_feature_names_out())

print("\nTF-IDF Matrix Shape:", X_tfidf.shape)

print("\nDense TF-IDF Array View:\n", X_tfidf.toarray().round(4))

```

Scikit-Learn TF-IDF Parameter Formulations:

  1. Smooth IDF Formula (smooth_idf=True):

Scikit-learn modifies the standard IDF equation by adding \(+1\) to both the numerator and denominator (as well as an outer \(+1\)) to prevent division-by-zero:

\[ \text{IDF}_{\text{sklearn}}(t) = \ln\left(\frac{N + 1}{\text{DF}(t) + 1}\right) + 1 \]

(Note: Scikit-learn uses natural logarithm \(\ln\) by default).

  1. \(L_2\)-Norm Row Normalization (norm='l2'):

After computing TF-IDF weights, TfidfVectorizer scales each document row vector \(\mathbf{v}_d\) to unit Euclidean norm (\(\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}_d\|_2 = 1.0\)):

\[ \hat{\mathbf{v}}_d = \frac{\mathbf{v}_d}{\|\mathbf{v}_d\|_2} = \frac{\mathbf{v}_d}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_{d, i}^2}} \] \[ \text{CosineSimilarity}(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_1, \hat{\mathbf{v}}_2) = \hat{\mathbf{v}}_1 \cdot \hat{\mathbf{v}}_2 \]
  • Critical Benefit: Because every document vector is pre-normalized to unit length (\(\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}\|_2 = 1\)), the cosine similarity between any two documents reduces simply to their dot product:

Complexity & Scalability Constraints:

  • Time Complexity: Fitting and transforming text scales linearly with total token volume \(\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{tokens}})\).
  • Space Complexity: Storing sparse matrices in Scipy CSR format uses memory proportional only to the number of non-zero entries (\(\text{NNZ}\)), preventing Out-Of-Memory (OOM) crashes on large vocabularies.

Common Pitfalls:

  1. Calling .toarray() on Massive Corpora: Executing X_tfidf.toarray() converts a sparse matrix into a dense NumPy array. For a corpus of 100,000 documents and 100,000 features, converting to dense requires 80 GB of RAM and will crash your Python process. Keep matrices in sparse format!
  2. Data Leakage in Test Sets: Always call fit_transform(X_train) on training data, but call ONLY transform(X_test) on test data. Calling fit_transform on test data leaks test vocabulary into your model training pipeline.

2.5.4 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Are text preprocessing utilities like stemming and lemmatization available in Spacey as well as NLTK?

A: Yes. Both NLTK and Spacey (SpaCy) offer APIs for stemming and lemmatization. NLTK provides individual algorithmic classes (such as PorterStemmer and WordNetLemmatizer). High-Probability Exam Calculation Types:

Exam problems routinely test step-by-step hand calculations. Practice performing the following computations manually:

  1. Cosine Similarity Calculations:
  • Given two numerical vectors \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\), compute the dot product \(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} = \sum v_i w_i\).
  • Compute Euclidean magnitudes \(\|\mathbf{v}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum v_i^2}\) and \(\|\mathbf{w}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum w_i^2}\).
  • Divide dot product by magnitude product: \(\text{CosineSimilarity} = \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_2 \|\mathbf{w}\|_2}\).
  1. Log-Scaled Term Frequency (TF):
  • Apply Laplace smoothing \(\text{TF}(t, d) = \log_{10}(\text{count}(t, d) + 1)\).
  • Remember that zero counts evaluate to \(\log_{10}(1) = 0\).
  1. Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) & Combined TF-IDF Weights:
  • Compute \(\text{IDF}(t) = \log_{10}\left(\frac{N}{\text{DF}(t)}\right)\).
  • Multiply log TF by IDF: \(w_{t, d} = \text{TF}(t, d) \times \text{IDF}(t)\).
  • Note that ubiquitous words appearing in all \(N\) documents (\(\text{DF} = N\)) get \(\text{IDF} = \log_{10}(1) = 0\).

Critical Conceptual Distinctions to Memorize:

  • Document Frequency (\(\text{DF}\)) vs. Collection Frequency (\(\text{CF}\)): \(\text{DF}\) counts distinct documents containing the term; \(\text{CF}\) sums total term occurrences across all documents. TF-IDF uses \(\text{DF}\), not \(\text{CF}\).
  • One-Hot Orthogonality Failure: Explain why dot product of one-hot vectors is always 0.
  • Context Window Radius Rationale: Explain why a symmetric window radius of \(k = \pm 4\) or \(\pm 5\) is chosen (average English sentence length is 8–10 words).

Term Frequency and Co-occurrence Matrices

Must-know: Term-document matrices capture document topics (columns); word-word matrices capture word semantics (rows) using sliding windows of radius k=+-4.

\[M_{i,c} = \text{count of } w_i \text{ within } \pm k \text{ window of } w_c\]

Top pitfall: Confusing document-level co-occurrence with local sliding window co-occurrence, or chunking text instead of sliding windows.

Mathematical Formulation of Vector Similarity

Must-know: Cosine similarity divides dot product by the product of Euclidean norms to calculate cos(theta), isolating direction from length.

\[\text{CosineSimilarity}(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}) = \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_2 \|\mathbf{w}\|_2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_i w_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i^2}}\]

Top pitfall: Confusing dot product with cosine similarity, or taking the square of sums instead of sum of squares for vector magnitude.

Self-check: Why does raw dot product fail as a similarity metric for documents of different lengths?

Connects to: 2.2, 2.4

Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF)

Must-know: TF-IDF multiplies log term frequency by log(N/DF); DF measures document presence, CF measures total corpus count; ubiquitous terms (DF=N) get IDF=0.

\[w_{t, d} = \log_{10}(\text{count}(t, d) + 1) \times \log_{10}\left(\frac{N}{\text{DF}(t)}\right)\]

Top pitfall: Confusing DF with CF in the IDF formula denominator, or omitting +1 Laplace smoothing inside the TF log.
