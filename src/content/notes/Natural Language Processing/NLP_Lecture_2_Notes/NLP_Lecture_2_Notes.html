The Need for Vector Semantics

2.1 Introduction: From Words to Numbers

Every modern NLP application—whether it is conversational AI, question answering, machine translation, sentiment analysis, or text generation—requires a fundamental understanding of language. And at the heart of language understanding lies the meaning of words.

Key Concept:

Lexical Semantics is the study of word meanings and the systematic meaning-related connections between words. A lexeme is a pairing of a particular orthographic or phonological form with its meaning. The lexicon is a finite list of lexemes—the complete vocabulary of a language.

A lemma (or citation form) is the grammatical base form used to represent a lexeme in dictionaries. For example, sing is the lemma for wordforms like sang and sung; carpet is the lemma for carpets. The specific inflected or derived forms like sang or carpets are called wordforms. The process of mapping a wordform to its lemma is called lemmatization. Lemmatization is not always deterministic—the wordform found could map to lemma find (“to locate”) or found (“to create an institution”) depending on context. Lemmas are also part-of-speech specific: the wordform tables has two possible lemmas (noun table and verb table).

The central challenge is: How do we make machines understand the meaning of words? Machines are inherently better at processing numbers than strings of characters. A computer cannot "understand" the word dog any more than it can understand cat just by looking at the sequence of letters. Representing words as raw strings of characters gives no information about their meaning, their relationships, or their usage.

Example:

Consider the words bad and terrible. A human knows they are semantically related (both express negative sentiment). But to a machine seeing only the character strings "b-a-d" and "t-e-r-r-i-b-l-e," there is no obvious connection. There is no shared substring, no edit-distance measure that reveals they are synonyms.

The solution is to represent words numerically—specifically, as vectors (ordered arrays of real numbers). When words are encoded as vectors, machines can perform mathematical operations (such as computing distances and similarities) to infer relationships among them.

Key Concept:

Word Embedding (or Vector Semantics) is the technique of mapping words from a discrete symbolic domain (strings) into a continuous vector space \(\mathbb{R}^d\), where \(d\) is the embedding dimension. Each word becomes a point in this \(d\)-dimensional space.

2.1.1 The Problem with Meaning

What does a word mean? Traditional approaches often fall short.

  • The Index Approach: "dog" is word #5342. This tells us nothing about what a dog is.
  • The Logical Approach: The meaning of "dog" is the concept DOG. As Barbara Partee famously joked, "What’s the meaning of life? LIFE." It’s technically correct but entirely unhelpful computationally.

2.1.2 Lemmas and Senses

The relationship between words and concepts is complex. As established above, a lemma is the base or dictionary form (e.g., ‘run’), and a sense (or word sense) is a discrete representation of one aspect of the meaning of a word. In lexicographic tradition, we represent different senses by placing superscripts on the lemma: bank1 and bank2.

2.1.3 Homonymy vs. Polysemy

Key Concept:

When two senses of a word share the same form but are semantically unrelated, they are called homonyms, and the relationship is homonymy. When two senses are semantically related, the relationship is called polysemy.

Example — Homonymy:

Consider the lemma bank:

  • bank1: financial institution (e.g., "a bank can hold the investments in a custodial account")
  • bank2: sloping mound (e.g., "agriculture burgeons on the east bank, the river will shrink")

These two senses seem completely unrelated—it is almost coincidental that they share the same orthographic form. They are homonyms.

Example — Polysemy:

Now consider additional uses of bank:

  • bank3: biological repository (e.g., "some banks furnish sperm only to married women") — related to bank1, since both are repositories for entities that can be deposited and withdrawn.
  • bank4: the building belonging to a financial institution (e.g., "the bank is on the corner of Nassau and Witherspoon") — a systematic relationship we can represent as BUILDING ↔ ORGANIZATION.

These senses are semantically connected. In many cases of polysemy the relationship between senses is systematic and structured across the language.

2.1.4 Metonymy

Key Concept:

Metonymy is a specific subtype of polysemy where one aspect of a concept or entity is used to refer to other aspects of the entity, or to the entity itself. For example, using “the White House” to refer to the administration whose office is in the White House.

Common metonymy pairings include:

  • Author ↔ Works of Author: Jane Austen wrote Emma ↔ I really love Jane Austen
  • Animal ↔ Meat: The chicken was domesticated in Asia ↔ The chicken was overcooked
  • Tree ↔ Fruit: Plums have beautiful blossoms ↔ I ate a preserved plum
  • Organization ↔ Building: The university admitted 5000 students ↔ The university is on Main Street

2.1.5 How to Tell Senses Apart: Zeugma

One practical technique for determining whether two uses of a word constitute distinct senses is zeugma — conjoining two uses in a single sentence where each use requires a different sense. If the resulting sentence sounds semantically odd, the two uses likely represent different senses.

Example — Zeugma:
  • ✓ Which of those flights serve breakfast?
  • ✓ Does Midwest Express serve Philadelphia?
  • ? Does Midwest Express serve breakfast and Philadelphia? (semantically ill-formed)

The oddness of the combined sentence reveals that serve has two distinct senses here: one meaning “provide food” and another meaning “provide a commercial service to a location.”

2.1.6 Homophones and Homographs

Two special cases of multiple senses cause particular problems for speech and language systems:

  • Homophones: Senses linked to lemmas with the same pronunciation but different spellings (e.g., wood/would, to/two/too). These are problematic for speech recognition and spelling correction.
  • Homographs: Distinct senses with the same orthographic form but different pronunciations (e.g., bass as in the fish [b ae s] vs. bass as in low musical sound [b ey s]). These are problematic for speech synthesis.

There is no hard threshold for “how related” two senses must be to be considered polysemous rather than homonymous; the difference is one of degree. This makes it very difficult to decide exactly how many senses a word has in computational systems. Dictionaries tend to use many fine-grained senses, while for NLP we often group or cluster senses for computational efficiency.

Words are often polysemous (having multiple senses). Consider the lemma "mouse":

  • A small rodent.
  • A hand-operated device that controls a cursor.

A robust model of meaning must be able to differentiate these senses and understand how they relate to other words (e.g., one sense relates to "cheese"; the other relates to "click").

2.1.7 Why Meaning Matters in Practice

Question Answering:

Q: “How tall is Mt. Everest?”
A: “The official height of Mount Everest is 29029 feet.”

To connect the question to the answer, the system must recognize that "tall" is semantically similar to "height".

Plagiarism Detection:

Systems must identify similarity beyond simple keyword matching, detecting paraphrased or reworded content by analyzing the underlying semantics.

2.1.8 Why This Matters

Word embeddings are the foundational input to virtually all modern NLP architectures:

  • Transformers (BERT, GPT, T5) — input tokens are converted to embeddings before being processed by attention layers.
  • Large Language Models (LLMs) — the first layer of any LLM is an embedding layer that maps token IDs to dense vectors.
  • Agentic AI systems — the "brain" of any autonomous agent is an LLM, which relies on embeddings.
  • Search and Information Retrieval — queries and documents are compared in vector space.
  • Speech Recognition — acoustic features are ultimately mapped to word embeddings for language modeling.

Understanding vector semantics is therefore the ABCD or foundation for all advanced NLP courses and applications.

2.2 The Distributional Hypothesis

How can we assign meaningful vector representations without manually labeling every word? The answer comes from a foundational idea in linguistics and NLP:

Key Concept:

The Distributional Hypothesis (attributed to Zellig Harris, 1954, and popularized by J.R. Firth): "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." Words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings.

Example:

Inferring meaning from context:

Suppose you encounter the unfamiliar word oculist in a text:

"The oculist examined the patient's eyes and prescribed new glasses."

Even without knowing the word, you can infer from the surrounding words (examined, patient, eyes, glasses) that an oculist is a medical professional who deals with eyes—likely an ophthalmologist or optometrist. You inferred this purely from the context words, not from any prior dictionary definition.

Example:

The "ong choy" example (from Jurafsky & Martin):

Imagine you see these sentences about an unknown word ong choy:

  • Stir-fry ong choy with garlic and salt.
  • Ong choy is delicious served over rice.
  • The leaves of ong choy are tender and spinach-like.

From the context words—stir-fry, garlic, delicious, rice, leaves, spinach—you can deduce that ong choy is an edible leafy green vegetable, even if you have never heard of it. The context words tell you what it is.

This insight leads directly to a computational principle: if we can build vectors that capture the distributional context of each word, then words with similar vectors will be semantically related.

2.3 Semantic Spaces and Word Relations

Words do not exist in isolation. Their meanings are defined by their relationships with other words. Understanding these relationships is key to building computational models of meaning.

2.3.1 Synonymy: The Same Meaning?

Synonyms are words with the same (or nearly the same) meaning. Formally, two words are synonymous if they are substitutable one for the other in any sentence without changing the truth conditions of the sentence. In this case we say the two words have the same propositional meaning.

  • Examples: couch/sofa, car/automobile, vomit/throw up, filbert/hazelnut.

However, perfect synonymy is extremely rare — probably no two words are absolutely identical in meaning across all contexts and connotations. Context, genre, and nuance often matter:

Example — The big/large distinction:

Consider big and large. These may seem synonymous:

  • ✓ How big is that plane? ← can substitute large
  • ✓ Would I be flying on a large or small plane? ← can substitute big

But substitution fails in other contexts:

  • ✓ Miss Nelson became a kind of big sister to Benjamin.
  • ? Miss Nelson became a kind of large sister to Benjamin. (semantically odd)

This is because big has a sense meaning “older” or “grown up” that large lacks. This is why in NLP we define synonymy as a relation between senses, not between entire words. Some senses of big and large are (nearly) synonymous while other ones are not.

Other examples of near-synonyms differentiated by genre or usage:

  • An H2O molecule vs. a glass of water — same propositional meaning, different genre (scientific vs. everyday).
  • A chemistry paper uses "H2O," while a surfing guide uses "water."

In practice, synonymy is usually approximate or rough synonymy, and computational systems define it as a relation between specific senses rather than between whole words.

2.3.2 Similarity vs. Relatedness: A Crucial Distinction

Important Distinction:
RelationshipDefinitionExamples
SimilarityWords sharing core elements of meaning; often belong to the same class.cat/dog (both pets, mammals); car/bicycle (both modes of transport)
Relatedness (Association)Words frequently appearing together or belonging to the same semantic field/frame, but not meaning the same thing.coffee/cup; doctor/scalpel; car/gasoline

"Coffee" is similar to "Tea", but "Coffee" is related to "Cup". Both relationships matter for different NLP applications.

2.3.3 Other Key Lexical Relations

  • Antonymy: Words with opposite meaning. Two senses can be antonyms if they define a binary opposition or are at opposite ends of some scale. Examples: long/short, big/little, fast/slow, cold/hot, dark/light. Another group of antonyms is reversives, which describe change or movement in opposite directions, such as rise/fall or up/down. Interestingly, antonyms have very different meanings (they are opposite), yet they also share almost all aspects of their meaning except their position on a scale or their direction—making automatically distinguishing synonyms from antonyms computationally difficult.
  • Hypernymy: Hierarchical "Is-A" relationships. A sense A is a hyponym of sense B if A is more specific and denotes a subclass of B. Conversely, B is a hypernym (or superordinate) of A. Formally, hyponymy can be defined in terms of entailment: sense A is a hyponym of sense B if everything that is A is also B, i.e., \(\forall x\; A(x) \Rightarrow B(x)\).

    Transitivity: Hyponymy is usually a transitive relation — if A is a hyponym of B and B is a hyponym of C, then A is a hyponym of C.

    Taxonomy vs. Ontology: An ontology is a set of distinct objects resulting from analysis of a domain. A taxonomy is a particular arrangement of the elements of an ontology into a tree-like class-inclusion structure. The word taxonomy is normally used for the hypernymy relation where the concepts motivating the relation are consistent (e.g., poodle → dog), distinguishing it from other related but different subclass relations (e.g., hound → dog, mutt → dog, puppy → dog).
  • Meronymy: Part-Whole relationships. A meronym is a part of something; the whole is called a holonym. For example, wheel is a meronym of car, and car is a holonym of wheel. A leg is a meronym of a chair. This relationship captures how objects, concepts, and entities are composed of constituent parts.

2.3.4 Connotation: The Emotional Color

Beyond the literal meaning (denotation), words carry emotional, cultural, or social baggage (connotation). This is the foundation of sentiment analysis.

Research (Osgood et al., 1957) suggests that affective meaning varies along three primary dimensions:

  • Valence: Pleasantness (Happy vs. Sad).
  • Arousal: Intensity of emotion (Excited vs. Calm).
  • Dominance: Degree of control (Powerful vs. Weak).

2.3.5 Vector Space Properties

When words are represented as vectors, these lexical relationships emerge geometrically:

  • Synonyms and similar words (e.g., happy and joyful) have vectors that lie close to each other in the space.
  • Antonyms (e.g., hot and cold) occupy opposite sides of a semantic axis.
  • Words sharing a topic (e.g., surgeon, nurse, hospital, anesthetic) cluster together in the same semantic field.
  • Analogies can sometimes be captured by vector arithmetic: the classic example is \(\vec{\text{king}} - \vec{\text{man}} + \vec{\text{woman}} \approx \vec{\text{queen}}\).
Key Concept:

A semantic field (or semantic space) is an attempt to capture an integrated, holistic relationship among entire sets of words from a single domain. While relations like synonymy, antonymy, and hypernymy are binary relations between two senses, a semantic field captures how groups of words are defined with respect to a coherent chunk of common-sense background knowledge.

For example, consider the set {reservation, flight, travel, buy, price, cost, fare, rates, meal, plane}. We could assert individual lexical relations between many of these words, but that wouldn’t add up to a complete account. They are all defined with respect to a shared understanding of air travel. This kind of background knowledge has been studied under various frameworks known as a frame (Fillmore, 1985), a model (Johnson-Laird, 1983), or a script (Schank and Abelson, 1977).

The FrameNet project (Baker et al., 1998) is a major computational resource that encodes this frame knowledge. In FrameNet, each word in a frame is defined with respect to the frame and shares aspects of meaning with other words in that frame. A frame is a script-like structure that instantiates a set of frame-specific semantic roles called frame elements, and each word evokes a frame while profiling some aspect of it. Frames can inherit from each other, and generalizations among frame elements are captured by inheritance. For instance, the Change position on a scale frame covers words like rise, fall, increase, decrease, climb, plummet, soar, swing, fluctuate, drop, gain, dwindle, triple — all verbs (and their corresponding nouns) that indicate the change of an Item’s position on a scale (the Attribute) from a starting point to an end point.

In the vector space, these semantic fields manifest as clusters: medical terms occupy one region, culinary terms another, air-travel terms another, and so on. The distance between vectors in this space quantifies semantic (dis)similarity.

The figure below conceptually illustrates how word vectors are distributed in a 2D projection:

Visualization intuition (t-SNE plot):

  • Positive sentiment words (good, excellent, wonderful, amazing) cluster together.
  • Negative sentiment words (bad, terrible, worst, dislike) form a separate cluster, far from the positive cluster.
  • Prepositions and function words form their own grouping.
Even though the machine was never taught dictionary definitions, it learns these groupings purely from distributional statistics in the training corpus.

Sparse Vector Representations: Counting Words

2.4 Overview of Representation Approaches

There are two broad families of techniques for generating word vector representations:

  1. Frequency-based (Sparse) approaches — Vectors are built by counting word occurrences in documents or context windows. These include:

    • Term Frequency (TF)
    • TF-IDF (Term Frequency – Inverse Document Frequency)
    • PPMI (Positive Pointwise Mutual Information)
    They are simple, computationally inexpensive, and can run on CPUs or edge devices. However, they produce long, sparse vectors and cannot capture nuanced contextual distinctions (e.g., bank as a financial institution vs. river bank gets the same vector).

  2. Prediction-based (Dense) approaches — Vectors are learned by training a neural network to predict words from their contexts (or vice versa). These include:

    • Word2Vec (Skip-gram, CBOW)
    • GloVe (Global Vectors)
    • Contextual embeddings (BERT, ELMo, GPT)
    They produce short, dense vectors where most values are non-zero, and can capture richer semantic relationships.

This lecture focuses primarily on frequency-based approaches, especially TF-IDF.

2.5 Term Frequency: The Simplest Representation

2.5.1 Document-Level Term Frequency

Consider a toy corpus of four Shakespeare plays. We can construct a term-document matrix where rows correspond to terms (words) and columns to documents. Each cell \((t, d)\) contains the raw count of term \(t\) in document \(d\).

Table: Term-document matrix: raw counts of each word in each document.
Term As You Like It Twelfth Night Julius Caesar Henry V
battle 1 0 7 13
good 114 80 62 89
fool 36 58 1 4
wit 20 15 2 3
Key Concept:

Term Frequency \(\text{TF}(t, d)\) is the number of times term \(t\) appears in document \(d\). It is the simplest measure of how important a word is to a document.

2.5.2 Using Vectors to Compare Documents

From Table 2.1, each document can be viewed as a vector over the vocabulary. For instance, using only two dimensions (battle and fool):

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec{d}_{\text{Julius Caesar}} &= (7,\; 1) \\ \vec{d}_{\text{Henry V}} &= (13,\; 4) \\ \vec{d}_{\text{As You Like It}} &= (1,\; 36) \\ \vec{d}_{\text{Twelfth Night}} &= (0,\; 58) \end{aligned} \]

By plotting these points, we immediately see that Julius Caesar and Henry V (both histories/tragedies about war) lie close to each other, while As You Like It and Twelfth Night (both comedies) form a separate close cluster. The angle between document vectors reveals their thematic similarity—all from simple word counts!

2.5.3 From Documents to Words

We can also flip the perspective: each word becomes a vector across documents. For example:

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec{v}(\texttt{battle}) &= (1,\; 0,\; 7,\; 13) \\ \vec{v}(\texttt{fool}) &= (36,\; 58,\; 1,\; 4) \\ \vec{v}(\texttt{wit}) &= (20,\; 15,\; 2,\; 3) \end{aligned} \]

When we compare these word vectors, fool and wit are closer to each other than either is to battle, suggesting a semantic relationship. This is the essence of frequency-based vector semantics.

Note:

The simple term frequency approach gives us a first approximation of word meaning purely from counting. It reveals some relation exists among words that co-occur in similar documents, but it does not yet tell us how much they are related, nor does it account for the fact that common words (like the) appear everywhere and are not particularly meaningful.

Co-occurrence and Context Windows

2.6 Beyond Documents: Local Context

Instead of counting how often a word appears in the same document as another word, we can count how often words appear near each other within a fixed-size context window. This captures a more fine-grained notion of distributional similarity.

Key Concept:

A context window of size \(\pm k\) around a target word \(w\) includes the \(k\) words to the left and the \(k\) words to the right of \(w\) in the text. The word and its neighboring words within this window are said to co-occur.

2.6.1 Why \(\pm 4\) or \(\pm 5\)?

The typical English sentence averages 8–10 words in length. A context window of \(\pm 4\) or \(\pm 5\) words captures most of the within-sentence context without crossing sentence boundaries too often. Larger windows capture more topical similarity (words about the same subject), while smaller windows capture more functional/syntactic similarity (words that play similar grammatical roles).

Example:

Constructing a co-occurrence matrix:

Consider the following short corpus of sentences:

  1. The cherry pie is a traditional dessert.
  2. Strawberry pie is also delicious.
  3. Digital computers process information quickly.
  4. Information retrieval uses digital techniques.

For each target word (e.g., cherry, strawberry, digital, information), we count how many times every other word in the vocabulary appears within a \(\pm 4\) window across the entire corpus.

For cherry:

  • pie appears within \(\pm 4\) of cherry — count: 442 (from the full corpus, not just these four sentences)

  • sugar appears within \(\pm 4\) of cherry — count: 25

  • computer appears within \(\pm 4\) of cherry — count: 2 (rare)

  • data appears within \(\pm 4\) of cherry — count: 8

Similarly for digital:

  • computer — count: 1670
  • data — count: 1683
  • pie — count: 5 (rare)

The resulting word-word co-occurrence matrix has dimensions \(|V| \times |V|\) (vocabulary size squared). Each cell \((i, j)\) records how many times word \(j\) appeared within the context window of word \(i\).

Note:

Unlike the document-based approach, the co-occurrence matrix captures local syntactic and semantic relationships. Words like cherry and strawberry will have high co-occurrence counts with similar context words (pie, sweet, fruit, delicious) and therefore will have similar vector representations.

2.6.2 Counting in Both Directions

The context window is symmetric: \(\pm 4\) means we count words appearing up to 4 positions to the left AND up to 4 positions to the right. If pie appears once on the left of cherry and once on the right, it counts as two separate co-occurrences.

Measuring Similarity: Cosine Similarity

2.7 Why Not Just Dot Product?

Once words are represented as vectors, we need a way to measure how "close" two vectors are. The natural first candidate is the dot product (inner product):

\[ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \, b_i \]

The dot product gives us a scalar value related to both the direction and the magnitudes (lengths) of the two vectors. However, raw dot product is scale-sensitive: a longer vector will produce a larger dot product even if it points in the same direction as a shorter one. This makes dot product unreliable for comparing vectors of different lengths.

Example:

Consider two vectors: \[ \vec{v} = (1, 2), \quad \vec{w} = (3, 4) \] Dot product: \(\vec{v} \cdot \vec{w} = 1 \times 3 + 2 \times 4 = 3 + 8 = 11\).

Now consider a longer vector in the same direction as \(\vec{v}\): \[ \vec{v}' = (10, 20) \] Dot product with \(\vec{w}\): \(10 \times 3 + 20 \times 4 = 30 + 80 = 110\).

The dot product changed from 11 to 110 even though the direction of \(\vec{v}\) and \(\vec{v}'\) is identical. This shows why raw dot product is problematic for measuring semantic similarity.

2.8 Cosine Similarity: Normalizing by Length

To address the scale issue, we normalize the dot product by the lengths (Euclidean norms) of both vectors. This yields the cosine similarity:

Key Concept:

Cosine Similarity measures the cosine of the angle \(\theta\) between two vectors. It is defined as:

\[ \boxed{\text{cosine}(\vec{a}, \vec{b}) = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{\|\vec{a}\| \; \|\vec{b}\|} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i^2} \; \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} b_i^2}}} \]

where \(\|\vec{a}\| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i^2}\) is the Euclidean norm (length) of vector \(\vec{a}\).

2.8.1 Interpreting Cosine Similarity Values

  • \(\text{cosine} \approx +1\): The vectors point in nearly the same direction → highly similar (angle close to \(0^\circ\)).

  • \(\text{cosine} \approx 0\): The vectors are nearly orthogonal → unrelated (angle close to \(90^\circ\)).

  • \(\text{cosine} \approx -1\): The vectors point in opposite directions → opposite meaning (angle close to \(180^\circ\)).

Caution:

In frequency-based vector representations (like TF and TF-IDF), all count values are non-negative, so cosine similarity will always fall in the range \([0, 1]\). Negative values can arise in prediction-based dense embeddings (e.g., Word2Vec, GloVe) where vector components can be negative learned weights.

2.8.2 Worked Example: Cosine Similarity

Example:

Comparing cherry and information using their co-occurrence vectors:

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec{v}(\texttt{cherry}) &= (442,\; 8,\; 2) \quad \text{(co-occurrences with pie, sugar, computer)} \\ \vec{v}(\texttt{information}) &= (5,\; 3982,\; 3325) \end{aligned} \]

Numerator (dot product): \[ 442 \times 5 \;+\; 8 \times 3982 \;+\; 2 \times 3325 = 2210 + 31856 + 6650 = 40{,}716 \]

Denominator (product of norms): \[ \begin{aligned} \|\vec{v}(\texttt{cherry})\| &= \sqrt{442^2 + 8^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{195{,}364 + 64 + 4} = \sqrt{195{,}432} \approx 442.08 \\[4pt] \|\vec{v}(\texttt{information})\| &= \sqrt{5^2 + 3982^2 + 3325^2} = \sqrt{25 + 15{,}856{,}324 + 11{,}055{,}625} \approx 5187.77 \end{aligned} \]

Cosine similarity: \[ \text{cosine}(\texttt{cherry}, \texttt{information}) = \frac{40{,}716}{442.08 \times 5187.77} \approx 0.018 \]

This is very close to 0, confirming cherry and information are unrelated.

Now compare digital and information: \[ \vec{v}(\texttt{digital}) = (5,\; 1683,\; 1670) \]

Repeating the calculation yields a cosine similarity of approximately 0.996, very close to 1. These words are highly related.

2.9 Visualizing with Vector Angles

When word vectors are plotted in a 2D projection of the semantic space:

  • Small angle between vectors → high cosine similarity → words are semantically related.

  • Large angle (near \(90^\circ\)) → low cosine similarity → words are unrelated.

This geometric intuition is why representing words as vectors is so powerful: we can use the rich toolkit of linear algebra to quantify semantic relationships that would otherwise require human judgment.

TF-IDF: Term Frequency – Inverse Document Frequency

2.10 The Problem with Raw Term Frequency

Using raw term frequency has a critical flaw: it overvalues common words. Words like the, is, and, of appear frequently in almost every document but carry very little semantic information. A word appearing 100 times in a document is not necessarily 100 times more important than a word appearing once.

Furthermore, a word that appears in every document provides no discriminatory power—it cannot help us distinguish between documents or understand what makes a particular document unique.

Key Concept:

TF-IDF addresses this by combining two factors:

  1. Term Frequency (TF): How often does the term appear in this document? (Local importance)
  2. Inverse Document Frequency (IDF): How rare is the term across all documents? (Global importance)
A term is important to a document if it appears frequently in that document but rarely in the collection as a whole.

2.11 Term Frequency (TF) - Formal Definition

The raw term frequency is simply \(\text{count}(t, d)\) — the number of times term \(t\) occurs in document \(d\). However, to squash extreme values (some words may appear thousands of times) and to make computations faster, we typically apply a logarithmic scaling:

\[ \boxed{\text{TF}(t, d) = \log_{10}\big(\text{count}(t, d) + 1\big)} \]
Note:

The \(+1\) inside the logarithm serves as Laplace smoothing: it ensures that even when \(\text{count}(t, d) = 0\), we get \(\log_{10}(1) = 0\) rather than \(\log_{10}(0)\) which is undefined. This avoids zero-count issues and provides a small non-zero floor for unseen terms. This is analogous to the additive smoothing used in Naïve Bayes classifiers.

Example:

For the word wit in document \(D_1\) (As You Like It), \(\text{count}(\texttt{wit}, D_1) = 20\): \[ \text{TF}(\texttt{wit}, D_1) = \log_{10}(20 + 1) = \log_{10}(21) \approx 1.322 \]

Without the log, the value would be 20. The log squashes it to a more manageable scale.

2.12 Document Frequency (DF) and Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

2.12.1 Document Frequency

Key Concept:

Document Frequency \(\text{DF}(t)\) is the number of documents in the collection that contain term \(t\) at least once. It is a binary per-document check: is the term present or absent? It does not count how many times the term appears within each document.

Caution:

Do not confuse Document Frequency (DF) with Collection Frequency (CF):

  • \(\text{DF}(t)\) = number of documents containing \(t\) (binary per-document check).

  • \(\text{CF}(t)\) = total number of occurrences of \(t\) across all documents = \(\sum_d \text{count}(t, d)\).

For example, the word Romeo might appear only in 1 document (DF = 1) but appear 113 times in that document (CF = 113). We use DF in IDF, not CF.

2.12.2 Inverse Document Frequency

The Inverse Document Frequency gives higher weight to rare terms and lower weight to common terms:

\[ \boxed{\text{IDF}(t) = \log_{10}\left(\frac{N}{\text{DF}(t)}\right)} \]

where \(N\) is the total number of documents in the collection.

Example:

IDF calculation for a toy collection of \(N = 37\) documents:

Term DF\((t)\) \(\dfrac{N}{\text{DF}(t)}\) IDF\((t)\) Interpretation
Romeo 1 \(37/1 = 37\) \(\log_{10}(37) \approx 1.57\) Very rare → high IDF
salad 2 \(37/2 = 18.5\) \(\log_{10}(18.5) \approx 1.27\) Rare
Falstaff 4 \(37/4 = 9.25\) \(\log_{10}(9.25) \approx 0.97\) Moderately common
good 37 \(37/37 = 1\) \(\log_{10}(1) = \mathbf{0}\) Appears in ALL docs → zero weight

The word good appears in every document (\(\text{DF} = N = 37\)), so its IDF is \(\log_{10}(1) = 0\). This means good contributes nothing to the TF-IDF score, regardless of its high term frequency. This is exactly what we want: ubiquitous words should not influence similarity or ranking.

2.13 TF-IDF: The Combined Weight

The final TF-IDF weight is the product of the two components:

\[ \boxed{\text{TF-IDF}(t, d) = \text{TF}(t, d) \;\times\; \text{IDF}(t)} \]

Explicitly: \[ \text{TF-IDF}(t, d) = \log_{10}\big(\text{count}(t, d) + 1\big) \;\times\; \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{N}{\text{DF}(t)}\right) \]

Key Concept:

TF-IDF assigns a high weight to a term \(t\) in document \(d\) when:

  1. \(t\) appears many times in \(d\) (high TF), AND

  2. \(t\) appears in few documents overall (high IDF).

This balances local prominence with global rarity.

2.13.1 Worked Example: Full TF-IDF

Example:

Computing TF-IDF for wit in document \(D_1\) (As You Like It), with \(N = 37\):

Given: \(\text{count}(\texttt{wit}, D_1) = 20\), and \(\texttt{wit}\) appears in 34 out of 37 documents.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{TF}(\texttt{wit}, D_1) &= \log_{10}(20 + 1) = \log_{10}(21) \approx 1.322 \\[4pt] \text{IDF}(\texttt{wit}) &= \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{37}{34}\right) \approx \log_{10}(1.088) \approx 0.037 \\[4pt] \text{TF-IDF}(\texttt{wit}, D_1) &= 1.322 \times 0.037 \approx 0.049 \end{aligned} \]

The low TF-IDF value reflects that wit is fairly common across the collection (34/37 docs). A rarer word like battle would receive a higher IDF and thus a higher TF-IDF score in documents where it appears frequently.

For good: Even though \(\text{count}(\texttt{good}, D_1) = 114\), since \(\text{DF}(\texttt{good}) = 37\): \[ \text{IDF}(\texttt{good}) = \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{37}{37}\right) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{TF-IDF}(\texttt{good}, D_1) = 0 \] This demonstrates how IDF zeroes out the contribution of stopwords and overly common terms, regardless of their raw frequency.

2.14 TF-IDF Vectors

For a vocabulary of size \(|V|\) and a collection of \(N\) documents, each word \(t\) is represented by an \(N\)-dimensional TF-IDF vector:

\[ \vec{v}_{\text{TF-IDF}}(t) = \big(\text{TF-IDF}(t, D_1),\;\; \text{TF-IDF}(t, D_2),\;\; \ldots,\;\; \text{TF-IDF}(t, D_N)\big) \]

Similarly, each document \(d\) can be represented by a \(|V|\)-dimensional TF-IDF vector. Cosine similarity can then be applied to these vectors to find similar words or similar documents.

Note:

TF-IDF vectors are still sparse—most entries are zero or near-zero because any given word appears in only a small fraction of all documents. However, they are far more informative than raw count vectors because the IDF weighting suppresses noise from common words and amplifies the signal from discriminative rare words.

Applications and Practical Intuition

2.15 Document Ranking in Search Engines

One of the most important historical applications of TF-IDF is in information retrieval (search engines). When a user types a query:

  1. The query is treated as a short pseudo-document and converted to a TF-IDF vector.
  2. Each document in the collection also has a pre-computed TF-IDF vector.
  3. Cosine similarity is computed between the query vector and each document vector.
  4. Documents are ranked by descending similarity score.
Example:

Search ranking example:

User searches for "battle" in the Shakespeare collection. The TF-IDF weights for battle in each document are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{TF-IDF}(\texttt{battle}, \text{Henry V}) &\quad\text{— highest} \\ \text{TF-IDF}(\texttt{battle}, \text{Julius Caesar}) &\quad\text{— second} \\ \text{TF-IDF}(\texttt{battle}, \text{As You Like It})&\quad\text{— third} \\ \text{TF-IDF}(\texttt{battle}, \text{Twelfth Night}) &\quad\text{— last (TF = 0)} \end{aligned} \]

Henry V, which contains the most occurrences of battle, ranks highest. This is how search engines decide the order of results. Modern search systems (Google, Bing) still use TF-IDF (or its descendant BM25) as one of many ranking signals, alongside PageRank, neural embeddings, user behavior signals, and more.

2.16 TF-IDF in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

In modern RAG systems (used by ChatGPT, Copilot, and other AI assistants):

  1. A user query is embedded into a vector.
  2. The system retrieves the most relevant document chunks from a knowledge base using similarity search (often employing TF-IDF, BM25, or dense embeddings).
  3. The retrieved context is fed into an LLM to generate a grounded response.

TF-IDF and its variants remain popular in the retrieval step because they are fast, interpretable, and require no GPU training.

2.17 BM25: The Modern Successor to TF-IDF

BM25 (Best Matching 25) is a refined version of TF-IDF widely used in modern search and RAG systems. It introduces:

  • Term saturation: diminishing returns for repeated term occurrences (a word appearing 100 times isn't 100× as important as appearing once).
  • Document length normalization: longer documents are penalized slightly, since a term appearing 5 times in a 100-word doc is more significant than 5 times in a 10,000-word doc.

Limitations of Sparse Vectors and the Road Ahead

2.18 Two Fundamental Problems

Despite their simplicity and effectiveness, TF-IDF vectors have significant limitations:

2.18.1 Long Vectors (The "Curse of Dimensionality")

If the vocabulary size is \(|V|\) (number of unique words), then each word's vector has dimension \(|V|\) (in the co-occurrence approach) or dimension \(N\) (in the document-based approach, where \(N\) is the number of documents).

Example:

At web scale:

  • Vocabulary size \(|V|\) can reach 1 billion unique words/tokens.

  • Each word vector would have 1 billion dimensions.
  • Storing, searching, and computing cosine similarity over billion-dimensional vectors is computationally prohibitive.

2.18.2 Sparsity (Most Values Are Zero)

In a large corpus, any given word co-occurs with only a tiny fraction of the total vocabulary. The resulting vector is overwhelmingly filled with zeros. These zeros:

  • Waste storage space.
  • Waste computation time (multiplying and adding zeros during dot products).
  • Provide no useful semantic information.

Key Concept:

A sparse vector is one where the vast majority of entries are zero. TF-IDF vectors are inherently sparse because most words do not appear in most documents, and most word pairs never co-occur within a context window.

2.19 The Need for Dense Embeddings

These challenges motivated the development of dense vector representations, where:

  • Vectors have a fixed, relatively small dimension \(d\) (e.g., 50, 100, 300, 768) regardless of vocabulary size.

  • Most entries are non-zero, making the vectors "dense."
  • The values are learned by training a neural network rather than simply counted.

Table: Comparison of sparse vs. dense vector representations.
Property Sparse (TF-IDF) Dense (Word2Vec/GloVe)

2.20 The Big Idea: Prediction Instead of Counting

The revolutionary approach, popularized by models like Word2Vec (Mikolov et al., 2013), shifts from counting co-occurrences to predicting them. This is called self-supervised learning:

Key Concept: Self-Supervised Learning

We train a classifier on a "fake" prediction task. We don’t actually care about the task itself, but we use the internal weights learned by the classifier as the word embeddings. The "supervision" comes from the data itself — the fact that two words appear together in the corpus provides the training signal. No human labeling is required.

2.20.1 Word2Vec Skip-Gram: The Prediction Task

The Skip-Gram model trains on a simple task: Given a target word, predict its neighbors.

Corpus: "...the quick brown fox jumps over..."

Training Strategy (Simplified):

  • Positive Examples: Treat actual neighboring words as correct predictions. (e.g., Target: "fox", Context: "brown" → Positive).
  • Negative Examples: Randomly sample words from the vocabulary that are NOT neighbors. (e.g., Target: "fox", Context: "keyboard" → Negative).
  • Training: Train a classifier (e.g., logistic regression) to distinguish between positive and negative pairs.

During this training process, the model adjusts its internal weight matrix (the embeddings) so that words appearing in similar contexts (like "fox" and "cat") end up with similar weights, enabling the classifier to perform the task effectively.

2.20.2 Characteristics of Dense Vectors

  • Low-Dimensional: Typically 50–300 dimensions (instead of 50,000).
  • Dense: Most elements are non-zero real numbers.
  • Learned Representation: The values are learned through a training process, rather than counted explicitly.
TypeExample Vector (Conceptual)
Sparse (TF-IDF)[0, 0, 5.2, 0, ..., 0, 1.1, 0, ...] (Length 50,000)
Dense (Word2Vec)[0.25, -0.11, 0.67, ..., 0.03] (Length 300)

2.20.3 The Mechanics: How Embeddings Work

The Baseline: One-Hot Encoding

Before embeddings, words were represented using One-Hot Encoding. If the vocabulary size \(V = 10{,}000\):

  • Each word is a vector of length 10,000.
  • It contains a ‘1’ at the index corresponding to the word, and ‘0’s everywhere else.
\[ \vec{v}(\texttt{king}) = [\,0,\; 0,\; ...\;, 1,\; ...\;, 0,\; 0\,] \]

The Embedding Matrix

The core of a dense embedding model is the Embedding Matrix \(W\):

  • Rows: Equal to the vocabulary size \(V\).
  • Columns: Equal to the desired embedding dimension \(D\) (e.g., 300).
  • Values: The learned weights (initialized randomly, then adjusted during training).

The "Lookup Trick"

To get the embedding for a word, we multiply its One-Hot vector by the Embedding Matrix \(W\):

\[ \text{Embedding}_K = \text{OneHot}_K \times W \]

Because the One-Hot vector is all zeros except for a single ‘1’ at index \(K\), this matrix multiplication effectively selects the \(K\)-th row of the matrix \(W\). This means the rows of the weight matrix are the word embeddings. Training the model (adjusting the weights) is the process of learning the embeddings.

2.21 A Glimpse Ahead: What's Coming

The next lecture will introduce dense word embeddings, specifically:

  • Word2Vec — the Skip-gram and CBOW architectures that learn embeddings by predicting context words.
  • GloVe (Global Vectors) — combines count-based matrix factorization with prediction-based learning.
  • The concept of contextual embeddings (BERT, GPT) where a word's vector depends on its surrounding sentence, solving the polysemy problem (e.g., bank as river bank vs. financial bank gets different vectors in different contexts).
Key Concept:

In dense embeddings like Word2Vec, the vector components can be negative, which means cosine similarity can range from \(-1\) to \(+1\), enabling the representation of antonymy (opposite meanings) as vectors pointing in opposite directions.

Practical Implementation with Scikit-learn

2.22 CountVectorizer and TfidfVectorizer

The scikit-learn library in Python provides ready-to-use implementations of both count-based and TF-IDF vectorization, making it extremely simple to generate these representations.

2.22.1 CountVectorizer - Raw Term Frequencies

CountVectorizer converts a collection of text documents into a matrix of token counts:

Listing: Using CountVectorizer for raw term frequency

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer

corpus = [
    "The cherry pie is a traditional dessert",
    "Strawberry pie is also delicious",
    "Digital computers process information",
    "Information retrieval systems are digital"
]

vectorizer = CountVectorizer()
X = vectorizer.fit_transform(corpus)

# X is a sparse matrix: rows = documents, columns = vocabulary
print(vectorizer.get_feature_names_out())
# ['also', 'are', 'cherry', 'computers', 'delicious', ...]

print(X.toarray())
# Each row shows how many times each word appears in that document

The output is a sparse matrix where most entries are zero—exactly the challenge we discussed.

2.22.2 TfidfVectorizer - TF-IDF Weights

TfidfVectorizer computes the TF-IDF scores automatically:

Listing: Using TfidfVectorizer for TF-IDF computation

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

corpus = [
    "The cherry pie is a traditional dessert",
    "Strawberry pie is also delicious",
    "Digital computers process information",
    "Information retrieval systems are digital"
]

tfidf = TfidfVectorizer(
    norm='l2',            # L2 normalization of vectors
    use_idf=True,         # Use IDF weighting
    smooth_idf=True,      # Add smoothing to avoid division by zero
    sublinear_tf=True     # Use 1 + log(tf) instead of raw tf
)
X_tfidf = tfidf.fit_transform(corpus)

print(X_tfidf.toarray())
# Each row now contains TF-IDF weighted values (decimal numbers)
Note:

The key parameters of TfidfVectorizer:

  • norm='l2': Each document vector is normalized to unit length, so cosine similarity reduces to a simple dot product.
  • use_idf=True: Enables IDF weighting; setting to False reduces it to pure term frequency.
  • smooth_idf=True: Adds 1 to document frequencies to prevent zero divisions (additive smoothing).

  • sublinear_tf=True: Applies \(1 + \log(\text{tf})\) scaling to dampen the effect of very high term frequencies.

2.23 Effect of Preprocessing

Stemming and lemmatization significantly affect the resulting vectors. Without stemming, tradition, traditional, and traditionally are treated as three separate tokens, each with lower frequencies. After stemming to tradition, their counts merge, giving a more accurate frequency. This is why text normalization (covered in Lecture 1) is a crucial preprocessing step before vectorization.

Summary and Key Takeaways

  1. Why vectors? Machines process numbers, not strings. Representing words as vectors enables mathematical comparison of semantic meaning. Word embeddings are the universal input to all modern NLP systems—from search engines to LLMs to agentic AI.
  2. Distributional Hypothesis: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." Words appearing in similar contexts have similar meanings. This is the theoretical foundation of all vector semantics.

  3. Two families of representations:

    • Sparse (frequency-based): Count occurrences directly. Simple, CPU-friendly, but produces long, sparse vectors. Example: TF-IDF.
    • Dense (prediction-based): Learn embeddings via neural networks. Richer semantics, fixed small dimension, but requires training. Examples: Word2Vec, GloVe, BERT.

  4. Term Frequency (TF): \(\text{TF}(t, d) = \log_{10}(\text{count}(t, d) + 1)\). Measures how often a term appears in a specific document. The log squashes extreme values; the \(+1\) provides smoothing.

  5. Inverse Document Frequency (IDF): \(\text{IDF}(t) = \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{N}{\text{DF}(t)}\right)\). Gives high weight to rare terms and zero weight to terms appearing in all documents. DF (document frequency) counts how many documents contain the term—not how many total occurrences.

  6. TF-IDF: \(\text{TF-IDF}(t,d) = \text{TF}(t,d) \times \text{IDF}(t)\). Balances local frequency with global rarity. A word is important if it appears often in this document but rarely in the overall collection.

  7. Cosine Similarity: \(\displaystyle \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{\|\vec{a}\| \, \|\vec{b}\|}\). Measures the cosine of the angle between vectors. Range \([0, 1]\) for non-negative vectors, \([-1, 1]\) for dense embeddings. Values near 1 indicate high similarity; values near 0 indicate unrelatedness.

  8. Context Windows: Co-occurrence is measured within a symmetric window (typically \(\pm 4\) or \(\pm 5\) words) around each target word. This captures local syntactic and semantic relationships.

  9. Limitations of sparse vectors: Vocabulary-sized dimensions (curse of dimensionality) and extreme sparsity (\(>99\%\) zeros) make these representations impractical for large-scale NLP, motivating dense embeddings.

  10. TF-IDF is still widely used: In search engines (as one ranking signal), in RAG systems for document retrieval, and anywhere computational efficiency is critical. Its descendant, BM25, is the industry standard for lexical retrieval.
Key Concept:

The Big Picture: Word embeddings transform discrete, symbolic language into continuous, differentiable vector spaces where semantic relationships become geometric relationships. This bridge between language and mathematics is what makes all modern NLP—from simple text classification to GPT-4—possible.
