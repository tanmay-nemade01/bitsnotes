8.2 Foundations of NLP and Evaluation Metrics

8.2.1 Scope, Objectives, and Real-World Applications

Hook: How can a computer—a machine built solely to execute binary arithmetic—understand whether "I saw the man with the telescope" means the man had the telescope or the observer used it?

Intuition: Natural Language Processing (NLP) acts as a mathematical translator between human language and computational algorithms. It transforms messy, ambiguous human text into structured numerical representations that algorithms can reason about, classify, and generate.

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is an interdisciplinary field at the intersection of computer science, artificial intelligence, and computational linguistics. Its primary objective is to enable computational systems to understand, interpret, analyze, and generate human language in a semantically meaningful and contextually appropriate manner.

Real-World Application: Enterprise NLP applications span automated machine translation (Google Translate, DeepL), conversational AI and virtual assistants (ChatGPT, Claude, Siri, Alexa), sentiment analysis for real-time brand monitoring, clinical information extraction from medical records, automated document summarization, coreference resolution, and semantic search engines.

8.2.2 Challenges and Multi-Level Ambiguity in Human Language

Human language is inherently complex, non-deterministic, context-dependent, and evolutionarily dynamic. What makes NLP an extraordinarily challenging computational problem is the presence of pervasive ambiguity across six distinct linguistic levels:

1. Phonetic and Phonological Ambiguity: Ambiguity in speech sound perception and acoustic word boundary segmentation. - Example: "recognize speech" vs. "wreck a nice beach". 2. Morphological Ambiguity: Ambiguity in word structure and component morphemes (stems, prefixes, suffixes). - Example: "unlockable" can mean "unable to be locked" (\(\text{un-} + [\text{lock} + \text{able}]\)) or "able to be unlocked" ([\text{un} + \text{lock}] + \text{able}). 3. Lexical Ambiguity: A single word possessing multiple semantic senses or part-of-speech roles depending on context. - Example: "bank" (financial institution vs. river bank); "duck" (noun referring to a bird vs. verb meaning to lower one's head). 4. Syntactic (Structural) Ambiguity: A single sentence admitting multiple plausible grammatical parse trees. - Example: "I saw the man with the telescope" (PP-attachment ambiguity: did the man have the telescope, or was the telescope used to see him?). 5. Semantic Ambiguity: Multiple distinct interpretations for a syntactically unambiguous sentence structure. - Example: "The chicken is ready to eat" (is the chicken about to consume food, or is the cooked chicken ready for human consumption?). 6. Pragmatic and Discourse Ambiguity: Meaning dependent on real-world context, speaker intent, shared cultural knowledge, or multi-sentence discourse flow. - Example: "Can you pass the salt?" is pragmatically a polite request for action, not a literal inquiry about physical ability.

Common Trap: Assuming that semantic analysis alone can resolve sentence intent. Without pragmatic context (speaker intent, situational awareness, and discourse history), literal semantic parsing frequently fails on indirect speech acts, sarcasm, and idioms.

8.2.3 Hierarchy of Language Understanding Levels

Classical and modern NLP architectures process text across a formal hierarchy of linguistic analysis, moving from low-level acoustic/textual signals to high-level intent:

[Phonetics / Phonology] -> [Morphology] -> [Syntax] -> [Semantics] -> [Pragmatics] -> [Discourse]
  • Phonetics / Phonology: Raw acoustic signal processing and speech sound categorization.
  • Morphology: Breakdown of tokens into constituent root morphemes, prefixes, and inflectional endings.
  • Syntax: Sentence structure analysis, constituency/dependency parsing, and Part-of-Speech (POS) tagging.
  • Semantics: Literal word-level and composition-level meaning extraction.
  • Pragmatics: Context-driven intent interpretation beyond literal meaning.
  • Discourse: Multi-sentence contextual linking, coreference resolution, and document-level coherence.

Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) perform joint implicit processing across all levels up to the discourse level, using self-attention mechanisms to resolve complex coreferences and maintain pragmatic context over long context windows.

8.2.4 Architecture of Classical, Modern, and Hybrid NLP Systems

NLP system architectures have evolved across three primary paradigms:

1. Classical Rule-Based & Statistical Pipelines: Rely on hand-crafted linguistic rules, regular expressions, deterministic parsers, and statistical models (e.g., N-gram models, HMMs, TF-IDF). - Strengths: Computationally lightweight, highly deterministic, fully interpretable, zero cold-start latency. - Weaknesses: Brittle to out-of-vocabulary terms, fragile rule interactions, incapable of handling complex deep semantics. 2. Modern Deep Neural & LLM Architectures: Utilize deep Transformer neural networks trained on massive web-scale corpora end-to-end. - Strengths: Exceptional semantic reasoning, robust zero-shot generalization, fluid multilingual capabilities. - Weaknesses: High computational cost, GPU memory demands, latency overhead, hallucination risks. 3. Hybrid NLP Architectures: Synthesize classical pipelines with neural LLMs. - Design Pattern: Lightweight classical modules execute high-speed initial filtering, rule-based entity extraction, and deterministic validation; Transformer LLMs perform deep semantic reasoning; classical guardrails enforce strict domain and safety constraints.

Analogy: A hybrid NLP pipeline operates like a modern medical triage center. The classical pipeline acts as the front-desk nurse doing fast, deterministic checks (vitals, temperature), while the neural LLM acts as the specialist doctor performing complex diagnostic reasoning.

8.2.5 Symbol Registry — System Evaluation Metrics

  • \(TP\) — True Positives — Count of correctly predicted positive instances — Non-negative integer
  • \(FP\) — False Positives — Count of negative instances incorrectly predicted as positive — Non-negative integer
  • \(FN\) — False Negatives — Count of positive instances incorrectly predicted as negative — Non-negative integer
  • \(TN\) — True Negatives — Count of correctly predicted negative instances — Non-negative integer
  • \(\text{Precision}\) — Precision score — Ratio of correct positive predictions to total positive predictions — Real scalar in \([0, 1]\)
  • \(\text{Recall}\) — Recall score — Ratio of correct positive predictions to total actual positive instances — Real scalar in \([0, 1]\)
  • \(\text{F1-Score}\) — F1 measure — Harmonic mean of precision and recall — Real scalar in \([0, 1]\)

8.2.6 System Evaluation Metrics and Performance Assessment

Evaluating NLP system performance requires distinguishing between intrinsic metrics (measuring algorithm-specific mathematical properties) and extrinsic metrics (measuring downstream task success).

For classification, text mining, and information retrieval tasks, system performance is evaluated using Precision, Recall, and F1-Score: 1. Precision (\(P\)): The proportion of predicted positive instances that are actually true positives. \[ \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \] 2. Recall (\(R\)): The proportion of actual positive instances that were successfully identified by the model. \[ \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \] 3. F1-Score: The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, which penalizes extreme imbalances between the two metrics: \[ \text{F1-Score} = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} \]

Step-by-Step Derivation of the F1-Score Formula:

Starting from the definition of the harmonic mean of Precision (\(P\)) and Recall (\(R\)): \[ \begin{aligned} \text{F1} &= \frac{2}{\frac{1}{P} + \frac{1}{R}} &\quad \text{(Definition of Harmonic Mean)} \\ &= \frac{2}{\frac{TP+FP}{TP} + \frac{TP+FN}{TP}} &\quad \text{(Substitute } P = \frac{TP}{TP+FP} \text{ and } R = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}\text{)} \\ &= \frac{2}{\frac{(TP+FP) + (TP+FN)}{TP}} &\quad \text{(Combine numerators over common denominator } TP\text{)} \\ &= \frac{2}{\frac{2 \cdot TP + FP + FN}{TP}} &\quad \text{(Simplify numerator sum)} \\ &= \frac{2 \cdot TP}{2 \cdot TP + FP + FN} &\quad \text{(Multiply numerator by reciprocal of denominator)} \end{aligned} \]
Worked Example — Evaluation Metrics Calculation:

Problem Statement: An information extraction system processes a test collection of 100 documents to identify medical entity references. The ground truth contains 100 actual positive entity mentions. The system outputs 100 positive predictions, of which 80 are correct (\(TP = 80\)). As a result, 20 predictions are incorrect (\(FP = 20\)), and 20 actual positive entities were missed (\(FN = 20\)). Compute the system's Precision, Recall, and F1-Score.

Step 1: Calculate Precision (\(P\)) \[ \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} = \frac{80}{80 + 20} = \frac{80}{100} = 0.80 \quad (80.0\%) \] Step 2: Calculate Recall (\(R\)) \[ \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} = \frac{80}{80 + 20} = \frac{80}{100} = 0.80 \quad (80.0\%) \] Step 3: Calculate F1-Score \[ \text{F1-Score} = 2 \cdot \frac{P \cdot R}{P + R} = 2 \cdot \frac{0.80 \cdot 0.80}{0.80 + 0.80} = 2 \cdot \frac{0.64}{1.60} = \frac{1.28}{1.60} = \mathbf{0.80} \quad (80.0\%) \] Using Direct Count Formula: \[ \text{F1-Score} = \frac{2 \cdot TP}{2 \cdot TP + FP + FN} = \frac{2 \cdot 80}{2(80) + 20 + 20} = \frac{160}{160 + 40} = \frac{160}{200} = \mathbf{0.80} \]

Sense Check: When Precision equals Recall (\(P = R = 0.80\)), their harmonic mean must equal their arithmetic mean, confirming \(F1 = 0.80\).

Assumptions & Scope: - Precision and Recall assume binary classification or target entity retrieval. - F1-Score places equal weight on Precision and Recall. In applications where false positives are more dangerous than false negatives (e.g., spam filtering) or vice versa (e.g., cancer detection), weighted \(F_\beta\) scores should be used: \[ F_\beta = (1 + \beta^2) \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{(\beta^2 \cdot \text{Precision}) + \text{Recall}} \] where \(\beta > 1\) weights recall higher and \(\beta < 1\) weights precision higher.
Recap: NLP bridges human text and computational logic across six levels of linguistic hierarchy. Metrics like Precision, Recall, and F1-Score evaluate retrieval accuracy, while intrinsic metrics like Perplexity measure language model quality.

8.3 Vector Semantics and Sparse Frequency-Based Representations

8.3.1 Intuition of Lexical Semantics and Vector Space Embedding

Hook: How can a computer know that "doctor" and "physician" mean the same thing, while "doctor" and "scalpel" are merely related? The answer lies in a single powerful idea: you can deduce what a word means by examining the company it keeps.
Intuition & Analogy: To enable mathematical and computational operations on natural language, words must be mapped from discrete textual symbols into numerical representations. Lexical semantics focuses on capturing word meaning and the semantic relationships between words — synonymy (couch/sofa), antonymy (hot/cold), hypernymy (animal → dog → poodle), and meronymy (wheel is part of car).

The core insight is the distributional hypothesis (J.R. Firth, 1957): "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." Words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings.

Everyday analogy: Think of a city map where every word is a city. If "dog" and "cat" frequently appear in similar sentences (near "pet", "feed", "walk"), they are placed close together on the map. "Computer" would be far away, near "data" and "process". The distance between cities represents the semantic similarity between the words. This analogy breaks down for very high-dimensional spaces (hundreds of dimensions), where human spatial intuition fails, but the geometric principle holds.

Vector semantics implements the distributional hypothesis by embedding each word as a point (vector) in a high-dimensional vector space. Semantically similar words cluster close together in this space. These vectors are called embeddings because the word's meaning is embedded into the coordinate system.

Three compelling reasons to use vectors: 1. Generalization: In a bag-of-words model, "car" and "automobile" are entirely distinct features. With vector embeddings, \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{car}}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{automobile}}\) are nearly identical — learning about one generalizes to the other. 2. Dimensionality reduction: Vectors compress vast contextual information into a manageable fixed-length format. 3. Mathematical operations: Vectors enable the entire toolkit of linear algebra — distances, angles, projections — to measure and manipulate meaning computationally.

8.3.2 Term-Document and Co-Occurrence Matrices

Vector space models construct word representations using frequency counts extracted from a text corpus:

1. Term-Document Matrix: A matrix \(M \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times |D|}\), where rows represent unique vocabulary terms \(v \in V\), columns represent documents \(d \in D\), and cell \(M(i, j)\) contains the frequency of term \(i\) in document \(j\). - Document vector: A column of \(M\), representing a document by the frequencies of all vocabulary terms across it. Used in information retrieval to compute document similarity. - Word vector: A row of \(M\), representing a word by its occurrence pattern across all documents. Two words with similar row vectors tend to be semantically related.

  1. Term Co-Occurrence (Term-Term) Matrix: A symmetric square matrix \(M \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times |V|}\), where cell \(M(i, j)\) records how many times target word \(i\) co-occurs with context word \(j\) within a specified context window of size \(\pm k\) words around the target.
Scope: Both matrix types produce sparse vectors — the vast majority of entries are zero because most word pairs never co-occur. Vector lengths equal vocabulary size \(|V|\) (often 50,000–500,000 dimensions), making storage and distance computation expensive. These limitations motivate the transition to dense representations (Section 8.4).

8.3.3 Symbol Registry — Sparse Representations and Vector Similarity

  • \(V\) — Unique vocabulary set — Set of distinct words in corpus — Size \(|V|\)
  • \(D\) — Document corpus collection — Set of documents — Size \(|D| = N\)
  • \(t\) — Term / Word index — Individual vocabulary element — \(t \in V\)
  • \(d\) — Document index — Individual document element — \(d \in D\)
  • \(\text{count}(t, d)\) — Raw term frequency — Integer occurrence count of term \(t\) in document \(d\) — Non-negative integer \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\)
  • \(\text{TF}(t, d)\) — Log-squashed term frequency — Weighted term frequency — Real scalar \(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\)
  • \(\text{DF}(t)\) — Document frequency — Number of documents containing term \(t\) — Integer \(1 \le \text{DF}(t) \le N\)
  • \(\text{IDF}(t)\) — Inverse document frequency — Rarity weight of term \(t\) across the corpus — Real scalar \(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\)
  • \(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\) — Sparse term/document vectors — Feature vectors in vector space — Vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{|V|}\) or \(\mathbb{R}^{|D|}\)
  • \(\|\mathbf{a}\|_2\) — Euclidean (L2) norm — Length of vector \(\mathbf{a}\) — \(\sqrt{\sum_i a_i^2}\)

8.3.4 Vector Similarity Measures: Dot Product vs. Cosine Similarity

To measure the semantic similarity between two word or document vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), two standard metrics are used:

1. Vector Dot Product: \[ \text{dot}(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \, b_i \]

The dot product multiplies corresponding components of two vectors and sums the results. It measures how much two vectors "agree" — like measuring how closely two judges' scores align. However, the dot product is unnormalized: it scales with vector magnitude, so a long document with many word occurrences will produce a larger dot product against any query simply because its counts are higher, not because it is more relevant.

2. Cosine Similarity (normalized dot product): \[ \cos(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|_2 \; \|\mathbf{b}\|_2} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \, b_i}{\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i^2} \;\; \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} b_i^2}} \]

Cosine similarity divides the dot product by the product of the L2 norms (Euclidean lengths) of both vectors. This normalizes the metric to the range \([0, 1]\) for non-negative count vectors, making it completely independent of document length. It measures the angle between two vectors: vectors pointing in the same direction have cosine similarity near \(1.0\); orthogonal vectors (no shared terms) have cosine similarity of \(0.0\).

Analogy: The dot product is like measuring how much two people agree by counting the total dollar amounts they both bet on the same horse. A billionaire will always produce a larger dot product simply because they bet more money. Cosine similarity corrects for this by measuring the fraction of their portfolio allocated to each horse — two investors with identical portfolio allocations have cosine similarity of 1.0, regardless of wealth.
Pitfall — Dot Product vs. Cosine in Exams: Never use raw dot product for document similarity comparison when documents have different lengths. The raw dot product will always favor longer documents. Always normalize using cosine similarity unless the question explicitly asks for the dot product.

8.3.5 Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) Formulation

Raw term counts over-emphasize extremely frequent words (such as "the", "is", "and") that carry minimal discriminative information. TF-IDF corrects this bias by combining a local term frequency signal with a global inverse document frequency weight.

1. Log-Weighted Term Frequency (TF): \[ \text{TF}(t, d) = \begin{cases} 1 + \log_{10}\!\bigl(\text{count}(t, d)\bigr) & \text{if } \text{count}(t, d) > 0 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Why the log? Without it, a word appearing 100 times would be treated as 100× more important than a word appearing once. The logarithm compresses this dynamic range: \(\log_{10}(100) = 2\), so a 100× increase in raw count becomes only a 2-unit increase in TF. The "+1" ensures that a term appearing at least once contributes a TF of at least 1.0 (since \(\log_{10}(1) = 0\)).

2. Inverse Document Frequency (IDF): \[ \text{IDF}(t) = \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{N}{\text{DF}(t)}\right) \]

where \(N = |D|\) is the total number of documents and \(\text{DF}(t)\) is the number of documents containing term \(t\).

Why IDF? A term appearing in every document (\(\text{DF}(t) = N\)) has \(\text{IDF} = \log_{10}(1) = 0\), contributing zero discriminative weight. A rare term appearing in only 1 out of 10,000 documents has \(\text{IDF} = \log_{10}(10000) = 4\), receiving very high weight. IDF acts as a rarity multiplier — it measures how surprising it is to encounter a term.

3. Combined TF-IDF Weight: \[ \text{TF-IDF}(t, d) = \text{TF}(t, d) \times \text{IDF}(t) \]

A word scores high TF-IDF when it appears frequently in a specific document (high TF) but is rare across the corpus (high IDF). Words that appear everywhere ("the", "is") get zeroed out.

Exam note: Use \(\log_{10}\) for both TF and IDF calculations (the web-scale standard). If you choose to use natural log (\(\ln\)), state your choice explicitly and use it consistently for both TF and IDF. Always write out the TF and IDF formulas before substituting numbers.

8.3.6 Worked Example: TF-IDF and Cosine Similarity Computation

Worked Example — TF-IDF Weight Computation

Problem: Consider a document corpus of \(N = 37\) documents. Compute TF-IDF weights for three terms in Document 1 (\(d_1\)):

Term Raw Count in \(d_1\) Document Frequency \(\text{DF}(t)\)
"good" 5 37
"Romeo" 1 1
"battle" 10 4

Step 1: Compute Log TF Values for \(d_1\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{TF}(\text{"good"}, d_1) &= 1 + \log_{10}(5) = 1 + 0.6990 = 1.6990 \\ \text{TF}(\text{"Romeo"}, d_1) &= 1 + \log_{10}(1) = 1 + 0 = 1.0000 \\ \text{TF}(\text{"battle"}, d_1) &= 1 + \log_{10}(10) = 1 + 1.0000 = 2.0000 \end{aligned} \]

Step 2: Compute IDF Values

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{IDF}(\text{"good"}) &= \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{37}{37}\right) = \log_{10}(1) = 0.0000 \\ \text{IDF}(\text{"Romeo"}) &= \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{37}{1}\right) = \log_{10}(37) \approx 1.5682 \\ \text{IDF}(\text{"battle"}) &= \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{37}{4}\right) = \log_{10}(9.25) \approx 0.9661 \end{aligned} \]

Step 3: Compute Final TF-IDF Weights for \(d_1\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{TF-IDF}(\text{"good"}, d_1) &= 1.6990 \times 0.0000 = \mathbf{0.0000} \\ \text{TF-IDF}(\text{"Romeo"}, d_1) &= 1.0000 \times 1.5682 = \mathbf{1.5682} \\ \text{TF-IDF}(\text{"battle"}, d_1) &= 2.0000 \times 0.9661 = \mathbf{1.9322} \end{aligned} \]

Sense Check: Even though "good" appears 5 times in \(d_1\) — more than "Romeo" — its TF-IDF weight is zero because it appears in all 37 documents (\(\text{DF} = N\)), making it completely non-discriminative. Conversely, "Romeo" appears only once but receives a weight of 1.5682 because it is extremely rare across the corpus (\(\text{DF} = 1\)). "Battle" gets the highest weight (1.9322) because it combines moderate rarity (\(\text{DF} = 4\)) with high local frequency (count = 10). This demonstrates TF-IDF's core design: promote terms that are locally frequent yet globally rare.

8.3.7 Limitations of Sparse Representations

While TF-IDF and co-occurrence matrices are straightforward to compute and highly interpretable, sparse frequency-based vector representations suffer from three fundamental limitations:

  1. Extreme Sparsity: The vast majority of matrix entries are zero (a typical document uses only a tiny fraction of the full vocabulary), leading to inefficient memory usage and wasted computation on zero-valued dimensions.
  2. High Dimensionality: Vector length equals vocabulary size \(|V|\), often 100,000+ dimensions for real-world corpora. Computing pairwise cosine similarities across millions of documents at this dimensionality is computationally expensive.
  3. Lack of Generalization (Synonym Blindness): Sparse vectors treat every vocabulary item as an independent orthogonal dimension. "Doctor" and "physician" occupy completely different axes — their cosine similarity is zero unless they literally co-occur in the same document. Sparse representations cannot capture latent synonymy or semantic similarity between unseen word pairs.

These three limitations motivate the shift to dense word embeddings (Section 8.4), where words are represented as short, dense vectors (typically 50–300 dimensions) that encode semantic similarity as geometric proximity.

8.3.8 Student Questions and Answers on Sparse Embeddings

Q: Should we use natural logarithm (\(\ln\)) or base-10 logarithm (\(\log_{10}\)) for TF-IDF calculations?

A: At web scale, \(\log_{10}\) is the standard convention because it produces more interpretable values for large document counts. In examinations, use \(\log_{10}\) for both TF and IDF. If you choose natural log (\(\ln\)), state your choice explicitly on the answer sheet and use it consistently across both the TF and IDF steps. Mixing logarithm bases within a single computation is an error.

Q: What is the exact difference between Collection Frequency (CF) and Document Frequency (DF)?

A: Collection Frequency (CF) is the total raw count of how many times a term appears across all text in all documents combined. Document Frequency (DF) is the count of distinct documents that contain the term at least once — it ignores multiple occurrences within the same document. For IDF computation, we always use DF, not CF. A term appearing 1,000 times in a single document has \(\text{DF} = 1\), but \(\text{CF} = 1000\).

8.4 Dense Vector Representations and Skip-Gram Negative Sampling (SGNS)

8.4.1 Self-Supervised Paradigm for Dense Word Embeddings

Hook: Sparse TF-IDF vectors treat "doctor" and "physician" as completely unrelated dimensions — their cosine similarity is zero unless they literally co-occur. Can we build word representations where semantically similar words are automatically close together, without ever telling the model what words mean?

Intuition: The answer is self-supervised learning — we let the algorithm generate its own training labels from raw text, with no human annotation required. The key trick: a word's meaning can be predicted from its neighbors, so we train a simple classifier to distinguish real neighbors from random imposters.

To overcome the sparsity and extreme dimensionality of TF-IDF vectors, modern NLP uses short, dense vector representations (typically 100 to 300 dimensions). Dense embeddings are learned using self-supervised learning from raw unlabeled text.

Training data construction: The algorithm slides a context window of size \(\pm k\) (e.g., \(\pm 2\)) over a text corpus. At each position: - The central word is the target word \(w\). - Words within the window are positive context words \(c_{pos}\) (label \(y = 1\)). - Words randomly sampled from outside the window are negative context words \(c_{neg}\) (label \(y = 0\)).

Analogy: Imagine a detective investigating who someone's real friends are. The detective observes who the person actually hangs out with (positive context) and compares them against random strangers pulled from a phone book (negative samples). After enough observations, the detective builds an accurate profile of the person's social circle — and by extension, their identity.

8.4.2 Symbol Registry — Skip-Gram Negative Sampling (SGNS)

  • \(w\) — Target word — Central vocabulary item being embedded — Discrete index
  • \(c_{pos}\) — Positive context word — Word occurring within \(\pm k\) window of \(w\) — Discrete index
  • \(c_{neg}\) — Negative context word — Randomly sampled word outside context — Discrete index
  • \(\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) — Target vector representation — Embedding vector for target word — Vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
  • \(\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) — Context vector representation — Embedding vector for context word — Vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
  • \(d\) — Embedding dimensionality — Number of dense hidden features — Integer (e.g., \(d = 300\))
  • \(K\) — Negative sampling ratio — Number of negative context words per positive pair — Integer (e.g., \(K = 3\))

  • \(L_{SGNS}\) — SGNS loss function — Binary cross-entropy loss over positive and negative pairs — Real scalar
  • \(\eta\) — Learning rate — Gradient descent step size parameter — Positive scalar (e.g., \(\eta = 0.05\))

8.4.3 Mathematical Formulation of SGNS Objective and Sigmoid Probabilities

SGNS recasts word embedding learning as a binary classification problem: for each word pair \( (w, c)\), predict whether \(c\) is a genuine context word (\(y = 1\)) or a randomly sampled negative word (\(y = 0\)).

1. Probability of Positive Context Pair: \[ P(+ \mid w, c_{pos}) = \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w}}} \]

The probability that context word \(c_{pos}\) genuinely appears near target word \(w\) is the sigmoid of their vector dot product. When the dot product is large and positive, \(\sigma\) is close to 1 (high confidence of co-occurrence). When the dot product is negative, \(\sigma\) is close to 0.

2. Probability of Negative Context Pair: \[ P(- \mid w, c_{neg}) = 1 - \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{neg}^\top \mathbf{w}) = \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{neg}^\top \mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\mathbf{c}_{neg}^\top \mathbf{w}}} \]

For a randomly sampled negative word, the probability that it does NOT co-occur with target \(w\) is \(1 - \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{neg}^\top \mathbf{w})\). Using the sigmoid identity \(1 - \sigma(x) = \sigma(-x)\), this simplifies to \(\sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{neg}^\top \mathbf{w})\), which produces the sign flip in the exponent.

Sigmoid Identity Derivation: \[ \begin{aligned} 1 - \sigma(x) &= 1 - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} &\quad\text{(Substitute definition of } \sigma\text{)} \\ &= \frac{(1 + e^{-x}) - 1}{1 + e^{-x}} &\quad\text{(Common denominator)} \\ &= \frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} &\quad\text{(Simplify numerator)} \\ &= \frac{1}{e^{x} + 1} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(-x)}} = \sigma(-x) &\quad\text{(Multiply top and bottom by } e^{x}\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

8.4.4 Loss Function Derivation and Gradient Descent Update Equations

For a single positive context word \(\mathbf{c}_{pos}\) and \(K\) sampled negative context words \(\mathbf{c}_{neg, i}\), the SGNS loss is the negative log-likelihood:

\[ L_{SGNS} = -\log \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w}) - \sum_{i=1}^{K} \log \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{neg, i}^\top \mathbf{w}) \]

Why this form? We want to simultaneously: - Maximize the dot product \(\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w}\) (push positive pair vectors closer together). - Minimize each dot product \(\mathbf{c}_{neg,i}^\top \mathbf{w}\) (push negative pair vectors apart).

Taking the negative log of the product of probabilities converts the maximization into a sum of log-loss terms that we minimize via gradient descent.

Gradient Derivation for the Positive Context Vector \(\mathbf{c}_{pos}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{c}_{pos}} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{c}_{pos}} \bigl[-\log \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w})\bigr] \\ &= -\frac{1}{\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w})} \cdot \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w}) \cdot (1 - \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w})) \cdot \mathbf{w} &\quad\text{(Chain rule + } \sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))\text{)} \\ &= -(1 - \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w})) \cdot \mathbf{w} &\quad\text{(Cancel } \sigma \text{ terms)} \\ &= (\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w}) - 1) \cdot \mathbf{w} &\quad\text{(Rearrange sign)} \end{aligned} \]

Gradient for the \(i\)-th Negative Context Vector \(\mathbf{c}_{neg, i}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{c}_{neg, i}} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{c}_{neg, i}} \bigl[-\log \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{neg, i}^\top \mathbf{w})\bigr] \\ &= -\frac{1}{\sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{neg, i}^\top \mathbf{w})} \cdot \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{neg, i}^\top \mathbf{w}) \cdot (1 - \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{neg, i}^\top \mathbf{w})) \cdot (-\mathbf{w}) \\ &= (1 - \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{neg, i}^\top \mathbf{w})) \cdot \mathbf{w} \\ &= \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{neg, i}^\top \mathbf{w}) \cdot \mathbf{w} &\quad\text{(Apply identity } 1 - \sigma(-x) = \sigma(x)\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Gradient for the Target Vector \(\mathbf{w}\):

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = (\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w}) - 1) \cdot \mathbf{c}_{pos} + \sum_{i=1}^{K} \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{neg, i}^\top \mathbf{w}) \cdot \mathbf{c}_{neg, i} \] The gradient descent weight update rule: \[ \mathbf{w}^{(\text{new})} = \mathbf{w}^{(\text{old})} - \eta \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{w}} \]
Push-Pull Dynamics: The gradient has an elegant physical interpretation: - The positive term \( (\sigma - 1) \cdot \mathbf{c}_{pos}\) always has a negative sign (since \(\sigma < 1\)), which pulls the target vector \(\mathbf{w}\) toward the positive context vector. - Each negative term \(\sigma \cdot \mathbf{c}_{neg,i}\) always has a positive sign, which pushes the target vector away from each negative context vector.

Over millions of training steps, similar words get pulled together and unrelated words get pushed apart — the geometric structure of meaning emerges from this simple push-pull dynamic.

8.4.6 Conceptual Foundations of CBOW and GloVe

In addition to Skip-Gram Negative Sampling, two other foundational static word embedding paradigms are commonly tested:

1. Continuous Bag-of-Words (CBOW): - Architecture: Predicts the central target word given the averaged context vectors of surrounding words (the inverse of Skip-Gram). - Training efficiency: Trains faster than SGNS because it averages context vectors, reducing the number of gradient updates per training sample. - Trade-off: CBOW produces slightly better representations for frequent words; SGNS performs better for rare words because each rare word gets individual training updates. 2. GloVe (Global Vectors for Word Representation): - Architecture: A hybrid approach that combines global matrix factorization with local context window methods. - Key insight: Instead of learning from individual context windows, GloVe directly fits a model to the global word co-occurrence statistics. - Objective function: \[ J = \sum_{i, j=1}^{|V|} f(X_{ij}) \left( \mathbf{w}_i^\top \widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j - \log X_{ij} \right)^2 \] where \(X_{ij}\) is the global co-occurrence count of words \(i\) and \(j\), and \(f(X_{ij})\) is a weighting function that prevents extremely frequent co-occurrences from dominating the objective (capping at a threshold, typically \(X_{\max} = 100\)).
Feature Skip-Gram (SGNS) CBOW GloVe
Input → Output Target → Context Context → Target Co-occurrence matrix

Rare word performance Better Weaker Moderate
Speed Slower Faster Batch (fast)
Interpretability Low Low Higher (log-bilinear)
Recap: SGNS learns dense embeddings by training a binary classifier to distinguish real context words from random negatives. The push-pull gradient dynamic causes semantically similar words to cluster together. CBOW inverts the prediction direction; GloVe operates on global co-occurrence statistics. All three produce high-quality static word embeddings suitable for downstream NLP tasks.

Q: Why do the prediction probability formulas differ between positive and negative context pairs?

A: For positive context words, we predict class \(y = 1\) using the standard sigmoid: \(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w}}}\). For negative context words, we predict class \(y = 0\), so we need \(P(y=0) = 1 - \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{neg}^\top \mathbf{w})\). Using the identity \(1 - \sigma(x) = \sigma(-x)\), this becomes \(\frac{1}{1 + e^{\mathbf{c}_{neg}^\top \mathbf{w}}}\). The positive sign in the exponent for negative samples comes from flipping the sign inside the sigmoid.

8.5 Statistical N-Gram Language Modeling and Perplexity

8.5.1 Sequence Probability Formulation and Chain Rule of Probability

Hook: If you type "I want to" into your phone, how does the keyboard predict that "eat", "go", or "know" are far more likely next words than "quantum" or "elephant"? The answer is a language model — a probability distribution over word sequences learned from massive amounts of text.

Intuition: A language model assigns a probability to every possible sequence of words. High-probability sequences sound natural ("The cat sat on the mat"); low-probability sequences sound odd ("Mat the on sat cat the"). The model learns these probabilities by counting how often words follow each other in a training corpus.

The central objective of a language model is to compute the joint probability of a word sequence \(W = (w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n)\) or to predict the conditional probability of the next word \(w_n\) given all preceding context words.

Using the chain rule of probability, the joint sequence probability decomposes as:

\[ P(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n) = P(w_1) \cdot P(w_2 \mid w_1) \cdot P(w_3 \mid w_1, w_2) \cdots P(w_n \mid w_1, \dots, w_{n-1}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i \mid w_1, \dots, w_{i-1}) \]

Each factor \(P(w_i \mid w_1, \dots, w_{i-1})\) represents the probability of word \(w_i\) given the entire preceding history. This decomposition is exact — it introduces no approximation. The challenge is estimating each conditional probability from finite training data.

8.5.2 Symbol Registry — Language Modeling and Perplexity

  • \(W\) — Word sequence / sentence — Sequence of tokens \( (w_1, \dots, w_N)\) — Ordered discrete sequence
  • \(N\) — Sentence length — Total number of tokens in evaluation sequence — Positive integer
  • \(k\) — N-gram context order — Number of preceding context words used — Non-negative integer
  • \(C(w_{i-1}, w_i)\) — Joint bigram count — Raw frequency of word pair in training corpus — Non-negative integer
  • \(C(w_{i-1})\) — Unigram context count — Raw frequency of preceding word in training corpus — Non-negative integer
  • \(|V|\) — Vocabulary size — Total count of distinct tokens in the vocabulary — Positive integer
  • \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3\) — Interpolation weights — Convex combination weights — \(\sum_j \lambda_j = 1, \; \lambda_j \ge 0\)
  • \(\text{PP}(W)\) — Perplexity — Intrinsic model evaluation metric — Real scalar \(\ge 1.0\)

8.5.3 Markov Assumption: Unigram, Bigram, and Trigram Models

As sentence length grows, the full history \( (w_1, \dots, w_{i-1})\) becomes intractably long — most long word sequences never appear in training data, making direct estimation impossible. The Markov assumption simplifies the context by assuming that the probability of the current word depends only on the preceding \(k\) words:

1. Unigram Model (\(k = 0\), no context): \[ P(w_i \mid w_1, \dots, w_{i-1}) \approx P(w_i) \] Each word is predicted independently based on its overall corpus frequency. This ignores all word order. 2. Bigram Model (\(k = 1\), 1-word context): \[ P(w_i \mid w_1, \dots, w_{i-1}) \approx P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) \] Each word depends only on the immediately preceding word. This captures local word associations. 3. Trigram Model (\(k = 2\), 2-word context): \[ P(w_i \mid w_1, \dots, w_{i-1}) \approx P(w_i \mid w_{i-2}, w_{i-1}) \] Each word depends on the two preceding words, capturing slightly richer context.
Analogy: The Markov assumption is like a board game where your next move depends only on the square you are currently standing on (bigram) or the last two squares you visited (trigram) — not on the entire history of moves that brought you there. The shorter the memory, the simpler the model, but the more context is lost.

8.5.4 Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) and Sentence Boundaries

N-gram conditional probabilities are estimated from corpus counts using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE):

Bigram MLE: \[ P_{\text{MLE}}(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = \frac{C(w_{i-1}, w_i)}{C(w_{i-1})} \]

where \(C(w_{i-1}, w_i)\) is the count of the bigram \( (w_{i-1}, w_i)\) in the training corpus, and \(C(w_{i-1})\) is the total count of word \(w_{i-1}\).

Sentence Boundaries: To model the probability of starting and ending sentences, explicit boundary markers `\lt s\gt>` (start-of-sentence) and `\lt /s\gt>` (end-of-sentence) are prepended and appended to every sentence during corpus processing. This allows the model to learn, for example, that "The" is a common sentence-starting word while "." is a common sentence-ending token.

8.5.5 Zero-Probability Problem and Smoothing Techniques (Laplace, Interpolation, Backoff)

The Zero-Probability Problem: If a bigram \( (w_{i-1}, w_i)\) never appears in the training corpus, its MLE probability is exactly zero: \(P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = 0\). Since the joint sequence probability is a product of conditional probabilities, a single zero factor causes the entire sentence probability to collapse: \(P(W) = 0\). This means the model assigns zero probability to any sentence containing even one unseen bigram — which is unreasonable for any real text.

Solution: Smoothing techniques redistribute probability mass from observed N-grams to unseen ones.

1. Laplace (Add-1) Smoothing: \[ P_{\text{Laplace}}(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = \frac{C(w_{i-1}, w_i) + 1}{C(w_{i-1}) + |V|} \]

We add 1 to every bigram count in the numerator and add \(|V|\) (total vocabulary size) to the denominator so that probabilities still sum to 1 across all possible next words.

Limitation: Laplace smoothing redistributes far too much probability mass to zero-count items, significantly distorting the probabilities of frequently observed bigrams. It is rarely used in production systems but is important to understand conceptually and is frequently tested.

2. Linear Interpolation: \[ P_{\text{interp}}(w_i \mid w_{i-2}, w_{i-1}) = \lambda_1 \, P(w_i \mid w_{i-2}, w_{i-1}) + \lambda_2 \, P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) + \lambda_3 \, P(w_i) \]

where \(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3 = 1\), \(\lambda_j \ge 0\).

Interpolation blends trigram, bigram, and unigram estimates using convex weights. If the trigram count is sparse, the bigram and unigram components provide a fallback signal, preventing zero probabilities. The weights \(\lambda_j\) are tuned on a held-out development set.

3. Katz Backoff: Uses the higher-order N-gram estimate if its count exceeds a threshold; otherwise, it "backs off" to the next lower-order model (bigram → unigram), multiplied by a redistribution factor \(\alpha\) that reallocates the probability mass from discounted higher-order estimates.

Pitfall — Laplace Denominator: When applying Laplace smoothing, students frequently forget to add \(|V|\) to the denominator, or confuse \(|V|\) with the total corpus size. The denominator must be \(C(w_{i-1}) + |V|\) to maintain valid probability normalization: \(\sum_{w_i \in V} P_{\text{Laplace}}(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = 1\).

8.5.6 Intrinsic Model Evaluation: Perplexity Formulation and Interpretation

Perplexity (PP) is the standard intrinsic metric for evaluating language models. It measures how "confused" or uncertain the model is when predicting the next token in a test sequence.

Formal definition: Perplexity is the inverse geometric mean of the per-word probabilities:

\[ \text{PP}(W) = P(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_N)^{-\frac{1}{N}} = \sqrt[N]{\frac{1}{\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{N} P(w_i \mid w_1, \dots, w_{i-1})}} \]

Log-domain computation (for numerical stability with long sequences):

\[ \text{PP}(W) = \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \ln P(w_i \mid w_1, \dots, w_{i-1}) \right) \]

Interpretation: - Lower perplexity = better model. The model is more confident in its predictions. - A perplexity of \(k\) means the model is, on average, as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among \(k\) equally likely candidate words at each position. - A perfect model that always predicts the correct next word with probability 1.0 has \(\text{PP} = 1\). - A model that guesses uniformly over a vocabulary of size \(|V|\) has \(\text{PP} = |V|\).

8.5.7 Worked Example: Bigram Probability and Perplexity Calculation

Worked Example — Bigram Sequence Probability and Perplexity

Problem: Compute the sequence probability and perplexity for the sentence \(W = \text{"\lt s\gt> I want food \lt /s\gt>"}\) using a bigram language model with the following training corpus counts:

Bigram Count Context Word Count
\( (\text{\lt s\gt>}, \text{I})\) 40 \(C(\text{\lt s\gt>}) = 100\)
\( (\text{I}, \text{want})\) 50 \(C(\text{I}) = 200\)
\( (\text{want}, \text{food})\) 10 \(C(\text{want}) = 100\)
\( (\text{food}, \text{\lt /s\gt>})\) 5 \(C(\text{food}) = 50\)

Total sequence length: \(N = 4\) bigram transitions.

Step 1: Compute MLE Bigram Probabilities

\[ \begin{aligned} P(\text{I} \mid \text{\lt s\gt>}) &= \frac{C(\text{\lt s\gt>}, \text{I})}{C(\text{\lt s\gt>})} = \frac{40}{100} = 0.40 \\ P(\text{want} \mid \text{I}) &= \frac{C(\text{I}, \text{want})}{C(\text{I})} = \frac{50}{200} = 0.25 \\ P(\text{food} \mid \text{want}) &= \frac{C(\text{want}, \text{food})}{C(\text{want})} = \frac{10}{100} = 0.10 \\ P(\text{\lt /s\gt>} \mid \text{food}) &= \frac{C(\text{food}, \text{\lt /s\gt>})}{C(\text{food})} = \frac{5}{50} = 0.10 \end{aligned} \]

Step 2: Compute Joint Sequence Probability \(P(W)\)

\[ P(W) = P(\text{I} \mid \text{\lt s\gt>}) \times P(\text{want} \mid \text{I}) \times P(\text{food} \mid \text{want}) \times P(\text{\lt /s\gt>} \mid \text{food}) \] \[ P(W) = 0.40 \times 0.25 \times 0.10 \times 0.10 = \mathbf{0.0010} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3} \]

Step 3: Compute Perplexity \(\text{PP}(W)\)

\[ \text{PP}(W) = P(W)^{-1/N} = (0.0010)^{-1/4} = \sqrt[4]{\frac{1}{0.0010}} = \sqrt[4]{1000} \approx \mathbf{5.623} \] Verification via log-domain: \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{4} \ln P_i &= \ln(0.40) + \ln(0.25) + \ln(0.10) + \ln(0.10) \\ &= -0.9163 + (-1.3863) + (-2.3026) + (-2.3026) \\ &= -6.9078 \\ \text{PP}(W) &= \exp\!\left(-\frac{-6.9078}{4}\right) = \exp(1.7269) \approx \mathbf{5.623} \end{aligned} \]

This is reasonable for a small bigram model — a well-trained trigram model on a large corpus would achieve lower perplexity (typically 50–200 for open-domain English text).

This prevents evaluation ambiguity and demonstrates methodological rigor.

Q: How is Perplexity evaluated when testing across multiple sentences in a test dataset?

A: When evaluating over multiple sentences, concatenate all words across sentences into a single total token count \(N\) (including all `\lt /s\gt>` tokens), multiply all sentence probabilities together into one joint product, and take the \(N\)-th root of the reciprocal. Equivalently, sum all log-probabilities across all sentences, divide by total \(N\), negate, and exponentiate.

Recap: Statistical N-gram language models estimate word sequence probabilities using the chain rule and Markov assumption. MLE provides the baseline estimate; smoothing (Laplace, interpolation, backoff) handles unseen N-grams. Perplexity is the standard intrinsic evaluation metric — lower is better. These models bridge to neural language models (Section 8.6) which overcome N-gram limitations by using continuous representations and unlimited context.

8.6 Neural Language Modeling, Transformer LLMs, and Prompt Engineering

8.6.1 Limitations of N-Gram Models and Neural Transition

Hook: N-gram models can predict "I want to eat" but struggle with "I want to devour" — because "devour" may never have followed "want to" in the training corpus, despite being semantically equivalent to "eat". How can we build a language model that understands meaning, not just word co-occurrence counts?

Intuition: Neural language models solve this by operating in dense vector space. Since "eat" and "devour" have similar embeddings (from models like SGNS), a neural model that learned to predict "eat" in a context automatically assigns reasonable probability to "devour" too — even if that exact bigram was never observed.

While statistical N-gram models are effective for short contexts, they suffer from two fundamental limitations:

  1. Inability to Generalize to Unseen Combinations: If a word pair never appeared in training, N-grams assign it zero probability (without smoothing). Smoothing helps but cannot capture true semantic similarity.
  2. Context Window Limitation: N-gram table sizes grow exponentially with context length (\(|V|^{k+1}\) entries for order \(k\)), making long-range dependencies intractable.

Neural language models solve both problems by projecting words into dense embedding spaces where semantically similar words share nearby representations. This enables generalization to unseen but semantically plausible word sequences.

8.6.2 Feedforward Neural Architecture for Multi-Class Next-Word Prediction

A basic feedforward neural language model predicts the next token \(w_t\) given a fixed context of \(k\) preceding words \( (w_{t-k}, \dots, w_{t-1})\): 
Input Tokens -> Embedding Lookup -> Concatenate -> Hidden Layer (Tanh/ReLU) -> Output Softmax -> P(w_t | context)

Layer-by-layer breakdown:

  1. Embedding Layer: Retrieves \(d\)-dimensional dense embedding vectors \(\mathbf{e}_{t-k}, \dots, \mathbf{e}_{t-1} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) for each input context word from a learned embedding matrix \(E \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times d}\).
2. Hidden Layer: Concatenates context vectors into a single input vector \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \cdot d}\), then applies a linear projection followed by a non-linear activation: \[ \mathbf{h} = \tanh(W_h \, \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_h) \] where \(W_h \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times kd}\) is the weight matrix, \(\mathbf{b}_h \in \mathbb{R}^m\) is the bias, and \(m\) is the hidden layer dimensionality. 3. Output Softmax Layer: Projects hidden state \(\mathbf{h}\) to a score for every vocabulary token, then normalizes via Softmax: \[ \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \text{Softmax}(W_o \, \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}_o) \quad \in \mathbb{R}^{|V|} \] where \(W_o \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times m}\) and \(\hat{y}_i = P(w_t = i \mid \text{context})\).

8.6.3 Symbol Registry — Neural Language Models

  • \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \cdot d}\) — Concatenated context embedding vector — Input vector to hidden layer
  • \(\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}\) — Hidden state activation vector — Representation after non-linear projection
  • \(W_h \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times kd}, \; \mathbf{b}_h \in \mathbb{R}^m\) — Hidden layer weight matrix and bias — Model parameters
  • \(W_o \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times m}, \; \mathbf{b}_o \in \mathbb{R}^{|V|}\) — Output layer weight matrix and bias — Projection to vocabulary space
  • \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^{|V|}\) — Predicted output probability distribution — Softmax probability vector, \(\sum_i \hat{y}_i = 1\)
  • \(z_i\) — Unnormalized logit score for token \(i\) — Raw linear output before Softmax — Real scalar

8.6.4 Loss Function, Softmax Output Layer, and Sentence Embeddings

Next-word prediction in neural language models is formulated as a multi-class classification problem where the number of target classes equals the total vocabulary size \(|V|\).

1. Softmax Activation Function: \[ P(w_t = i \mid \text{context}) = \hat{y}_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} e^{z_j}} \]

Softmax exponentiates each raw logit score \(z_i\) and divides by the sum of all exponentiated logits across the entire vocabulary, converting raw scores into a valid probability distribution that sums to 1. Every entry \(\hat{y}_i \in (0, 1)\).

Properties of Softmax: - Output always sums to exactly 1.0 (valid probability distribution). - Shift-invariant: \(\text{Softmax}(\mathbf{z} + c) = \text{Softmax}(\mathbf{z})\) for any constant \(c\). - Preserves relative ordering of logits.

2. Cross-Entropy Loss Function: \[ L_{\text{CE}} = -\log \hat{y}_y = -\log \left( \frac{e^{z_y}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} e^{z_j}} \right) = -z_y + \log \sum_{j=1}^{|V|} e^{z_j} \]

where \(y\) is the index of the ground-truth target word. Cross-entropy loss penalizes the model for assigning low probability to the correct word. Minimizing this loss is equivalent to maximizing the predicted probability of the correct next word.

Sigmoid vs. Softmax — Critical Distinction:
Property Sigmoid Softmax
Use case Binary classification (2 classes) Multi-class classification (\(|V|\) classes)
Output range Single scalar in \( (0, 1)\) Vector in \( (0, 1)^{|V|}\), sums to 1
Independence Each output is independent Outputs are mutually exclusive and sum-constrained
NLP example Sentiment (positive/negative), SGNS Next-word prediction, POS tagging

Exam rule: Neural language modeling ALWAYS uses Softmax at the output layer because it selects among \(|V|\) mutually exclusive next-word candidates. SGNS uses Sigmoid because it makes independent binary decisions for each context pair.

3. Sentence Embeddings:

To represent an entire sentence as a single fixed-length vector, individual word embeddings are aggregated: - Mean Pooling: Average all token vectors element-wise: \(\mathbf{s} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{e}_i\). Simple and effective for many downstream tasks. - Max Pooling: Take the maximum value per dimension: \(s_j = \max_i e_{i,j}\). Captures the most salient feature per dimension.

8.6.5 Overview of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Fine-Tuning

Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) extend neural language modeling principles using deep multi-layer Transformer architectures trained on billions of tokens:

- Pre-Training: Training a massive base Transformer model on unstructured web-scale text using self-supervised next-token prediction. The model learns rich linguistic representations across all levels of language understanding (syntax, semantics, pragmatics, discourse). - Fine-Tuning: Adapting a pre-trained base model to specific downstream tasks (e.g., medical QA, legal summarization) by continuing training on domain-specific labeled data. Only a fraction of the original training cost is needed.

Key examples: GPT-4 (autoregressive, decoder-only), BERT (masked language model, encoder-only), T5 (encoder-decoder). Pre-trained models are publicly accessible on platforms such as Hugging Face for direct fine-tuning.

8.6.6 Prompt Engineering Paradigms: Zero-Shot vs. Few-Shot In-Context Learning

Prompt engineering controls pre-trained generative LLM outputs without modifying model weights:

1. Zero-Shot Prompting: Providing task instructions directly to the model without any completed input-output examples. The model relies entirely on its pre-training knowledge to interpret the task.

Example:

"Classify the following movie review as positive or negative: 'This film was breathtaking.'"
Model output: "Positive"

2. Few-Shot (In-Context) Prompting: Providing task instructions alongside a few demonstration examples (typically 2–5) within the prompt. The model learns the input-output pattern from these examples without any weight updates.

Example:

"Review: 'Amazing movie!' → Positive
Review: 'Terrible acting.' → Negative
Review: 'A cinematic masterpiece.' → ?"
Model output: "Positive"

Feature Zero-Shot Few-Shot
Examples in prompt None 2–5 demonstrations
Task specification Instruction only Instruction + examples
When to use Simple, well-known tasks Ambiguous output format, domain-specific tasks
Cost Minimal tokens More tokens (examples consume context window)
Recap: Neural language models overcome N-gram limitations by operating in dense embedding space, enabling generalization to unseen word sequences. The feedforward architecture maps context embeddings through hidden layers to a Softmax output distribution over the full vocabulary. LLMs scale this paradigm with deep Transformers and billions of parameters. Prompt engineering (zero-shot and few-shot) enables task adaptation without fine-tuning.

Foundations of NLP and Evaluation Metrics

Must-know: Precision = TP/(TP+FP), Recall = TP/(TP+FN), F1 = 2·P·R/(P+R) = 2TP/(2TP+FP+FN). F1 is the harmonic mean, not the arithmetic mean, of Precision and Recall.

\[\text{F1} = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} = \frac{2 \cdot TP}{2 \cdot TP + FP + FN}\]

Top pitfall: Using arithmetic mean instead of harmonic mean for F1, or confusing FP with FN in the Precision vs Recall denominators.

Vector Semantics and Sparse Frequency-Based Representations

Must-know: TF(t,d) = 1 + log10(count(t,d)) if count>0, else 0. IDF(t) = log10(N/DF(t)). TF-IDF = TF × IDF. Cosine similarity normalizes dot product by L2 norms. Use log10 for both TF and IDF.

\[\cos(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|_2 \|\mathbf{b}\|_2}; \quad \text{TF-IDF}(t,d) = (1 + \log_{10}(\text{count}(t,d))) \times \log_{10}(N / \text{DF}(t))\]

Top pitfall: Using raw dot product instead of cosine similarity when comparing documents of different lengths; confusing Collection Frequency (CF) with Document Frequency (DF) in the IDF formula.

Self-check: A term appears 100 times in a document but occurs in all N documents. What is its TF-IDF weight?

Connects to: 8.2, 8.4

Dense Vector Representations and Skip-Gram Negative Sampling (SGNS)

Must-know: SGNS loss: L = -log σ(c_pos·w) - Σ log σ(-c_neg·w). Gradient for w: (σ(c_pos·w)-1)·c_pos + Σ σ(c_neg·w)·c_neg. Update: w_new = w_old - η·∂L/∂w. Sigmoid identity: 1-σ(x) = σ(-x).

\[L_{SGNS} = -\log \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{pos}^\top \mathbf{w}) - \sum_{i=1}^K \log \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{neg,i}^\top \mathbf{w})\]

Top pitfall: Forgetting the sign flip in the negative sample probability: P(-|w,c_neg) uses σ(-c·w), not σ(c·w). Also confusing the gradient error factor: (σ-1) for positive vs σ for negative.

Self-check: If σ(c_pos·w) = 0.8, what is the gradient error factor for the positive context pair?

Connects to: 8.3, 8.5, 8.6

Statistical N-Gram Language Modeling and Perplexity

Must-know: Bigram MLE: P(w_i|w_{i-1}) = C(w_{i-1},w_i)/C(w_{i-1}). Laplace: add 1 to numerator, |V| to denominator. Perplexity: PP(W) = P(W)^{-1/N}. Lower PP = better model.

\[\text{PP}(W) = P(w_1, \dots, w_N)^{-1/N} = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \ln P(w_i \mid w_1, \dots, w_{i-1})\right)\]

Top pitfall: Forgetting to add |V| to the Laplace smoothing denominator; confusing |V| with total corpus size; not including <s>/</s> boundary markers in N-gram count calculations.
