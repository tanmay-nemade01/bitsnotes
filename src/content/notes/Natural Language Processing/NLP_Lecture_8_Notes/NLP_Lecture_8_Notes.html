8.1 What Is Natural Language Processing and Why Is It Hard?

Hook. You type "best pizza near me" and get an answer in under a second. You ask your phone "what's the weather?" and it replies with today's forecast. How does a machine — a pile of silicon running math — understand your words? And why does it still fail spectacularly when you ask something slightly unusual?

8.1.1 Definition

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the field that builds systems to understand, interpret, and generate human language. The core loop is understand then generate — an encoder-decoder pattern even in classical systems.

Intuition. Think of NLP as building a bridge between human communication and machine computation. On one side: messy, ambiguous, context-rich human language. On the other side: precise, deterministic, numerical computation. NLP is everything that sits on that bridge — tokenizing words, parsing grammar, resolving meaning, and generating fluent responses. The encoder compresses human language into numbers; the decoder expands numbers back into human language.

Everyday Analogy — A translator at the UN. A United Nations interpreter hears a speech in Mandarin, mentally converts it to an internal representation of meaning, then produces the same meaning in English. NLP does the same: raw text → internal numerical representation → generated text. The interpreter doesn't memorize phrasebooks — they understand the ideas and re-express them. Modern NLP models work the same way. Where the analogy breaks: The interpreter has a lifetime of embodied experience. The NLP model only has text patterns. It doesn't know what "cold" feels like.

8.1.2 Why Natural Language Is a Hard Problem

Human language is full of complexity that makes it difficult for machines:

  • Words can mean different things in different contexts.
  • The same idea can be expressed in many different ways.
  • Language relies on shared background knowledge that machines do not have.
  • Sentences can be grammatically perfect but nonsensical.

Consider the sentence: "Time flies like an arrow; fruit flies like a banana." A human instantly parses both meanings. A machine sees "flies" as a verb in the first half and a noun in the second — but how does it know which is which? This single sentence packs lexical ambiguity, syntactic ambiguity, and world knowledge into nine words.

8.1.3 Ambiguities in Language

Ambiguity is one of the central challenges. There are several distinct types:

Type What it means Example
Lexical ambiguity A single word has multiple meanings "bank" — financial institution vs. river bank
Syntactic ambiguity A sentence can be parsed in multiple ways "I saw a man with a telescope" — who has the telescope?
Semantic ambiguity The meaning of a phrase is unclear even with correct syntax "The chicken is ready to eat" — is the chicken eating or being eaten?
Pragmatic ambiguity The intended meaning depends on context or speaker intent "Can you pass the salt?" — a request, not a question about ability
Referential ambiguity A pronoun or reference could point to multiple things "John told Mike he was tired" — who is tired?

You must be able to identify each type and give your own examples.

Test yourself — classify these ambiguities:

  1. "Visiting relatives can be boring." → Syntactic (is "visiting" a gerund or participle?)
  2. "She saw the man with binoculars." → Syntactic (who has the binoculars?)
  3. "The bass was delicious." → Lexical (fish or musical instrument?)
  4. "Could you open the window?" → Pragmatic (question or request?)
  5. "Sara told Priya that she won the prize." → Referential (who won?)

Ambiguity is not a bug in language — it's a feature that lets us communicate efficiently. But it's NLP's hardest problem, because resolving ambiguity requires world knowledge that machines must learn from data alone.

8.1.4 Levels of Language Understanding

Language understanding happens at multiple levels, each building on the one before:

  1. Phonology — sounds of language (speech processing)
  2. Morphology — structure of words (prefixes, suffixes, roots)
  3. Lexical — individual word meanings
  4. Syntax — grammar, sentence structure
  5. Semantics — meaning of phrases and sentences
  6. Discourse — meaning across multiple sentences; coreference resolution
  7. Pragmatics — meaning in context, speaker intent, real-world knowledge

Modern Large Language Models can reach up to the discourse level — they handle coreference resolution and contextual understanding through attention mechanisms. But you should know how each level differs and be able to explain each with examples.

Visual Intuition — The Language Layer Cake. Imagine a seven-layer cake. Each layer sits on the one below and supports the one above.

  • Bottom layer (Phonology): Raw sound waves — "k", "ae", "t" → the word "cat"
  • Layer 2 (Morphology): Word pieces — "un" + "break" + "able" → three morphemes, one word
  • Layer 3 (Lexical): Individual words with dictionary meanings — "bank" as a standalone word
  • Layer 4 (Syntax): How words arrange — "The cat sat on the mat" vs. "Sat the cat on the mat"
  • Layer 5 (Semantics): What the arrangement means — who did what to whom
  • Layer 6 (Discourse): How sentences connect — "The cat sat. It was tired." (it = cat)
  • Top layer (Pragmatics): Why the speaker said it — "It's cold in here" (meaning: close the window)

The key insight: a system that only handles layers 1-4 can parse grammar perfectly but still miss that "It's cold in here" is a request, not a weather report.

Common trap: Don't conflate discourse and pragmatics. Discourse is about how sentences relate to each other (coreference, coherence). Pragmatics is about why the speaker said something — intent, implicature, real-world context. A model can resolve that "it" refers to "the cat" (discourse) but still not know that "Can you pass the salt?" is a request, not a yes/no question (pragmatics).

8.1.5 Classical NLP vs. Modern NLP vs. Hybrid Systems

Classical NLP pipeline: A sequence of explicit modules — tokenization → morphology → parsing → semantic analysis → discourse processing. Each stage uses hand-crafted rules or statistical models trained on annotated data.

Modern NLP (LLMs): A single large neural network handles everything end-to-end, learning patterns from massive text corpora.

Hybrid NLP systems combine both approaches.

A typical hybrid architecture:

  • Pre-processing: classical pipeline (tokenization, parsing, rule-based filtering) — lighter, faster, more controllable
  • Core reasoning: an LLM for understanding and generation — handles ambiguity and complex reasoning
  • Post-processing: guardrails and rules — classical validation to prevent hallucinations or enforce formats

This is the recommended practical approach for real-world NLP application development. Use classical methods for lighter processing; use LLMs for advanced reasoning; use rules again for post-processing guardrails.

Analogy — Autonomous car with a human supervisor. A self-driving car handles 99% of driving (the LLM). But it has hardcoded rules that override the AI: "never exceed the speed limit," "always stop for a red light" (classical guardrails). The hybrid approach uses the AI's flexibility where it shines and classical rules where reliability is non-negotiable.

8.1.6 Evaluating NLP Systems

Evaluation is not just about accuracy. Different measures matter for different tasks:

  • Accuracy — fraction of correct predictions overall
  • Precision — of all items the system flagged as positive, how many were truly positive?
  • Recall — of all truly positive items, how many did the system flag?
  • F1-score — harmonic mean of precision and recall; balances the two
  • Task-specific metrics — BLEU (translation), ROUGE (summarization), perplexity (language modeling)
  • Intrinsic evaluation — measures the algorithm itself (e.g., perplexity)
  • Extrinsic evaluation — measures the full system's end-to-end performance (black-box testing)

You should be able to compute precision, recall, and F1-score from a confusion matrix.

Worked example — Precision, Recall, F1 from a confusion matrix.

A spam classifier runs on 100 emails (60 spam, 40 ham). It flags 50 as spam. Of those 50, 45 are actually spam and 5 are ham incorrectly flagged. The remaining 10 spam emails were missed (marked ham).

Predicted Spam Predicted Ham
Actual Spam TP = 45 FN = 15
Actual Ham FP = 5 TN = 35
  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 45 / (45 + 5) = 45/50 = 0.90
  • Recall = TP / (TP + FN) = 45 / (45 + 15) = 45/60 = 0.75
  • F1 = 2 × (0.90 × 0.75) / (0.90 + 0.75) = 2 × 0.675 / 1.65 = 0.818

Sense-check: High precision (90% of flagged emails are truly spam) but lower recall (25% of spam gets through). The classifier is conservative — it only flags when confident.

NLP evaluation is task-specific. Accuracy alone misleads when classes are imbalanced. Always report precision AND recall — F1 is their balanced compromise.

Domain Connection. These evaluation concepts are not academic — every production NLP system (search engines, chatbots, translation services) uses precision, recall, and F1 daily. When Google measures search quality, they compute precision@k (how many top-k results are relevant) and recall (how many relevant documents were retrieved). When a medical NLP system screens radiology reports for urgent findings, high recall is non-negotiable — missing a critical case is worse than a false alarm.

8.2 Vector Semantics - Representing Words as Vectors

8.2.1 Why Vectors?

Hook. You know "king" relates to "queen" the way "man" relates to "woman." You know "pizza" and "burger" are more similar than "pizza" and "democracy." How do you give a machine this intuition — without programming every word relationship by hand?

Words that are similar in meaning tend to appear in similar contexts. If you represent each word as a vector (a list of numbers), then:

  • Similar words end up close together in the vector space.
  • You can measure similarity mathematically using dot products or cosine distance.
  • Relations among words (synonymy, analogy) become computable.

This is called word embedding — embedding words into a continuous vector space.

Analogy — A map of a city. Every word is a building in a vast city. Similar buildings cluster in neighborhoods: all the restaurants are near each other, all the banks are in the financial district. A word vector is like GPS coordinates for each building. "Pizza" and "burger" have nearby coordinates. "Pizza" and "democracy" are in different zip codes entirely. The vector space IS the map — and you can measure walking distance between any two words.

Where the analogy breaks: In a real city map, you need 2 coordinates (latitude, longitude). Word vectors need hundreds of dimensions to capture all the subtle ways words relate — synonyms, antonyms, analogies, domain-specific meanings.

8.2.2 Lexical Semantic Relations

  • Synonymy — two words mean (nearly) the same thing (e.g., "happy" and "glad")
  • Similarity — words share related meanings but are not identical (e.g., "car" and "truck")
  • Antonymy — opposites (e.g., "hot" and "cold")

These relations are intuitive. You do not need to memorize formal definitions — just understand the concepts.

8.2.3 Two Families of Word Embedding

Approach How it works Example methods
Frequency-based Count how often words occur in documents or near each other TF-IDF, co-occurrence vectors
Prediction-based Train a neural network to predict context words, keep the learned weights Word2Vec (Skip-gram, CBOW), GloVe

8.2.4 Term Frequency (TF)

Term frequency is how many times a term \(t\) appears in a document \(d\):

\[ \text{tf}_{t,d} = \text{count}(t, d) \]

Why take the log? Raw counts can be very large. Taking the logarithm squashes the values:

\[ \text{tf}_{t,d} = \log_{10}(\text{count}(t, d) + 1) \]

The \(+1\) prevents \(\log(0)\) when the term does not appear in that document. Log base 10 is preferred at web scale because it squashes large values more aggressively than natural log. For small toy datasets, log base 2 or natural log are also used.

Normalization variant: Some formulas divide the raw count by the total number of words in the document. This normalizes for document length.

Intuition — Why squash with log? If "algorithm" appears 100 times in document A and 10 times in document B, is A really ten times more about algorithms? Probably not. After 5-10 occurrences, extra mentions add diminishing information. The log captures this: \(\log_{10}(100) \approx 2.0\), \(\log_{10}(10) = 1.0\) — a 2× difference, not 10×. This is more realistic.

Student Q&A — Which log base to use?

At web scale, use \(\log_{10}\) because the counts are very large. For toy examples in an exam, any consistent base is fine. If the question specifies a base, use the specified one. Always write your formula clearly so the evaluator knows what you used. The key insight: different bases only change results by a constant factor, and since TF-IDF is typically used for ranking (not absolute scores), the relative order stays the same.

8.2.5 Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

Term frequency alone has a problem: it gives high weight to common words like "the" or "good." These words appear everywhere and are not useful for distinguishing documents.

You want to give more weight to rare, distinctive words. If a document is about "Sachin Tendulkar," you want that term to matter — even if it does not appear as often as "good" or "player."

Inverse Document Frequency captures this rarity:

\[ \text{idf}_t = \log_{10}\left(\frac{N}{\text{df}_t}\right) \]

Where:

  • \(N\) = total number of documents in the corpus
  • \(\text{df}_t\) = document frequency — how many documents contain term \(t\)

Crucial distinction — Document Frequency vs. Collection Frequency:

  • Collection frequency counts every occurrence of the term across all documents. If "Romeo" appears 5 times in document 1, you count all 5.
  • Document frequency only checks whether the term appears in a document at all — yes or no. "Romeo" appears in 1 out of 100 documents → \(\text{df} = 1\).

IDF uses document frequency, not collection frequency. Getting this wrong is a classic exam trap.

Why inverse? If a term appears in very few documents, it is more informative. Taking \(N / \text{df}\) makes rare terms have high IDF. The log squashes the values.

Worked intuition: If "Romeo" appears in 1 out of 37 documents, its IDF is \(\log_{10}(37/1) = 1.57\). If "good" appears in all 37 documents, its IDF is \(\log_{10}(37/37) = 0\). Rare term → high IDF. Common term → low (or zero) IDF.

8.2.6 TF-IDF - The Combined Weight

\[ \text{tf-idf}_{t,d} = \text{tf}_{t,d} \times \text{idf}_t \]

This gives high weight to terms that are:

  • Frequent in this specific document (high TF)
  • Rare across the corpus (high IDF)

The resulting vector for each word has values in decimal points (squashed). Each word's vector has length equal to the vocabulary size.

Worked TF-IDF example.

Consider a corpus of 3 documents:

  • Doc 1: "the cat sat on the mat"
  • Doc 2: "the dog sat on the log"
  • Doc 3: "the cat chased the dog"

Vocabulary (unique words): the, cat, sat, on, mat, dog, log, chased → \(V = 8\)

Compute TF-IDF for "cat" in Doc 1:

  • TF("cat", Doc1) = \(\log_{10}(1 + 1) = \log_{10}(2) \approx 0.301\)
  • "cat" appears in Doc 1 and Doc 3 → \(\text{df} = 2\)
  • IDF("cat") = \(\log_{10}(3/2) = \log_{10}(1.5) \approx 0.176\)
  • TF-IDF("cat", Doc1) = 0.301 × 0.176 ≈ 0.053

Now compute TF-IDF for "the" in Doc 1:

  • TF("the", Doc1) = \(\log_{10}(2 + 1) = \log_{10}(3) \approx 0.477\) (higher TF than "cat"!)
  • "the" appears in all 3 documents → \(\text{df} = 3\)
  • IDF("the") = \(\log_{10}(3/3) = \log_{10}(1) = 0\)
  • TF-IDF("the", Doc1) = 0.477 × 0 = 0

Sense-check: "the" had higher raw TF than "cat," but its TF-IDF is zero because it appears everywhere and distinguishes nothing. "cat" is rarer and more informative — exactly what we want.

8.2.7 Co-occurrence Vectors

Another frequency-based approach: build a matrix where each row is a word and each column is another word. The cell \((i, j)\) counts how often word \(j\) appears within \(\pm k\) words of word \(i\) in the corpus.

This captures which words "hang out together" — the same intuition later used by Skip-gram.

8.2.8 Cosine Similarity

To measure how similar two word vectors are, you cannot just subtract them. You need a similarity measure.

Dot product gives similarity but is not normalized — longer documents get larger values.

Cosine similarity normalizes by dividing by the lengths of both vectors:

\[ \text{cosine}(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{||\mathbf{a}|| \cdot ||\mathbf{b}||} \]
  • Result close to 1 → words are similar, lie in the same semantic space
  • Result close to 0 → words are unrelated
  • Result close to -1 → words are opposite (rare in practice for word vectors)

Analogy — Two spotlights on a wall. Imagine two flashlights pointing at a wall. Cosine similarity measures the angle between the two beams. If they point the same direction (angle ≈ 0°), cosine = 1 — the words are semantically aligned. If they point perpendicular (angle = 90°), cosine = 0 — unrelated. If they point opposite (angle = 180°), cosine = -1 — antonyms. The length of the beam (how bright) doesn't matter — only the direction. That's what dividing by the norm does.

Worked cosine similarity example.

Vector a = \([1, 2, 3]\), Vector b = \([2, 4, 6]\)

  • Dot product: \((1×2) + (2×4) + (3×6) = 2 + 8 + 18 = 28\)
  • \(||\mathbf{a}|| = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{1+4+9} = \sqrt{14} \approx 3.742\)
  • \(||\mathbf{b}|| = \sqrt{2^2 + 4^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{4+16+36} = \sqrt{56} \approx 7.483\)
  • Cosine = \(28 / (3.742 × 7.483) = 28 / 28.0 =\) 1.0

Sense-check: Vector b is exactly 2 × a. They point in the exact same direction — perfectly similar in cosine terms. This is correct: doubling the TF-IDF of every term shouldn't change what the document is about, just its length.

8.2.9 The Sparsity Problem

Frequency-based vectors have two major problems:

  1. Sparsity — most entries are zero because most words do not appear in most documents
  2. Dimensionality — each vector's length equals the vocabulary size (e.g., 100,000 dimensions)

This makes computation (cosine similarity, storage) expensive. Yet TF-IDF is still used today — in hybrid search systems, sparse TF-IDF vectors are combined with dense neural embeddings for better retrieval.

Vector semantics transforms the fuzzy notion of "word meaning" into geometry. TF-IDF does it by counting; Word2Vec does it by predicting. Both produce vectors where similar words cluster together. The sparse → dense transition (next section) is what made modern NLP possible.

Domain Connection. Vector semantics powers every modern search engine. When you search Google, your query and every candidate document are both embedded as vectors. Cosine similarity between query and document vectors determines your search ranking. The same technique drives recommendation systems (Netflix: "because you watched X"), plagiarism detectors, and legal document discovery.

8.3 Dense Word Embeddings - Word2Vec

8.3.1 Motivation: From Sparse to Dense

Hook. You have 100,000 unique words in your vocabulary. A TF-IDF vector for a single word is 100,000 numbers long — and 99,997 of them are zero. Storing this for a million documents is a nightmare. Computing cosine similarity means multiplying 100,000 mostly-zero pairs. Can we compress all that meaning into, say, 300 numbers — where every number carries some meaning?

The idea of representing words as vectors is powerful. But the frequency-based method produces vectors that are sparse and huge. The solution: learn shorter, dense vectors (e.g., 300 dimensions instead of 100,000) where every dimension carries some meaning.

These dimensions are learned features. Their names do not matter — they are discovered automatically by the training algorithm. Common dimensionalities:

  • 300 (classic Word2Vec)
  • 768 (GPT-2, GPT-3)
  • 1024 (GPT-4 and beyond)

Analogy — From a checklist to a fingerprint. A TF-IDF vector is like a 100,000-item checklist: "Does word #1 appear? Check. Word #2? No. Word #3? Check…" — mostly empty boxes. A dense embedding is like a fingerprint: only a few hundred measurements, but every ridge and whorl contributes to uniquely identifying you. No single measurement means "blue eyes" or "curly hair" — but the combination captures everything.

8.3.2 Self-Supervised Learning

Word2Vec is a self-supervised algorithm. You need training data (raw text), but you do not need human-labeled data. The algorithm creates its own training examples automatically from the text.

8.3.3 One-Hot Encoding as Input

Every word in the vocabulary gets a unique position. A one-hot vector is a vector the size of the vocabulary with a single 1 at that word's position and 0 everywhere else.

One-hot vectors cannot capture similarity — every pair of words has the same distance. But they serve as a lookup mechanism: multiplying a one-hot vector by a weight matrix selects the row corresponding to that word.

8.3.4 Dimensionality Reduction via Weight Matrix

If your vocabulary size is \(V\) and you want \(d\)-dimensional embeddings:

  • One-hot vector: \(1 \times V\)
  • Weight matrix: \(V \times d\)
  • Result: \(1 \times d\) — the dense embedding for that word

You go from 50,000 sparse dimensions to (say) 5 dense dimensions.

Visual Intuition — A lookup table. Imagine a giant Excel spreadsheet. Row labels are words (50,000 rows). Columns are "feature 1," "feature 2," … "feature 300" (300 columns). A one-hot vector is like highlighting exactly one row. Multiplying by the weight matrix is just reading off that row's 300 numbers. The magic is not in the multiplication — it's in how those 300 numbers get learned so that similar words have similar rows.

8.3.5 Skip-gram Negative Sampling (SGNS) - The Algorithm

Step 1: Create Self-Supervised Training Data

Take a sentence. For each word \(w\) (the target), define a context window of \(\pm k\) words (typically \(k = 2\) or \(k = 5\)):

  • Words inside the window are positive context words (class = 1)
  • Words outside the window are negative context words (class = 0)

For each positive example, you sample \(K\) negative examples (typically \(K = 2\) to 15). More negatives help the model distinguish better.

This creates a binary classification problem: "Is this word a context word for the target?"

Analogy — Learning friendships by observing a cafeteria. Watch students at lunch for a month. Students who sit together often are "friends" (positive context). Students who never sit together are "not friends" (negative context). For each student (target word), you note their lunch companions (positive context words) and randomly pick a few students from other tables (negative samples). After enough observations, you can predict: "Given student A, is student B likely to be at their table?" — without ever asking anyone "are you friends?"

Step 2: Train a Binary Classifier

The task: given a target word \(w\) and a candidate context word \(c\), predict whether \(c\) is a true context word.

Use the sigmoid function to convert similarity (dot product) to a probability:

\[ P(+|w, c) = \sigma(c \cdot w) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-c \cdot w}} \]

For negative (out-of-context) words:

\[ P(-|w, c) = 1 - P(+|w, c) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{c \cdot w}} \]

This is standard logistic regression — just note the sign change in the exponent for the negative case.

Student Q&A — Clarifying the negative probability formula.

For negative context words, the sigmoid formula is NOT the same as for positive. The negative probability is \(1 - P(\text{positive})\):

\[ P(-|w, c) = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-c \cdot w}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{c \cdot w}} \]

The sign in the exponent flips. This is standard binary logistic regression — the two classes must have complementary probabilities. If you used the same formula for both, the model could not distinguish positive from negative.

The target values (actual labels) are:

  • Positive context word → target = 1
  • Negative context word → target = 0

These targets are fixed — they come from the data, not from the formula. The formula gives your prediction. The error is prediction minus target.

Step 3: The Loss Function

We maximize similarity for positive pairs and minimize it for negative pairs. Using log loss with \(K\) negative samples:

\[ L = -\log\sigma(c_{\text{pos}} \cdot w) - \sum_{i=1}^{K} \log\sigma(-c_{\text{neg}_i} \cdot w) \]

This is cross-entropy loss. The negative sign is there because we minimize loss (and want to maximize the probability).

Step 4: Update Weights via Gradient Descent

Take partial derivatives of the loss with respect to three variables:

With respect to positive context vector \(c_{\text{pos}}\):

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial c_{\text{pos}}} = [\sigma(c_{\text{pos}} \cdot w) - 1] \cdot w \]

The \(\sigma(c_{\text{pos}} \cdot w) - 1\) term is the error — predicted probability minus actual (actual = 1 for positive).

With respect to each negative context vector \(c_{\text{neg}}\):

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial c_{\text{neg}}} = \sigma(c_{\text{neg}} \cdot w) \cdot w \]

The actual target is 0 for negatives, and we get \(\sigma(c_{\text{neg}} \cdot w) - 0\).

With respect to target vector \(w\) (combines positive and all negatives):

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = [\sigma(c_{\text{pos}} \cdot w) - 1] \cdot c_{\text{pos}} + \sum_{i=1}^{K} \sigma(c_{\text{neg}_i} \cdot w) \cdot c_{\text{neg}_i} \]

Step 5: Update Rule

\[ w_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}} - \eta \cdot \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} \]

Where \(\eta\) (eta) is the learning rate — how big each update step is. A typical value is 0.05.

8.3.6 Worked SGNS Example (One Iteration)

Setup:

  • Target word: "net" — initial target vector: \([0.10, 0.20, 0.05, -0.10]\)
  • Positive context: "start" — context vector: \([0.05, -0.10, 0.15, 0.20]\)
  • Three negative contexts: "pimples" \([-0.10, 0.05, -0.20, 0.15]\), "zebra" \([0.15, -0.05, 0.10, 0.20]\), "idiot" \([-0.20, 0.10, -0.05, 0.05]\)
  • Learning rate \(\eta = 0.05\)

Step 1: Compute sigmoid for the positive pair.

Dot product \(c_{\text{pos}} \cdot w\):

\[ (0.05 \times 0.10) + (-0.10 \times 0.20) + (0.15 \times 0.05) + (0.20 \times -0.10) = 0.005 - 0.020 + 0.0075 - 0.020 = -0.0275 \]

Sigmoid: \(\sigma(-0.0275) \approx 0.493\)

Error term for positive: \(\sigma(c_{\text{pos}} \cdot w) - 1 = 0.493 - 1 = -0.507\)

Gradient for \(c_{\text{pos}}\): \((-0.507) \times [0.10, 0.20, 0.05, -0.10] = [-0.0507, -0.1014, -0.0254, 0.0507]\)

Step 2: Compute sigmoid for each negative pair.

For "pimples" (\(c_{\text{neg}_1}\)):

  • Dot product: \((-0.10 \times 0.10) + (0.05 \times 0.20) + (-0.20 \times 0.05) + (0.15 \times -0.10) = -0.01 + 0.01 - 0.01 - 0.015 = -0.025\)
  • Sigmoid: \(\sigma(-0.025) \approx 0.494\)
  • Gradient contribution to \(w\): \(0.494 \times [-0.10, 0.05, -0.20, 0.15] = [-0.0494, 0.0247, -0.0988, 0.0741]\)

For "zebra" and "idiot": similar computation with their vectors.

Step 3: Sum all contributions to get \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}\).

Step 4: Update the target vector.

\[ w_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}} - 0.05 \times \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} \]

Each dimension updates separately: \(w_{\text{new}}[i] = w_{\text{old}}[i] - 0.05 \times \text{gradient}[i]\)

Sense-check: The positive context gave a sigmoid of ~0.493 — barely above random (0.5). The error is large (-0.507), so the update will strongly adjust "net" toward "start". The negative contexts all give sigmoids near 0.5 — the model is uncertain about them too. After many iterations, positive sigmoids will rise toward 1.0 and negative sigmoids toward 0.0.

8.3.7 Key Practical Points About SGNS

  • In practice, you run many iterations (epochs) over the corpus. An exam may ask for just one iteration.
  • Each word in the vocabulary has two embeddings: a target word embedding (when the word is the center/target) and a context word embedding (when the word appears as context for other words).
  • The final word embedding is typically a combination (sum or average) of both.
  • Initial weights should be small random values, roughly in \([-0.05, +0.05]\). They should not be zero.

8.3.8 CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words)

CBOW is the mirror of Skip-gram: instead of predicting context words from a target, you predict the target word from its surrounding context words. Only conceptual understanding is expected — no mathematical problems on CBOW.

8.3.9 GloVe (Global Vectors)

GloVe combines the strengths of both frequency-based methods and prediction-based methods. It uses the global co-occurrence statistics of the entire corpus. Only conceptual understanding is expected — there could be a minor conceptual question, but no full mathematical problem.

SGNS turns the distributional hypothesis — "you shall know a word by the company it keeps" — into a trainable algorithm. Every word gets two dense vectors. Training is just binary classification: "do these two words appear together?" The learned vectors capture semantic relations so well that vector("king") − vector("man") + vector("woman") ≈ vector("queen").

Domain Connection. Pre-trained Word2Vec embeddings fueled the deep learning revolution in NLP (2013-2018). Before Word2Vec, NLP models used sparse bag-of-words features. After Word2Vec, every NLP pipeline started with a dense embedding layer. Today's LLMs still use the same core idea — they just learn the embeddings as part of a much larger transformer network rather than as a standalone step.

8.4 N-gram Language Modeling

8.4.1 What Is Language Modeling?

Hook. Finish this sentence: "The cat sat on the _____." Your brain instantly suggests "mat," "floor," "chair" — and rules out "democracy" or "running." A language model does the same thing mathematically: it assigns a probability to every possible next word. The better the model, the more it agrees with human intuition about what words are likely.

A language model predicts the next word (or sequence of words) given the preceding words. If you can model how sentences are generated in a language, you have a language model.

Applications are vast: speech recognition, machine translation, text generation, spelling correction, and the foundation of modern LLMs.

Analogy — A weather forecaster for words. A weather forecaster says "80% chance of rain tomorrow." A language model says "given 'The cat sat on the', there's a 60% chance the next word is 'mat' and 0.001% chance it's 'elephant'." Weather models use past patterns — temperature, pressure, season. Language models use preceding words instead. The "climate" is your training corpus.

8.4.2 The Chain Rule of Probability

To compute the probability of a sequence of words \(w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n\):

\[ P(w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n) = P(w_1) \cdot P(w_2 \mid w_1) \cdot P(w_3 \mid w_1, w_2) \cdots P(w_n \mid w_1, \ldots, w_{n-1}) \]

8.4.3 The Markov Assumption

The full chain rule is impractical — you will almost never see the same long sequence twice in training data. The Markov assumption simplifies: the current word depends only on the previous \(k\) words.

  • Unigram: \(P(w_i)\) — no context at all
  • Bigram: \(P(w_i \mid w_{i-1})\) — depends on the previous one word
  • Trigram: \(P(w_i \mid w_{i-2}, w_{i-1})\) — depends on the previous two words

Unigram is rarely used alone because it captures zero context. It is a fallback when bigram/trigram data is unavailable.

Analogy — Markov assumption as a goldfish memory. A bigram model has the memory of a goldfish: it only remembers the immediately preceding word. Given "the cat sat on the," a bigram model only looks at "the" to predict the next word. A trigram model looks at "on the" — slightly better. A human looks at the whole sentence. The Markov assumption trades accuracy for practicality: models with short memories are computable; models with perfect memory need infinite data.

8.4.4 Computing Bigram Probabilities from a Corpus

Given a small corpus (a few sentences), you can build a bigram probability table:

  1. Count how often each word appears (unigram counts).
  2. Count how often each pair of consecutive words appears (bigram counts).
  3. For each pair \((w_{i-1}, w_i)\): \(P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = \frac{\text{count}(w_{i-1}, w_i)}{\text{count}(w_{i-1})}\)

Start and end tokens: If the question says to include <s> (start) and </s> (end) tokens, you must add them. If not mentioned, state your assumption clearly. These tokens ensure the model can generate the beginning and end of sentences properly.

Table convention: In a bigram table, rows are the first word \((w_{i-1})\) and columns are the next word \((w_i)\). The cell value is the count or probability of the second word following the first. This is standard — the same convention used for emission and transition matrices in HMM tagging.

Student Q&A — Row/column convention: Rows = first word (context), Columns = next word (predicted). Always state this assumption in your answer.

8.4.5 The Zero-Probability Problem and Smoothing

If a bigram never appeared in training, its probability is zero — and then the probability of any sentence containing that bigram becomes zero. This is too harsh.

Laplace (Add-One) Smoothing

Add 1 to every bigram count, and add \(V\) (vocabulary size) to the denominator:

\[ P_{\text{Laplace}}(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = \frac{\text{count}(w_{i-1}, w_i) + 1}{\text{count}(w_{i-1}) + V} \]

Where \(V\) is the number of unique words in the vocabulary. Count the unique words from the sentences given.

Problem with Laplace smoothing: Laplace smoothing changes the counts substantially. The smoothed counts no longer reflect the true distribution well. For language modeling, this is often undesirable because it distorts the data too much — it gives too much probability mass to unseen events.

Interpolation

Instead of modifying counts, interpolation combines estimates from different n-gram orders:

\[ P_{\text{interp}}(w_i \mid w_{i-2}, w_{i-1}) = \lambda_1 \cdot P(w_i \mid w_{i-2}, w_{i-1}) + \lambda_2 \cdot P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) + \lambda_3 \cdot P(w_i) \]

Where \(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3 = 1\) and typically \(\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 > \lambda_3\) — more weight to the higher-order (more contextual) estimate. The \(\lambda\) values may be given in the question, or you can state reasonable assumptions.

Backoff

Backoff does not combine estimates. Instead, it uses a priority order:

  1. Use the trigram if available.
  2. If the trigram count is zero, back off to the bigram.
  3. If the bigram count is zero, back off to the unigram.

This avoids the problem of zero counts without changing the actual count values.

Analogy — Interpolation vs. Backoff as two safety strategies.

Interpolation is like a diversified investment portfolio: you always put some money in stocks (trigram), some in bonds (bigram), and some in cash (unigram). You never rely on just one source.

Backoff is like a hierarchy of emergency contacts: call your partner first (trigram). If they don't answer, call your friend (bigram). If they don't answer, call your mom (unigram). You only use the fallback when the preferred option fails.

8.4.6 Perplexity - Evaluating Language Models

Perplexity is an intrinsic evaluation measure — it evaluates the model itself, not an end-to-end system.

Intuition: Perplexity tells you how "confused" the model is when choosing the next word. A perplexity of 5 means the model is as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among 5 equally likely words.

Lower perplexity = better model.

Formula for a sequence of \(N\) words:

\[ \text{Perplexity} = P(w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_N)^{-\frac{1}{N}} \]

Equivalently:

\[ \text{Perplexity} = \sqrt[N]{\frac{1}{P(w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_N)}} \]

Where \(P(w_1, \ldots, w_N)\) is the sequence probability from your language model (bigram or trigram).

Worked perplexity example.

If your model assigns probability 0.001 to a test sentence of 5 words:

\[ \text{Perplexity} = (0.001)^{-1/5} = (1000)^{0.2} \approx 3.98 \]

The model is choosing among roughly 4 words — fairly good.

Sense-check: If the model gave probability 1.0 (perfect prediction), perplexity = 1 — the model is never confused. If the model gave uniform probability \(1/V\) to each word, perplexity = \(V\) — as confused as possible.

Practical note on smoothing: When computing perplexity, use probabilities from your model after handling zeros. Most real-world systems use interpolation rather than Laplace smoothing for language modeling. For an exam, use whatever smoothing is specified or state your assumption clearly.

Student Q&A — Perplexity with smoothed vs. unsmoothed probabilities: Always compute perplexity on the model as it will be used. If you applied Laplace smoothing, use the smoothed probabilities. If you used backoff, use the backoff probabilities. The perplexity reflects the model the user will actually interact with.

N-gram language models are simple, count-based, and surprisingly effective. They turn the chain rule into something computable via the Markov assumption. Their Achilles' heel is the zero-probability problem, which smoothing, interpolation, and backoff all try to solve. Perplexity gives you a single number to compare models — lower is better.

Domain Connection. N-gram models were the backbone of speech recognition (Siri, Alexa), machine translation (Google Translate pre-2016), and spell-checkers for decades. Even today, they're used as fast baselines and in resource-constrained settings. The concept of "predicting the next word given context" is also the exact training objective of GPT-style LLMs — they just use transformers instead of count tables.

8.5 Neural Language Modeling

8.5.1 From N-grams to Neural Networks

Hook. N-gram models have a hard ceiling: they can only memorize word pairs they've seen. "The cat sat on the mat" → known. "The feline sat on the mat" → unknown (zero probability). A neural network can generalize: "feline" has a similar embedding to "cat," so it gets a similar probability. How?

A neural language model applies neural network techniques to the language modeling problem. The key advantage: word embeddings allow the model to generalize to unseen word combinations.

8.5.2 Feedforward Network Structure

A basic feedforward neural network has:

  1. Input layer — word embeddings (not raw tokens) for the context words
  2. Hidden layer(s) — one or more layers with activation functions
  3. Output layer — a probability distribution over the vocabulary

The computation is a chain of: sum of products → activation → sum of products → activation → ...

8.5.3 Why Softmax at the Output

Language modeling is a multi-class problem — you are choosing the next word from the entire vocabulary (thousands of classes).

Therefore, the output layer must use softmax, not sigmoid:

\[ \text{softmax}(z_i) = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} \]

Softmax converts raw scores into a probability distribution that sums to 1.

Sigmoid is for binary classification (e.g., sentiment analysis: positive vs. negative).

Common trap — Sigmoid vs. Softmax: Sigmoid gives independent probabilities per class (sum ≠ 1). Softmax gives a proper distribution (sum = 1). Language modeling is "pick ONE next word" → softmax. Sentiment analysis is "is this positive? is this negative? is this neutral?" — if mutually exclusive, softmax; if multi-label, sigmoid per label.

Easy rule: One-right-answer → softmax. Multiple-right-answers → sigmoid per answer.

8.5.4 Key Activation Functions to Know

  • Sigmoid: \(\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\) — binary classification output
  • Softmax: multi-class output
  • Other activation functions (ReLU, tanh, etc.) are used in hidden layers but you do not need to memorize them for this module's exam

8.5.5 Word Embeddings as Input

You cannot feed raw text tokens into a neural network. You must convert each token ID to its word embedding vector first. These embeddings can be:

  • Pre-trained (e.g., from Word2Vec or GloVe)
  • Learned jointly with the language model task

8.5.6 Sentence Embedding

To represent an entire sentence, combine the word embeddings for its constituent words:

  • Mean pooling: average all word vectors
  • Max pooling: take the maximum value per dimension across all word vectors

8.5.7 Pros and Cons of Neural Language Models

Advantages over n-gram models:

  • Handle unseen words and contexts through embeddings (n-gram models give zero probability to unseen sequences)
  • Dense representations capture semantic similarity
  • Can model longer-range dependencies

Disadvantages:

  • Computationally more expensive to train
  • Require more data
  • Less interpretable than count-based models

8.5.8 Large Language Models (LLMs)

Large Language Models are neural language models with:

  • A very large number of parameters (billions)
  • Training on massive text corpora
  • Transformer architecture (not simple feedforward, but still neural networks)

Key concepts:

  • Pre-training: Train on a huge general corpus to learn language patterns
  • Fine-tuning: Further train on a smaller, task-specific dataset to adapt to a particular domain or task
  • Hugging Face: A platform hosting many open-source pre-trained models available for download and fine-tuning

8.5.9 Prompt Engineering

Prompt engineering is the practice of crafting input prompts to get desired outputs from an LLM without retraining:

  • Zero-shot: Give the model a task description with no examples. "Classify this review as positive or negative."
  • Few-shot: Give the model a few examples of the task before the actual input. "Here are 3 examples of classification. Now classify this one."

8.5.10 Generative AI

Generative AI means the model generates new content — not just classifies or extracts. Given a prompt, it creates:

  • Text (chatbots, story writing)
  • Images (text-to-image generation)
  • Video (text-to-video generation)
  • Code (coding agents that produce entire programs)

The core technology across all these modalities is neural networks — the same principles studied in this module.

Neural language models fix the fundamental limitation of n-gram models: generalization. By embedding words in a continuous space, similar words get similar predictions. LLMs scale this idea to billions of parameters, trained on internet-scale text. The output layer is always softmax (choosing one next word), the training objective is always "predict the next token," and the magic is all in the embeddings and attention mechanisms learned during pre-training.

Domain Connection. The neural language model architecture described here — embed → feedforward → softmax — is the conceptual ancestor of GPT-4, Claude, and every modern chatbot. The difference is scale. GPT-4 uses the same "predict next token" objective with a transformer architecture. It has 1.7 trillion parameters and trains on trillions of tokens. The softmax at GPT-4's output chooses from ~100,000 tokens, just like the feedforward network chooses from V words.

8.6 Cross-Cutting Themes

8.6.1 Always Write Your Assumptions

In any exam problem, if something is ambiguous:

  1. State your assumption clearly
  2. Write the formula you are using
  3. Show your work step by step

This applies to: which log base, whether to include start/end tokens, which smoothing method, the convention for bigram table rows vs. columns.

8.6.2 Use Log Probabilities for Sequence Computation

When multiplying many small probabilities, use log probabilities to avoid underflow:

\[ \log P(w_1, \ldots, w_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log P(w_i \mid \text{context}) \]

This turns multiplication into addition, which is faster and numerically stable.

8.6.3 The Recurring Pattern: Embed → Compute Similarity → Classify

Across NLP methods, a pattern repeats:

  1. Represent words as vectors (embedding)
  2. Compute similarity or dot product between vectors
  3. Use the similarity score for classification or prediction

This pattern appears in:

  • SGNS: embed → dot product → sigmoid → binary context prediction
  • Cosine similarity: embed → dot product / norms → similarity score
  • Neural language model: embed → feedforward → softmax → next-word prediction

Visual Intuition — The NLP Architecture Zoo, Unified.

Method Embedding Similarity Measure Output
TF-IDF Sparse count vector Cosine similarity Document ranking
SGNS Dense 300d vector Dot product → sigmoid Binary (context / not)
Neural LM Dense embedding (learned) Feedforward layers → softmax Next-word distribution
LLM (GPT) Dense embedding (learned) Transformer attention → softmax Next-token distribution

The embed → compare → classify pattern is the DNA of NLP. Once you see it, every new architecture becomes a variation on this theme.

Domain Connection. Understanding the embed → compare → classify pattern makes you architecture-agnostic. Build a RAG system: embed queries and documents, compare via cosine, classify relevance. Fine-tune BERT for sentiment: embed input, compare via attention, classify via softmax. Design a recommendation system: embed users and items, compare via dot product, rank. The pattern is the same. Master it once, apply it everywhere.

NLP Definition, Ambiguities, and Levels of Understanding

Must-know: NLP builds systems to understand and generate human language via an encoder-decoder loop. The five ambiguity types: lexical (word-level, e.g., "bank"), syntactic (structure-level, e.g., "I saw a man with a telescope"), semantic (meaning-level, e.g., "The chicken is ready to eat"), pragmatic (intent-level, e.g., "Can you pass the salt?"), and referential (pronoun-level, e.g., "John told Mike he was tired"). The seven levels stack from phonology through pragmatics. Modern LLMs reach the discourse level via attention mechanisms.

\[ \text{F1} = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} \quad \text{where} \quad \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \; \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Confusing discourse (how sentences connect via coreference) with pragmatics (why the speaker said something — intent and implicature). Also conflating document frequency (binary: does the term appear?) with collection frequency (total occurrences across all docs).

Self-check: Classify the ambiguity: "I saw her duck." Is it lexical, syntactic, or both? Can you give an original example of pragmatic ambiguity?

Vector Semantics — TF-IDF and Cosine Similarity

Must-know: TF-IDF combines term frequency (how often a word appears in a document, log-squashed with \(\log_{10}\)) with inverse document frequency (how rare the word is across the corpus). IDF uses document frequency (binary yes/no per document), not collection frequency. Cosine similarity normalizes dot product by vector lengths: \(\cos(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{||\mathbf{a}|| \cdot ||\mathbf{b}||}\). Result near 1 = similar, near 0 = unrelated.

\[ \text{tf-idf}_{t,d} = \log_{10}(\text{count}(t,d)+1) \times \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{N}{\text{df}_t}\right) \qquad \text{cosine}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{||\mathbf{a}|| \cdot ||\mathbf{b}||} \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Using collection frequency instead of document frequency in the IDF denominator. Collection frequency counts every occurrence; document frequency only checks presence/absence per document. Also: forgetting the +1 inside the log for TF to avoid \(\log(0)\).

Self-check: Why does "the" get a TF-IDF of zero even though it has high term frequency? What happens to cosine similarity if you double every element of one vector?

Connects to: Cosine similarity, Sparse vector problem, Dense embeddings (Word2Vec)

Word2Vec — Skip-gram Negative Sampling (SGNS)

Must-know: SGNS is a self-supervised algorithm that learns dense word embeddings by training a binary classifier. For each target word, words within a ±k window are positive (target=1). K randomly sampled words outside the window are negative (target=0). The loss is cross-entropy with K negatives. Each word gets two embeddings (target and context). Partial derivatives follow: (predicted − actual) × vector.

\[ L = -\log\sigma(c_{\text{pos}} \cdot w) - \sum_{i=1}^{K} \log\sigma(-c_{\text{neg}_i} \cdot w) \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = [\sigma(c_{\text{pos}} \cdot w) - 1] \cdot c_{\text{pos}} + \sum_{i=1}^{K} \sigma(c_{\text{neg}_i} \cdot w) \cdot c_{\text{neg}_i} \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Using the same sigmoid formula for both positive and negative context words. For negatives, the probability is \(P(-|w,c) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{c \cdot w}}\) — the exponent sign flips. The target values are fixed (1 for positive, 0 for negative) and come from the window data, not from the formula.

Self-check: In SGNS, why do we need negative samples? What would happen if we only trained on positive context pairs? How does the learning rate \(\eta\) affect the weight update?

Connects to: Self-supervised learning, One-hot encoding to dense embedding, Cosine similarity, Neural language modeling

N-gram Language Modeling and Smoothing

Must-know: An n-gram language model predicts the next word using the Markov assumption — the current word depends only on the previous k words. Bigram: \(P(w_i|w_{i-1})\); Trigram: \(P(w_i|w_{i-2},w_{i-1})\). Zero-probability events are handled by Laplace smoothing (add 1 to every count, add V to denominator), interpolation (weighted combination of trigram, bigram, unigram with \(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3=1\)), or backoff (trigram → bigram → unigram hierarchy). Perplexity = \(P(w_1,\ldots,w_N)^{-1/N}\) — lower is better; perplexity of 1 means perfect prediction.

\[ P_{\text{Laplace}}(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = \frac{\text{count}(w_{i-1},w_i) + 1}{\text{count}(w_{i-1}) + V} \qquad \text{Perplexity} = P(w_1,\ldots,w_N)^{-1/N} \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Laplace smoothing distorts the true distribution by adding too much probability mass to unseen events. Also: computing perplexity with unsmoothed probabilities when the model uses smoothing — always use the probabilities the model will actually produce. Confusing rows and columns in bigram tables: rows = first word (context), columns = next word (predicted).

Self-check: If a bigram model assigns probability 0.01 to a 3-word test sentence, what is the perplexity? What does a perplexity of 1 mean? What about a perplexity equal to the vocabulary size V?

Connects to: Chain rule of probability, Markov assumption, Neural language modeling, Evaluation metrics

Neural Language Models and LLMs

Must-know: Neural language models use word embeddings and feedforward networks. The output layer uses softmax (not sigmoid) because language modeling is multi-class. LLMs scale this to billions of parameters with transformer architectures. They are pre-trained on massive corpora and fine-tuned for specific tasks. Zero-shot = task description with no examples. Few-shot = task description with a few examples. Generative AI creates text, images, video, or code from prompts.

\[ \text{softmax}(z_i) = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Using sigmoid instead of softmax at the output layer for language modeling. Sigmoid gives independent probabilities per class (sum ≠ 1); softmax gives a proper probability distribution (sum = 1). Language modeling is "pick ONE next word" → softmax. Also: thinking zero-shot means no instructions — it means no examples; the task description is still provided.

The Embed-Compare-Classify Pattern

Must-know: Every major NLP architecture follows the same pattern. Embed words as vectors. Compute similarity between vectors (dot product, cosine, or attention). Classify or predict based on that similarity. This unifies TF-IDF, SGNS, neural LMs, and transformer LLMs. Always write assumptions clearly in exams: log base, table convention, smoothing method.

\[ \text{TF-IDF: sparse count} \rightarrow \text{cosine} \rightarrow \text{rank} \qquad \text{SGNS: dense embed} \rightarrow \text{dot+sigmoid} \rightarrow \text{binary} \qquad \text{LLM: dense embed} \rightarrow \text{attention+softmax} \rightarrow \text{next-token} \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Treating each NLP method as a completely separate technique rather than recognizing the shared embed-compare-classify pattern. Understanding this pattern makes every new architecture a variation on a theme. Also: not writing down assumptions clearly in exam problems — always state your log base, table convention, and smoothing method.
