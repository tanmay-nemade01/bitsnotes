Midsem Solved.1.1 Question 1

(a) A search engine retrieves documents containing the keyword "bank" but cannot determine whether the user means "financial institution" or "river bank." Which level of language analysis is required? Why? [1 Mark]

(b) Consider the following documents consisting of one sentence each:

  • Document 1: "Machine learning improves prediction accuracy."
  • Document 2: "Prediction models use machine learning techniques."

Convert all the words to lowercase. Do not remove stop words, perform lemmatization, stemming, etc.

  1. Calculate Term Frequency (TF) and Document Frequency (DF) for all terms. [2 Marks]
  2. Compute the TF-IDF vector for each document. [2 Marks]

Note: For Term Frequency, use the simple formula: TF = (Word Frequency) / (Document Length in words).

Detailed Solution

Midsem Solved.1.1.1 Concept Primer: Language Analysis Levels & TF-IDF

Levels of Language Analysis (from low to high): 1. Phonological: Sounds and pronunciation rules 2. Morphological: Word structure (prefixes, suffixes, roots) 3. Syntactic: Grammar and sentence structure (parts of speech, phrase structure) 4. Semantic: Meaning of words and sentences (what does "bank" mean here?) 5. Pragmatic: Context-dependent interpretation (sarcasm, intent, speech acts)

When a word has multiple meanings (ambiguity), you need semantic analysis — looking at context to figure out which meaning is intended.

TF-IDF is a technique to measure how important a word is to a document in a collection: - TF (Term Frequency): How often does the word appear in this document? (More frequent = more important to this document) - DF (Document Frequency): How many documents contain this word? (If a word appears in every document, it's not very useful for distinguishing them) - IDF (Inverse Document Frequency): log(N/DF) — words that appear in fewer documents get higher IDF (they're more distinctive) - TF-IDF = TF × IDF — combines how frequent the word is in the document with how rare it is across the collection

Midsem Solved.1.1.2 (a) Which level of language analysis is required for "bank"? [1 Mark]

Answer:

The word "bank" could mean a financial institution or a river bank. This is a classic case of lexical ambiguity / word sense disambiguation (WSD).

The appropriate level of language analysis is Semantic Analysis, because semantics deals with the meaning of words and phrases. The search engine must look at the surrounding context (e.g., "money" vs "river") to disambiguate which sense of "bank" is intended. Syntactic or morphological analysis alone cannot resolve this.

Midsem Solved.1.1.3 (b) TF-IDF Calculation [4 Marks]

We're building a TF-IDF vector for each document. This is a common way to represent text as numbers so that machines can work with it. Each document becomes a vector where each dimension corresponds to a word in the vocabulary, and the value is the TF-IDF score for that word in that document.

Why TF-IDF instead of just counting words? Because common words like "the" or "is" appear everywhere and don't help distinguish documents. TF-IDF downweights words that appear in many documents (low IDF) and upweights words that are frequent in one document but rare overall (high TF × high IDF).

Midsem Solved.1.2 Question 2

(a) An e-commerce company wants to build a product team building smart email typing assistant. One engineer suggests using a simple bigram model while another recommends a Neural Language Model. You are asked to decide which approach to use. Design a Neural Language Model solution for this task and justify why it is better than the bigram model. [3 Marks]

(b) A tutoring system uses an LLM to solve mathematical word problems. A student asks: "A train travels 240 km in 4 hours. What is its average speed?" Why would Chain-of-Thought prompting be useful in this scenario? [2 Marks]

Detailed Solution

Midsem Solved.1.2.1 Concept Primer: Language Models

A language model predicts the probability of the next word given the previous words. For example, after "I look forward to", a good language model assigns high probability to "hearing" or "seeing" and low probability to "elephant."

Two main approaches: - N-gram models: Count how often word sequences appear in training data. A bigram model looks only at the previous word. Simple but limited — can't capture long-range relationships. - Neural language models: Use neural networks (LSTM, Transformer) to learn dense representations of words and capture dependencies across the entire sentence. More powerful but require more data and computation.

Midsem Solved.1.2.2 (a) Bigram vs Neural Language Model for Smart Email Typing Assistant [3 Marks]

Answer:

I would recommend the Neural Language Model over a simple bigram model. Here is the design and justification.

Neural Language Model Design:

  1. Architecture: A lightweight Transformer-based or LSTM-based language model (e.g., a distilled GPT-style model)
  2. Input: Sequence of previous words/tokens typed so far
  3. Output: Probability distribution over the vocabulary for the next word
  4. Training: Fine-tune on a large corpus of email text (Enron Email Dataset, personal emails) to capture email-specific language patterns
  5. Integration: The model runs as a service; as the user types each keystroke, the top-k predicted words are shown as suggestions

Why Neural LM is better than Bigram:

Aspect Bigram Model Neural LM
Context Only looks at the previous 1 word Captures long-range dependencies (entire sentence or paragraph context)
Sparsity Highly sparse — most bigrams unseen in training data Dense embeddings generalise to unseen contexts
Grammar Cannot model complex grammar (e.g., subject-verb agreement across distance) Can model complex syntactic structures
Email-specific patterns Cannot capture email conventions like salutations, sign-offs, or topic shifts Learns email-specific structure from training data
Typo handling No mechanism; unseen bigrams give zero probability Embedding space allows similar words to substitute for typos

A bigram model suggesting the next word after "I look forward to" would only know what follows "to" on average, missing the crucial "look forward" context. The Neural LM captures all three words and gives a much better prediction like "hearing."

Midsem Solved.1.2.3 (b) Chain-of-Thought Prompting for Math Word Problems [2 Marks]

Answer:

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting is useful because:

  1. Intermediate reasoning steps: CoT instructs the LLM to break the problem into logical sub-steps (e.g., "Step 1: Identify the formula. Step 2: Plug in values. Step 3: Compute."). For the given problem, the CoT output would look like:

Step 1: Average speed = Total distance / Total time Step 2: Distance = 240 km, Time = 4 hours Step 3: Speed = 240 / 4 = 60 km/h

  1. Reduces arithmetic errors: By explicitly showing each calculation step, the model can self-correct and the user can verify intermediate results.

  2. Generalisation: CoT helps the model handle multi-step problems that would be difficult to answer in a single forward pass (e.g., compound interest, rate-of-work problems).

  3. Interpretability: The student (and tutoring system) can see the reasoning path, enabling better feedback and error diagnosis.

Detailed Solution — Skip-gram Model Calculations [5 Marks]

Midsem Solved.1.3.1 Concept Primer: Word Embeddings and Skip-gram

Word embeddings are dense, low-dimensional vector representations of words where words with similar meanings have similar vectors. Instead of representing "love" as a one-hot vector [0, 1, 0] (which says nothing about its meaning), an embedding like [0.5, 0.1] captures semantic information.

Skip-gram is a method (from Word2Vec) to learn these embeddings. The core idea: "A word's meaning is defined by the words that appear near it." So we train a simple neural network to predict context words (surrounding words) from a centre word.

How it works: 1. Take a sentence and pick a centre word 2. The "context words" are the words within a window around the centre word 3. Train the model to predict context words from the centre word 4. After training, the learned weight matrices contain the word embeddings

Weight matrices: - W (input → hidden): Each row is the embedding for one vocabulary word. This is what we ultimately want. - W' (hidden → output): Maps the embedding to scores for each possible context word. Used during training but discarded after.

Given: - Sentence: "We love NLP" - Window size: 1 - Vocabulary V = {We, love, NLP} - Embedding dimension = 2 - Centre word: "love"

Weight matrices:

W (input→hidden) — shape 3×2
     love  NLP
  [0.2, 0.4]    ← We
  [0.5, 0.1]    ← love
  [0.3, 0.6]    ← NLP

W' (hidden→output) — shape 2×3
  [0.1, 0.3, 0.2]   ← hidden dim 1 → (We, love, NLP)
  [0.4, 0.2, 0.5]   ← hidden dim 2 → (We, love, NLP)

Midsem Solved.1.3.2 (a) Centre-Context Training Pairs [1 Mark]

With window size = 1, for each centre word, the context words are the 1 word before and 1 word after.

For the sentence "We love NLP":

We love
love We, NLP

The centre-context pairs (centre → context) are: 1. We → love 2. love → We ← (this is the one centred on "love") 3. love → NLP ← (this is the one centred on "love") 4. NLP → love

The input is a one-hot vector. The vocabulary order is: {We=0, love=1, NLP=2}

One-hot vector for "love" = [0, 1, 0]

The hidden layer is computed as: h = x^T · W = row corresponding to "love" in W.

From W:

  [0.2, 0.4]    ← We (index 0)
  [0.5, 0.1]    ← love (index 1)
  [0.3, 0.6]    ← NLP (index 2)

Output scores: u = h · W'

h = [0.5, 0.1]
W' = [0.1, 0.3, 0.2]
     [0.4, 0.2, 0.5]

For "love" (column 1): u_love = 0.5 × 0.3 + 0.1 × 0.2 = 0.15 + 0.02 = 0.17

For "NLP" (column 2): u_nlp = 0.5 × 0.2 + 0.1 × 0.5 = 0.10 + 0.05 = 0.15

u = [0.09, 0.17, 0.15]

(If Softmax is applied: probabilities = [0.328, 0.356, 0.347] for We, love, NLP respectively. Softmax converts raw scores into a valid probability distribution that sums to 1.)

Midsem Solved.1.4 Question 4

A query-completion model has trained unigram, bigram, and trigram models. For the target word "food," the maximum-likelihood estimates are:

Estimate Context Value
Unigram P(food) — 0.05
Bigram P(food | thai) "thai" 0.40
Bigram P(food | indian) "indian" 0.30
Trigram P(food | priced thai) "priced thai" 0.60
Trigram P(food | priced indian) "priced indian" (unseen)

The models are combined by linear interpolation with weights λ₁ = 0.1 (unigram), λ₂ = 0.3 (bigram), λ₃ = 0.6 (trigram).

(a) Compute the interpolated probability of "food" after the context "priced thai." [1 Mark]

(b) For the unseen context "priced indian," the trigram model gives probability 0. Compute the interpolated estimate P_int(food | priced indian) and say in one line what interpolation achieved. [2 Marks]

(c) If simple back-off were used instead, what estimate would it give for P(food | priced indian)? [2 Marks]

Detailed Solution — Language Model Interpolation and Backoff [5 Marks]

Midsem Solved.1.4.1 Concept Primer: N-gram Models and the Zero-Probability Problem

N-gram models estimate the probability of a word given the previous (n−1) words. A bigram model uses 1 previous word; a trigram model uses 2 previous words. Higher-order models capture more context but suffer from the sparse data problem — many word combinations never appear in the training data, giving them a probability of 0.

Interpolation solves this by blending estimates from multiple n-gram models:

\[P_{\text{interp}}(w | \text{context}) = \lambda_1 \cdot P_{\text{unigram}}(w) + \lambda_2 \cdot P_{\text{bigram}}(w | \text{last}) + \lambda_3 \cdot P_{\text{trigram}}(w | \text{context})\]

The λ values (which sum to 1) control how much each model contributes. Typically, the highest-order model (trigram) gets the most weight.

Backoff takes a different approach: use the highest-order model if it has data; otherwise, "fall back" to a lower-order model.

Key difference: Interpolation always blends all models. Backoff only falls back when the higher-order model has no data.

Midsem Solved.1.4.2 (a) Interpolated probability for "priced thai" [1 Mark]

Formula: P_interp(food | context) = λ₁·P_unigram(food) + λ₂·P_bigram(food | last) + λ₃·P_trigram(food | context)

Given: λ₁ = 0.1, λ₂ = 0.3, λ₃ = 0.6

For context "priced thai": - P_unigram(food) = 0.05 - P_bigram(food | thai) = 0.40 - P_trigram(food | priced thai) = 0.60

P_interp(food | priced thai) = 0.1 × 0.05 + 0.3 × 0.40 + 0.6 × 0.60 = 0.005 + 0.12 + 0.36 = 0.485

Midsem Solved.1.4.3 (b) Interpolated estimate for "priced indian" [2 Marks]

For context "priced indian": - P_unigram(food) = 0.05 - P_bigram(food | indian) = 0.30 - P_trigram(food | priced indian) = 0 (unseen)

P_interp(food | priced indian) = 0.1 × 0.05 + 0.3 × 0.30 + 0.6 × 0.0 = 0.005 + 0.09 + 0.0 = 0.095

What interpolation achieved: Even though the trigram estimate is zero (unseen context), the model still assigns a non-zero probability of 0.095 by "backing off" to lower-order n-gram estimates, thereby avoiding the zero-probability problem and ensuring the model can still make predictions for unseen word sequences.

Midsem Solved.1.4.4 (c) Simple Backoff for "priced indian" [2 Marks]

Simple (Stupid) Backoff: If the trigram count is zero, fall back to the bigram probability, multiplied by a backoff factor α (typically 0.4).

P_backoff(food | priced indian) = α × P_bigram(food | indian)

Using α = 0.4: P_backoff(food | priced indian) = 0.4 × 0.30 = 0.12

(If α is not specified, the standard Katz backoff factor is often 0.4.)

Key difference from interpolation: Backoff only uses the lower-order model when the higher-order model fails (zero count), while interpolation always combines all n-gram estimates. Backoff typically gives lower (but still non-zero) probabilities for unseen sequences.

Detailed Solution — Viterbi Algorithm for POS Tagging [6 Marks]

Midsem Solved.1.5.1 Concept Primer: POS Tagging and the Viterbi Algorithm

POS (Part-of-Speech) Tagging assigns a grammatical tag (noun, verb, adjective, etc.) to each word in a sentence. For example: "the/DT small/JJ cat/NN chases/VB mouse/NN."

Hidden Markov Model (HMM) for POS tagging assumes: - Each word is "generated" by a hidden tag (we observe words, not tags) - Each tag depends only on the previous tag (Markov assumption) - Each word depends only on its tag (emission probability)

The Viterbi Algorithm finds the most likely sequence of tags for a given sentence. It's a dynamic programming algorithm that works by: 1. Forward pass: For each word position, compute the probability of the best path ending at each possible tag. Store the maximum probability and a "backpointer" to the previous tag. 2. Backtrace: Start from the tag with the highest probability at the last word, and follow backpointers backwards to reconstruct the full optimal tag sequence.

Why Viterbi instead of brute force? With T words and K tags, brute force would try K^T sequences (exponential). Viterbi computes K × T × K = O(K²T) values (polynomial), making it practical.

Detailed Solution — Experimental Study: Statistical vs Neural POS Tagger [4 Marks]

Midsem Solved.1.6.1 Concept Primer: Comparing POS Tagging Approaches

Two main approaches to POS tagging:

  1. Statistical (HMM) tagger: Uses transition and emission probabilities learned from data. Fast, lightweight, but limited by the Markov assumption (each tag depends only on the previous 1–2 tags) and sparse data for rare words.

  2. Neural (Bi-LSTM/Transformer) tagger: Uses contextual embeddings that capture information from the entire sentence (both left and right context). Better at handling rare words (via subword features), ambiguity, and domain-specific language — but requires more data and computation.

To compare them experimentally, we need a proper experimental design: a labelled dataset, appropriate metrics, statistical significance testing, and analysis of failure modes.

Midsem Solved.1.6.2 Answer:

1. Dataset Required

A labelled corpus with gold-standard POS tags is needed: - Primary dataset: Penn Treebank (WSJ section) — the standard benchmark for POS tagging (~1M words, 45+ tagset) - Domain-specific dataset: Customer service chat logs (e-commerce queries like "Show me running shoes", "Track my order") manually annotated with POS tags — to test real-world chatbot performance - Train/Dev/Test split: 80% / 10% / 10% stratified split ensuring all tag types appear in each set

2. Evaluation Metrics

Metric Purpose
Accuracy Percentage of correctly tagged tokens (most common metric)
Precision, Recall, F1-score Per-tag performance, especially for rare tags like proper nouns or foreign words
OOV (Out-of-Vocabulary) Accuracy Accuracy on words not seen in training — critical for chatbot queries with product names
Inference Time (ms/token) Latency — crucial for real-time chatbot responses
Model Size (MB) Storage/memory footprint

3. How Performance Will Be Compared

  • Cross-validation: 5-fold cross-validation on the training set to measure stability
  • Statistical significance test: McNemar's test or paired bootstrap to determine if accuracy differences are significant (p < 0.05)
  • Error analysis: Manually examine 100 misclassified examples from each model to identify systematic errors (e.g., does the HMM tagger confuse NN and VB more often for short words?)
  • OOV robustness test: Measure accuracy specifically on words not present in the training vocabulary
  • Latency benchmark: Average inference time across 10,000 sentences measured on the same hardware (CPU/GPU)

4. Expected Advantages of Neural POS Taggers

Advantage Explanation
Contextual embeddings Neural taggers (Bi-LSTM/Transformer) use full sentence context in both directions, unlike HMM which only sees the previous 1-2 tags via Markov assumption
OOV handling Character-level or subword (BPE/WordPiece) processing allows neural models to tag unseen words by their morphology (e.g., "unhackable" → JJ even if never seen)
No feature engineering HMM requires manual feature design; neural models learn features automatically from data
Robust to domain shift Pre-trained transformers (BERT, RoBERTa) fine-tune easily to e-commerce/customer service domains with minimal labelled data
Ambiguity resolution Neural models leverage deep semantic context — for "Track my order," they correctly tag "order" as NN (not VB) even in ambiguous positions

Bottom line: For the e-commerce chatbot, the Neural POS tagger would deliver higher accuracy (especially on product names and informal language), better OOV handling, and faster domain adaptation — at the cost of larger model size and higher computational requirements.
