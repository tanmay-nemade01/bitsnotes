Lecture Summary

This session covers the evolution from sparse to dense word embeddings — one of the most important shifts in modern NLP. Sparse representations like TF-IDF and Bag of Words produce vectors whose length equals the entire vocabulary size, resulting in mostly-zero entries that are computationally expensive and poor at capturing nuanced meaning.

To overcome these limitations, the lecture introduces dense vector representations through Word2Vec, focusing on the Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW) and Skip-Gram architectures. The Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS) model receives special attention due to its popularity in production systems. Students walk through the mathematical intuition behind gradient descent and how it drives weight updates — a concept fundamental to virtually all modern deep learning.

The session also draws an important distinction between static and contextual embeddings. In static embeddings, a word like 'bank' gets one fixed vector regardless of context. Contextual embeddings (like those in BERT and GPT) dynamically adjust based on surrounding words — a topic that sets the stage for the Transformer modules after midterm.

The Need for Dense Word Embeddings

Why Represent Words as Vectors?

Computers cannot process raw text directly. Every string of letters must be converted into a numeric format — a vector — before any machine learning model can operate on it. The fundamental motivation for representing words as vectors is:

  1. Similarity computation: Vector representations allow us to mathematically compute how "similar" two words are (e.g., using cosine similarity or dot product).
  2. Semantic representation: A good vector representation captures the meaning of a word, not just its spelling. Words that appear in similar contexts should have similar vectors.
  3. Downstream tasks: Once words are vectors, we can feed them into classifiers, neural networks, and transformer models for tasks like sentiment analysis, machine translation, and question answering.
Key Concept: Word embeddings are dense, low-dimensional vector representations of words that capture semantic meaning. The term "embedding" comes from the idea that we embed a word from a discrete, high-dimensional space (like one-hot) into a continuous, lower-dimensional space.

Two Paradigms: Frequency-Based vs. Prediction-Based

Word embedding techniques fall into two broad families:

Frequency-Based (Sparse) Representations

In Natural Language Processing (NLP), computers require numerical representations of words.

Traditional methods, such as One-Hot Encoding, treat words as atomic symbols (e.g., "cat"=[1,

0, 0, ...], "dog"=[0, 1, 0, ...]). This approach fails completely to capture semantic relationships;

the similarity (dot product) between "cat" and "dog" is zero, the same as the similarity between

"cat" and "philosophy".

Word Embeddings solve this by representing words as dense, low-dimensional vectors of

real numbers. The positioning of these vectors in a high-dimensional space captures meaning,

such that similar words are physically close.

This concept is rooted in the Distributional Hypothesis (J.R. Firth, 1957):

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps."

Words appearing in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings.

The Skip-Gram model, part of the Word2Vec family introduced by Mikolov et al. (2013), is

a highly influential technique for learning these embeddings by analyzing the contexts in which

words appear across a large corpus.

These methods count word occurrences and build vectors from those counts:

  • Count Vector / Bag-of-Words
  • TF-IDF (Term Frequency – Inverse Document Frequency)
  • Co-occurrence matrices (counting words within \(\pm 4\) context windows)
Downstream Relevance: While dense embeddings dominate modern NLP, sparse representations remain essential. In Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD), supervised classifiers combine two types of sparse features: collocational features (words at exact positions relative to the target, e.g., "the word immediately to the right is player" for disambiguating bass as music vs. fish) and bag-of-words features (unordered context words like fishing, guitar, sound that capture the topic domain). The Lesk algorithm — a classic dictionary-based WSD method — directly exploits bag-of-words overlap: it selects the WordNet sense whose dictionary gloss shares the most words with the target word's surrounding context. This is exactly the kind of shallow but effective application of sparse count vectors that Word2Vec was designed to improve upon.

Prediction-Based (Dense) Representations

These methods learn vector representations by training a model to predict context words:

  • Word2Vec — Skip-Gram and CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words)
  • GloVe (Global Vectors for Word Representation)
  • Contextual embeddings (BERT, GPT, etc.)
Note: In modern NLP systems (transformers, LLMs, agentic AI), a hybrid approach is often used — combining sparse (frequency-based) retrieval with dense (embedding-based) retrieval. This hybrid retrieval covers both in-vocabulary words (via embeddings) and out-of-vocabulary words (via TF-IDF or BM25).

The Problem with Sparse Representations

Recall from the previous lecture that frequency-based representations suffer from two critical problems:

High Dimensionality

If your corpus has \(V = 100{,}000\) unique words (vocabulary size), then every word vector has dimension \(V = 100{,}000\). This is extremely long and computationally expensive.

Sparsity

Most entries in such vectors are zero. Consider the sentence: "The cat sat on the mat." In a vocabulary of 100{,}000 words, a vector for "cat" might look like: \[ [0,\,0,\,0,\,\ldots,\,0,\,1,\,0,\,\ldots,\,0] \] with 99{,}999 zeros and a single 1. This is highly inefficient and makes it difficult to compute meaningful similarities.

Key Concept: The goal of dense word embeddings: Compress each word vector from dimension \(V\) (vocabulary size, e.g., 100{,}000) to dimension \(D\) (a small number, e.g., 300), where most entries are non-zero — hence the term "dense."

Word2Vec: Overview and Intuition

What is Word2Vec?

Word2Vec is a pioneering technique introduced by Google researchers (Mikolov et al., 2013) that learns dense vector representations of words from large corpora. The name "Word2Vec" literally means "words to vectors."

Key Concept: The key idea behind Word2Vec is elegantly simple: You shall know a word by the company it keeps (J.R. Firth, 1957). Words that appear in similar contexts should have similar vector representations. This is the distributional hypothesis — the foundational principle of lexical semantics that underlies all modern word embedding techniques.
Lexical Semantics Connection: Word2Vec operates at the level of wordforms (surface strings like "bank" or "tables"), but meaning lives at the level of lemmas (dictionary headwords like sing for sang/sung) and word senses (distinct meanings of a lemma). A lemma is the canonical citation form of a word — for instance, the lemma for carpets is carpet, and the lemma for sang, sung is sing. A word sense is one discrete aspect of a lemma's meaning — for example, the lemma bank has a "financial institution" sense and a "sloping mound" sense. Lemmatization is the process of mapping a wordform to its lemma, which is not always deterministic (e.g., found maps to either find or found depending on context). Word2Vec learns one vector per wordform, collapsing all senses into a single representation — a limitation that contextual embeddings later address.

Word2Vec comes in two variants:

  • Skip-Gram: Given a target word, predict its surrounding context words.
  • CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words): Given context words, predict the target word.

This lecture focuses primarily on Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS), which is the most widely used variant in industry.

Note: Word2Vec was a foundational breakthrough. While today's LLMs use more advanced techniques (transformers, attention), the input to a transformer is still fundamentally a word embedding — and understanding Word2Vec is essential to understanding how modern NLP models represent language.

Self-Supervised Learning

The Core Skip-Gram Model

The Objective: Predicting the Context

The Skip-Gram model learns embeddings by training a neural network on a "fake" task. The

task is: Given a central target word (w), predict the surrounding context words (c).

Consider the sentence: "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog."

If our target word is "fox" and our window size is 2, the training pairs generated are:

  • (fox, quick), (fox, brown), (fox, jumps), (fox, over)

The goal is to adjust the word vectors such that the model assigns a high probability to the

words that actually appear near "fox". We don’t care about the prediction task itself; we care

about the internal representations the network learns in the process.

The Architecture: A Shallow Neural Network

The model is a shallow neural network with one hidden layer.

Architecture Focus: The Shallow Network Structure

Let V be the vocabulary size (e.g., 100,000) and D be the embedding dimension (e.g.,

300).

  • Input Layer: A One-Hot Encoded vector representing the target word w. Size

(1 × V ).

  • Input Weight Matrix (W): The primary embedding matrix. Size (V × D).
  • Hidden Layer (Projection): The result of the multiplication. Size (1 × D).
  • Output Weight Matrix (W ′): The context embedding matrix. Size (D × V ).
  • Output Layer: Produces scores for every word in the vocabulary. Size (1 × V ).

    • Important Note: Two Sets of Vectors

The model actually learns two distinct vectors for each word during training:

  • vw: The vector when the word is the input/target (stored in W).
  • uc: The vector when the word is the output/context (stored in W ′).

Typically, the input vectors (vw) are used as the final embeddings, as they tend to capture

semantics more effectively.

The "Lookup Trick": How Weights Become Vectors

A crucial insight is how the hidden layer is calculated. When a One-Hot vector is multiplied by

the weight matrix W, it effectively selects the single row corresponding to the input word.

One of the most important innovations in Word2Vec is that it uses self-supervised learning — a learning paradigm that sits between supervised and unsupervised learning.

  • Supervised Learning: Training data has explicit human-provided labels.\ Example: A dataset of emails labeled "spam" or "not spam."
  • Unsupervised Learning: No labels at all. The algorithm finds structure on its own.\ Example: K-means clustering on customer purchase data.
  • Self-Supervised Learning: The algorithm automatically generates its own labels from the data — no human labeling required.\ Example: Word2Vec creates (target, context) pairs directly from raw text.

How Self-Supervision Works in Word2Vec

Given a corpus of raw text, the algorithm:

  1. Selects a target word — any word in the corpus for which we want to learn an embedding.
  2. Defines a context window — typically \(\pm 2\) to \(\pm 5\) words around the target.
  3. Labels context words as positive examples: Any word within the window is automatically a "positive" context word.
  4. Samples negative examples: Words not in the context window are randomly sampled as "negative" examples.
Example: Consider the sentence fragment:
\texttt{... lemon tablespoon of \underline{apricot} jam a ...}

Let the target word be apricot and the window be \(\pm 2\) words. Then:

  • Positive context words: tablespoon, of, jam, a
  • Negative examples (sampled from outside the window): zebra, Tolstoy, pimple, honorable, ...

This labeling happens automatically — no human intervention needed. That is self-supervision.

Word2Vec as a Classification Problem

Once the self-supervised training data is generated, Word2Vec reframes the problem as binary classification:

  • Class 1 (Positive): The word IS a context word for this target.\ We want the predicted probability to be close to 1.
  • Class 0 (Negative): The word is NOT a context word for this target.\ We want the predicted probability to be close to 0.

The learning objective:

  • Maximize the similarity between the target word vector and positive context word vectors.
  • Minimize the similarity between the target word vector and negative (out-of-context) word vectors.
Note: This idea of contrasting positive and negative examples is the foundation of contrastive learning, which is widely used today in training transformers, vision-language models, and other deep learning systems.

Connection to Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD): Word2Vec's binary classification mirrors how selectional restrictions work in lexical semantics. A verb like eat imposes a constraint on its THEME argument — it should be edible. When we encounter eat a hamburger (positive: compatible with the restriction) vs. eat a takeoff (negative: incompatible), the verb's selectional restriction naturally classifies arguments as valid or invalid. Word2Vec learns a similar kind of compatibility signal, except statistically from raw text rather than from hand-coded rules. This concept is a building block for formal Word Sense Disambiguation, where a system must select the correct sense of an ambiguous word (e.g., bass as fish vs. music) from context. WSD classifiers typically use collocational features (position-specific neighboring words like the word immediately to the right) and bag-of-words features (unordered context words capturing general topic) — techniques directly descended from the sparse frequency-based representations we saw earlier.

Homonymy vs. Polysemy: These are two fundamental types of sense ambiguity in lexical semantics:
  • Homonymy: Two senses share the same orthographic form but are semantically unrelated (e.g., bank "financial institution" vs. "sloping mound"). The senses are coincidental — there is no discoverable semantic connection between them.

The senses derive from a common semantic core. A specific subtype is metonymy, where one aspect of an entity refers to another (e.g., the White House referring to the administration, or an author's name referring to their works: I love Jane Austen).
Static embeddings collapse both homonymous and polysemous senses into a single vector — a form of meaning blur that contextual models resolve.

One-Hot Vector Representation

Before we can understand how Word2Vec learns embeddings, we must understand the one-hot representation — the simplest possible way to encode words as vectors.

Construction

Given a corpus, let \(V\) be the unique vocabulary of words. Sort the vocabulary alphabetically (or lexically). Each word gets a unique integer index (often called a token ID) from \(0\) to \(V-1\). For a word at index \(i\), its one-hot vector is: \[ \mathbf{x} = [0,\,0,\,\ldots,\,0,\,\underbrace{1}_{\text{position } i},\,0,\,\ldots,\,0] \in \mathbb{R}^{V} \]

Example: Suppose we have a tiny vocabulary of 5 words sorted alphabetically:
[apple, banana, cat, dog, elephant]
  • apple \(\;\to\;\) [1, 0, 0, 0, 0]
  • banana \(\;\to\;\) [0, 1, 0, 0, 0]
  • cat \(\;\to\;\) [0, 0, 1, 0, 0]
  • dog \(\;\to\;\) [0, 0, 0, 1, 0]
  • elephant \(\;\to\;\) [0, 0, 0, 0, 1]

Problems with One-Hot Vectors

  1. No semantic similarity: The dot product of any two distinct one-hot vectors is always 0. \(\texttt{apple} \cdot \texttt{banana} = 0\) and \(\texttt{apple} \cdot \texttt{elephant} = 0\) — yet apple and banana are semantically more similar than apple and elephant.
  2. Huge dimensionality: Each vector is \(V\)-dimensional. For a realistic vocabulary of 100,000 or more, this is impractical.
  3. Extreme sparsity: \(V-1\) entries are zero.
  4. Cannot capture context: There is no way to infer that "orange" and "juice" often appear together, or that "king" relates to "queen" similarly to how "man" relates to "woman."

The Dense Vector Solution

The core idea is to multiply the one-hot vector by a weight matrix (also called an embedding matrix) to project it into a much smaller, dense space: \[ \underbrace{\mathbf{x}}_{1 \times V} \;\times\; \underbrace{\mathbf{W}}_{V \times D} \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbf{e}}_{1 \times D} \]

  • \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times V}\): One-hot vector of a word
  • \(\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times D}\): Embedding matrix (weights to be learned)
  • \(\mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times D}\): The resulting dense word embedding
  • \(D \ll V\): Typically \(D = 300\) while \(V = 100{,}000\)
Key Concept: Since \(\mathbf{x}\) is one-hot, multiplying \(\mathbf{x} \times \mathbf{W}\) is equivalent to selecting the \(i\)-th row of \(\mathbf{W}\). This row is the word embedding for the word at index \(i\). In other words, \(\mathbf{W}\) acts as a lookup table where each row stores the embedding of one vocabulary word.

The Dimension \(D\) as a Hyperparameter

The number of embedding dimensions \(D\) is a hyperparameter — it is chosen by the practitioner, not learned from data.

  • Andrew Ng's recommendation: \(D = 300\) for most applications.
  • GPT-2: \(D = 768\)
  • GPT-3/GPT-4: \(D = 1024\) up to \(\sim 4096\)
  • General rule: \(D\) should be much smaller than \(V\) (otherwise there is no dimensionality reduction). Typical values range from 100 to 500 for static embeddings and up to a few thousand for contextual embeddings.
Note: The individual dimensions (features) of a learned embedding are not human-interpretable. Unlike in traditional ML where you manually engineer features (e.g., "number of bedrooms," "square footage"), here we simply designate \(F_1, F_2, \ldots, F_D\) as abstract dimensions. The algorithm learns the weights — we do not assign meaning to specific dimensions. This is a hallmark of deep learning: features are learned, not engineered.

Skip-Gram Negative Sampling (SGNS)

Why Negative Sampling?

The original Skip-Gram model used a softmax over the entire vocabulary, which is computationally prohibitive for large \(V\) (100,000+). Negative Sampling solves this by reformulating the problem as binary classification over only a small subset of words.

Key Concept: Instead of predicting which specific word appears in the context (a \(V\)-way classification), SGNS asks a simpler question: Is word \(c\) likely to be a context word for target word \(t\)? — a binary (yes/no) question.

Generating the Training Data

For each target word \(w_t\) in the corpus:

  1. Define a context window of size \(L\) (e.g., \(L=2\) means \(\pm 2\) words around the target).
  2. Collect positive examples: All \(2L\) words (or fewer near sentence boundaries) within the window are positive context words \((w_t, c_{\text{pos}})\) with label \(= 1\).
  3. Sample negative examples: For each positive example, randomly sample \(K\) words from the vocabulary that are not in the context window. These are negative pairs \((w_t, c_{\text{neg}})\) with label \(= 0\).
Example: \label{ex:training-data} Let target \(= \texttt{apricot}\), window \(= \pm 2\). Context words: tablespoon, of, jam, a. With \(K = 2\) (two negatives per positive, giving \(2 \times 4 = 8\) negatives total):

Choosing \(K\) — The Number of Negative Samples

  • Larger datasets: \(K = 2\) to \(5\) is typical.
  • Smaller datasets: \(K = 5\) to \(20\) may be used.
  • Trade-off: More negatives \(\to\) better weight learning (more contrastive examples) but slower training.
  • In our running example, we use \(K = 2\) (or \(K = 3\) in the detailed worked example).

Two Sets of Embeddings

A crucial detail: every word has two vector representations:

  1. Target word embedding (\(\mathbf{w}\)): Used when the word acts as the target (center word).
  2. Context word embedding (\(\mathbf{c}\)): Used when the word acts as a context word (positive or negative).
Note: At the end of training, the final word embedding for any word is typically the sum (or sometimes the average) of its target embedding and its context embedding: \[ \mathbf{v}_{\text{final}}(word) = \mathbf{w}_{word} + \mathbf{c}_{word} \] This captures both roles — being a target and being a context word.

Mathematical Foundations of SGNS

The Sigmoid Function

The SGNS algorithm uses logistic regression for binary classification. The key activation function is the sigmoid (logistic) function: \[ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \] where \(z\) is typically the dot product between the target embedding and the context embedding: \(z = \mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}\).

Properties of the Sigmoid:
  • \(\sigma(z) \in (0, 1)\) — outputs a probability
  • \(\sigma(0) = 0.5\)
  • \(\sigma(z) \to 1\) as \(z \to +\infty\)
  • \(\sigma(z) \to 0\) as \(z \to -\infty\)
  • \(\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z) \cdot (1 - \sigma(z))\) — elegant derivative

Figure: Key properties of the sigmoid activation function
Key Concept: The derivative property \(\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))\) makes the sigmoid particularly attractive for gradient-based optimization. The derivative can be computed directly from the function value itself, requiring no additional computation. This property is shared with softmax and is why sigmoid/softmax are ubiquitous in neural networks and transformers.

Probabilities for Positive and Negative Pairs

For a target word \(w\) and a context word \(c\):

Probability that \(c\) is a context word of \(w\) (positive): \[ P(+ \mid w, c) = \sigma(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}}} \] Probability that \(c\) is NOT a context word of \(w\) (negative): \[ P(- \mid w, c) = 1 - P(+ \mid w, c) = \sigma(-\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}}} \]
Example: If \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c} = 0.508\) (target and context are somewhat similar): \[ P(+ \mid w, c) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-0.508}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.602} \approx 0.624 \] \[ P(- \mid w, c) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{0.508}} \approx 0.376 \] The model predicts a 62.4% chance that \(c\) is a context word — better than random (50%), but not perfect. We want to update the embeddings to push this closer to 100%.

The Loss Function

For a single training example consisting of:

  • One target word \(w\)
  • One positive context word \(c_{\text{pos}}\)
  • \(K\) negative context words \(c_{\text{neg},1}, \ldots, c_{\text{neg},K}\)

The loss function (cross-entropy / log loss) is:

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\Bigg[\log \sigma(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}) \;+\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log \sigma(-\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg},k})\Bigg] \] Equivalently: \[ \mathcal{L} = -\log P(+ \mid w, c_{\text{pos}}) \;-\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log P(- \mid w, c_{\text{neg},k}) \]
Note: Why log? Probabilities multiply across examples, and the product of many small probabilities can underflow numerically. Taking the logarithm converts multiplication to addition and squashes extreme values. The negative sign converts the maximization of likelihood into a minimization of loss — the standard convention in optimization.

Gradient Descent for Weight Updates

Stochastic vs. Batch vs. Mini-Batch

SGNS uses stochastic gradient descent (SGD) — weights are updated after every single training example (one positive + its \(K\) negatives), not after seeing the entire dataset.

Table: Comparison of gradient descent variants
Method Update Frequency Speed Noise
Batch GD After full dataset Slow Low
Mini-Batch GD After \(m\) examples Medium Medium
Stochastic GD After every example Fast High

The Weight Update Rule

General gradient descent update: \[ w_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}} - \eta \cdot \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w} \] where \(\eta\) is the learning rate (typically \(0.01\) or \(0.05\)).
Key Concept: If the derivative \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w}\) is positive, the weight decreases (moves left). If negative, the weight increases (moves right). The learning rate \(\eta\) controls the step size:
  • \(\eta\) too large \(\to\) may overshoot the minimum, oscillate, or diverge.
  • \(\eta\) too small \(\to\) very slow convergence.
  • Common values: \(0.001\), \(0.01\), \(0.05\).

Negative Sampling (SGNS) provides an elegant optimization by redefining the learning objective

It is an approximation technique related to Noise Contrastive Estimation (NCE),

The Shift: From Multi-Class to Binary Classification

We stop asking the V-way classification question:

  • Old Question (Softmax): "Given w, which word out of the 100,000 possibilities is the

context word?"

And start asking a binary classification question:

  • New Question (SGNS): "Is this specific pair (w, c) real or fake?"

    • The New Training Strategy

We generate a new dataset for this binary task. For each actual training pair (the Positive

Sample), we generate K artificial noise pairs (the Negative Samples). K is typically between

5 and 20.

Example: Target="King", Context="Rules". K=2.
  • Positive Pair (D = 1): ("King", "Rules"). Goal: Output 1 (Real).
  • Negative Pair 1 (D = 0): ("King", "Table"). Goal: Output 0 (Fake).
  • Negative Pair 2 (D = 0): ("King", "Water"). Goal: Output 0 (Fake).

    • The Intuition: The Security Guard Analogy (Optimized)

  • The Negative Sampling Approach (The Spot Check): The guard checks the

ticket of the person entering (Positive Sample). Then, instead of checking everyone

in the stadium, the guard glances at K (e.g., 5) random people nearby (Negative

Samples) and confirms they don’t have the ticket.

This reduces the computation from V (100,000) calculations to just K + 1 (e.g., 6)

calculations per training step.

Crucially, we only need to update the K + 1 context

vectors involved, not the entire vocabulary.

SGNS: Mathematical Formulation

Now we adapt the mathematics for this new binary objective.

The Sigmoid Function

Since we are now dealing with binary classification (Real vs. Fake), we replace Softmax with

the Sigmoid function σ(x). It squashes any real number (like the dot product score) into the

range [0, 1].

σ(x) =

1 + e−x

We use this to model the probability that a pair (w, c) is "Real" (D = 1).

The Math: Binary Probabilities

  • Probability the pair is Real: P(D = 1|w, c) = σ(vw · uc)
  • Probability the pair is Fake: P(D = 0|w, c) = 1 −σ(vw · uc)

A useful mathematical identity of the Sigmoid function is its symmetry: 1−σ(x) = σ(−x).

Therefore:

  • Probability the pair is Fake: P(D = 0|w, c) = σ(−vw · uc)

    • The New Objective Function

We want to simultaneously maximize the likelihood of the positive sample being identified as

real AND the likelihood of the K negative samples being identified as fake. We maximize the

joint probability, which in log space becomes a summation.

The Update Rules: Push and Pull Dynamics

We use Stochastic Gradient Ascent to update the vectors: vnew = vold + α · ∇J, where α is the

learning rate. The elegance of the SGNS derivation leads to a beautiful geometric interpretation

of how the vectors learn.

The Intuition: Geometric Interpretation: Attraction and Repulsion

The training process creates forces that shape the semantic space.

  • The Positive Pull (Attraction):

vw ←vw + α · (1 −σ(vw · ucpos)) · ucpos

We are adding a fraction of the context vector ucpos to the target vector vw. This action

physically pulls vw closer to ucpos. Words that appear together attract each other.

  • The Negative Push (Repulsion):

vw ←vw −α · σ(vw · ucneg) · ucneg

We are subtracting a fraction of the negative vector ucneg from the target vector vw. This

action pushes vw away from ucneg. Words that do not appear together (in this sampled

instance) repel each other.

Through billions of iterations of these pushes and pulls, the vectors settle into an equilibrium

where the geometric arrangement reflects the underlying semantics of the language.

We need derivatives with respect to three sets of parameters: the target embedding \(\mathbf{w}\), the positive context embedding \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}\), and each negative context embedding \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg},k}\).

Derivative w.r.t. positive context word \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}\): \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}} = \big[\sigma(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}) - 1\big] \cdot \mathbf{w} \] Derivative w.r.t. a negative context word \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg},k}\): \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg},k}} = \sigma(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg},k}) \cdot \mathbf{w} \] Derivative w.r.t. target word \(\mathbf{w}\): \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = \big[\sigma(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}) - 1\big] \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}} \;+\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sigma(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg},k}) \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg},k} \]
Note: Intuition behind the derivatives:
  • For \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}\): If \(\sigma(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}})\) is already close to 1 (good prediction), then \(\sigma - 1 \approx 0\), so the weight changes very little. If \(\sigma\) is far from 1, the term \((\sigma - 1)\) is large and negative, pushing \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}\) toward \(\mathbf{w}\).
  • For \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}}\): If \(\sigma(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}})\) is close to 0 (good prediction), the update is small. If \(\sigma\) is large (incorrectly predicting context), the update pushes \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}}\) away from \(\mathbf{w}\).

Lexical Semantics Perspective: What Word2Vec implicitly learns through co-occurrence statistics corresponds to well-studied lexical relations in formal semantics:
  • Synonymy — Words with identical or nearly identical meaning (e.g., couch/sofa, car/automobile). Distributional models assign near-identical vectors to synonyms because they appear in nearly identical contexts. Formally, two words are synonymous if they are mutually substitutable in any sentence without changing truth conditions. In practice, most synonyms are approximate — no two words share identical meaning across all contexts (e.g., big sister vs. large sister — only big carries the "older/grown-up" sense).
  • Hypernymy (IS-A) — The class-inclusion relation where one sense denotes a subclass of another (e.g., car is a hyponym of vehicle; dog is a hyponym of animal). Hypernymy is transitive and can be defined via entailment: if A is a hyponym of B, then everything that is A is also B (\(\forall x \, A(x) \Rightarrow B(x)\)). While Word2Vec vectors do not explicitly encode this tree-structured hierarchy, the vector offset between related hyponym/hypernym pairs often shows consistent directional patterns (e.g., \(\vec{king} - \vec{man} + \vec{woman} \approx \vec{queen}\)).
  • Word Similarity vs. Word Relatedness — Two words are similar if they are near-synonyms and roughly substitutable (e.g., car/bicycle). They are related if they share any semantic connection (e.g., car/gasoline — highly related but not similar). Antonyms (hot/cold) are highly related but have opposite meanings. Word2Vec tends to capture relatedness more readily than strict similarity.
  • Meronymy (PART-OF) — The part-whole relation (e.g., wheel is a meronym of car; leg is a meronym of chair). Distributional models partially capture this since parts and wholes frequently co-occur in similar contexts.

Practical Considerations & Summary

Initialization Guidelines

  • Range: Initialize embeddings randomly in \([-0.5, 0.5]\).
  • Avoid zero initialization: If all weights start at zero, all neurons learn the same thing (symmetry problem).
  • Avoid large values: Large initial values can cause the sigmoid to saturate (gradients become near-zero, slowing learning).

Hyperparameter Summary

Advanced Implementation: Noise Distribution & Subsampling

Several practical optimizations are crucial for high-quality embeddings in production systems.

How to Choose Negative Samples? (The Noise Distribution)

Negative words must be drawn from a "Noise Distribution" \(P_n(w)\). Using the raw unigram distribution (frequency in the corpus) leads to very frequent words (like "the", "is") dominating the samples, which is suboptimal. Mikolov et al. found that raising the unigram distribution \(U(w)\) to the power of \(3/4\) works best empirically:

\[ P_n(w) = \frac{[U(w)]^{3/4}}{\sum_{k} [U(k)]^{3/4}} \]

This heuristic acts as a smoothing factor, slightly increasing the sampling probability of rare words and decreasing it for frequent words.

Subsampling Frequent Words

Words like "the" and "is" appear frequently but carry less semantic information. For example, ("France", "the") is less informative than ("France", "Paris").

To counter this, Skip-Gram implements subsampling. Frequent words are randomly discarded from the training data before generating pairs, with a probability \(P(w_i)\):

\[ P(\text{discard } w_i) = 1 - \sqrt{\frac{t}{f(w_i)}} \]

Where \(t\) is a threshold (typically \(\approx 10^{-5}\)) and \(f(w_i)\) is the frequency of the word in the corpus. This speeds up training and improves the quality of embeddings for rarer words.

Handling Window Size > 1

In practice, we use a window (e.g., size 2), yielding multiple context words (e.g., \(c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4\)) for a center word \(w\).

In Skip-Gram, we do not average the context vectors (that is the CBOW approach). Instead, we treat each pair independently and sum their objectives (or equivalently, sum their gradients):

\[ \mathcal{J}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{J}(w, c_1) + \mathcal{J}(w, c_2) + \mathcal{J}(w, c_3) + \mathcal{J}(w, c_4) \[

The vector \(\mathbf{v}_w\) is simultaneously pulled in multiple directions, eventually settling near the geometric center (centroid) of its neighbors.

Why Contextual Embeddings Matter — A Concrete Motivation: WordNet, the most widely used lexical database for English, organizes 117,097 nouns, 11,488 verbs, 22,141 adjectives, and 4,601 adverbs into synsets — sets of near-synonyms that represent a concept. For real-world applications like Word Sense Disambiguation, even simple baselines like choosing the most frequent sense (the "take the first sense" heuristic, since WordNet senses are frequency-ordered) achieve surprisingly high accuracy. But the ceiling — human inter-annotator agreement on fine-grained WordNet-style sense inventories — is only about 75–80%, revealing just how nuanced and difficult word meaning truly is. This gap between static representations and human-level sense discrimination is precisely what contextual embeddings aim to close.

GloVe: Global Vectors for Word Representation

The goal of word embeddings is to transform language into geometry, encoding semantic meaning as positions and directions in a high-dimensional vector space. Before GloVe (Pennington et al., 2014), the field was dominated by two distinct approaches, each with significant drawbacks.

The Two Paradigms

  • Global Matrix Factorization Methods (e.g., Latent Semantic Analysis - LSA):

    These methods utilize the global statistics of the entire corpus. They typically construct a large matrix (e.g., word-document or word-word co-occurrence) and then use techniques like Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to reduce the dimensionality.

    • Pros: Efficiently leverages global statistical information.
    • Cons: The resulting vectors often performed poorly on analogy tasks (like the famous \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{King}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{Man}} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{Woman}} \approx \mathbf{v}_{\text{Queen}}\)), indicating issues with the linear structure of the vector space.
  • Local Context Window Methods (e.g., Word2Vec: Skip-Gram and CBOW):

    These methods slide a window across the corpus, learning embeddings by predicting words based on their local neighbors.

    • Pros: Demonstrated superior performance on analogy tasks, capturing fine-grained semantic and syntactic relationships.
    • Cons: They fail to utilize global statistics effectively. The model analyzes local windows independently, ignoring the vast amount of information contained in the overall counts of how often words appear together globally.

The GloVe Synthesis

GloVe (Global Vectors) was designed to combine the best of both worlds. It aims to achieve the linear substructures that made Word2Vec successful, but does so by training directly on the global co-occurrence statistics of the corpus.

The Intuition: Building the Map Efficiently

Word2Vec learns the map of language by taking billions of tiny, local glances (windows) at the terrain. GloVe starts by first compiling a comprehensive statistical summary of the entire terrain (the co-occurrence matrix) and then draws the map based on those statistics. This allows GloVe to be much more efficient during training while capturing global relationships.

The Foundation: The Co-occurrence Matrix

The starting point for GloVe is the global word-word co-occurrence matrix, denoted by \(\mathbf{X}\).

Constructing the Matrix

Let \(V\) be the size of the vocabulary. The matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) is a \(V \times V\) matrix, where:

  • \(\mathbf{X}_{ij}\) is the count of how often word \(j\) appears in the context of word \(i\) across the entire corpus.
  • "Context" is defined by a window size (e.g., 5 words before and 5 words after).

Interpreting the Ratios

GloVe's key insight is that the relationship between two words (e.g., \(i\) and \(j\)) is best captured by examining the ratios of their co-occurrence probabilities with various probe words \(k\), rather than looking at raw probabilities alone. Consider two words: \(i = \text{ice}\) and \(j = \text{steam}\).

  • If the ratio \(\frac{P(k \mid i)}{P(k \mid j)}\) is very high (\(\gg 1\)), the probe word \(k\) (e.g., \(k = \text{solid}\)) is strongly related to the numerator (ice) and weakly related to the denominator (steam).
  • If the ratio is very low (\(\ll 1\)), the probe word \(k\) (e.g., \(k = \text{gas}\)) is weakly related to the numerator (ice) and strongly related to the denominator (steam).
  • If the ratio is near 1, the probe word is either related to both (e.g., \(k = \text{water}\)) or unrelated to both (e.g., \(k = \text{fashion}\)).
The Intuition: Meaning Through Comparison

The raw probabilities alone are noisy. Both "ice" and "steam" are related to "water". But it is the comparison (the ratio) against other words that defines their specific concepts. The ratio effectively filters out the noise and isolates the distinguishing features (solid vs. gas).

This observation forms the starting point for the GloVe model: the relationship between two words (\(i\) and \(j\)) can be derived by examining the ratios of their co-occurrence probabilities with various probe words \(k\).

Mathematical Formulation: Deriving the Objective

We seek a mathematical model that can generate these probability ratios based on word vectors. Let \(\mathbf{v}_i\) and \(\mathbf{v}_j\) be the vectors for the primary words, and \(\mathbf{u}_k\) be the vector for the probe (context) word.

  1. Step 1: The Initial Postulate

    We want a function \(F\) that takes the vectors as input and outputs the ratio:

    \[ F(\mathbf{v}_i, \mathbf{v}_j, \mathbf{u}_k) = \frac{P_{ik}}{P_{jk}} \]
  2. Step 2: Enforcing Linear Structure (Vector Differences)

    We want to encode meaning in the differences between vectors (like \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{King}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{Man}}\)). Therefore, the function \(F\) should operate on the difference between \(\mathbf{v}_i\) and \(\mathbf{v}_j\):

    \[ F(\mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_j, \mathbf{u}_k) = \frac{P_{ik}}{P_{jk}} \]
  3. Step 3: Capturing Similarity (Dot Product)

    The most natural way for vectors to interact in this space is the dot product, which measures similarity. We reformulate \(F\) to take the dot product of the difference and the probe vector:

    \[ F((\mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_j)^T \mathbf{u}_k) = \frac{P_{ik}}{P_{jk}} \]
  4. Step 4: The Homomorphism Requirement (The Exponential Solution)

    We need to maintain symmetry between the roles of "center word" and "context word" (since the co-occurrence matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) is symmetric). The distinction between \(\mathbf{v}\) (main vectors) and \(\mathbf{u}\) (context vectors) is arbitrary.

    If we examine the structure of the equation: \( F(\mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{u}_k - \mathbf{v}_j^T \mathbf{u}_k) = \frac{P_{ik}}{P_{jk}} \), we see that we are looking for a function \(F\) that transforms subtraction on the left into division on the right, i.e., \(F(A - B) = \frac{F(A)}{F(B)}\). This is a homomorphism between the additive group of real numbers and the multiplicative group of positive real numbers. The solution is the exponential function, \(F(x) = e^x\).

    Substituting \(F = \exp\), we get:

    \[ \frac{\exp(\mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{u}_k)}{\exp(\mathbf{v}_j^T \mathbf{u}_k)} = \frac{P_{ik}}{P_{jk}} \]
  5. Step 5: The Log-Bilinear Model

    This implies a direct relationship for each individual term:

    \[ \exp(\mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{u}_k) = P_{ik} = \frac{X_{ik}}{X_i} \]

    Taking the natural logarithm of both sides:

    \[ \mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{u}_k = \log(P_{ik}) = \log(X_{ik}) - \log(X_i) \]
  6. Step 6: Handling Symmetry and Bias

    The term \(\log(X_i)\) depends only on \(i\) and is independent of the context word \(k\). We can absorb this term into a bias term \(b_i\). We also add a corresponding bias \(\tilde{b}_k\) for the context word \(\mathbf{u}_k\) to restore full symmetry:

    \[ \mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{u}_k + b_i + \tilde{b}_k = \log(X_{ik}) \]

    This is the core relationship GloVe aims to learn. The dot product of two vectors (plus biases) should equal the logarithm of their co-occurrence count.

The GloVe Objective: Weighted Least Squares

We now have a target relationship. How do we train the vectors and biases to satisfy this?

The Least Squares Approach

GloVe formulates this as a regression problem. We want to minimize the squared difference between the model’s prediction (\(\mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{u}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j\)) and the ground truth (\(\log(X_{ij})\)):

\[ J_{\text{unweighted}} = \sum_{i,j=1}^{V} \left( \mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{u}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j - \log(X_{ij}) \right)^2 \]

However, this unweighted approach has major flaws:

  • The Zero Problem: If \(X_{ij} = 0\) (which is extremely common, as the matrix is sparse), \(\log(X_{ij})\) is undefined (\(\log(0) = -\infty\)).
  • Equal Weighting: It treats all co-occurrences equally. A rare pair occurring once is treated the same as a very frequent pair occurring 10,000 times.

The Weighting Function \(f(X_{ij})\)

To address these issues, GloVe introduces a weighting function \(f(X_{ij})\) into the loss function. This function must satisfy:

  • \(f(0) = 0\): This solves the \(\log(0)\) problem by setting the entire term to 0.
  • \(f(x)\) should be non-decreasing: More frequent co-occurrences should generally carry more weight.
  • \(f(x)\) should saturate for large values of \(x\): This prevents extremely frequent stop words from dominating the training process.

The function proposed by the authors is:

\[ f(x) = \begin{cases} (x/x_{\max})^{\alpha} & \text{if } x < x_{\max} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Typical parameters are \(x_{\max} = 100\) and \(\alpha = 0.75\). The fractional power helps to increase the relative weight of rarer co-occurrences.

The Final GloVe Objective Function

Incorporating the weighting function gives us the final objective function that GloVe minimizes:

\[ \mathcal{J} = \sum_{i,j=1}^{V} f(X_{ij}) \left( \mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{u}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j - \log(X_{ij}) \right)^2 \]

Training and Implementation Details

Optimization

The objective function \(\mathcal{J}\) is minimized using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) or variants like AdaGrad. Although GloVe is often described as a "count-based" model, the optimization process is iterative and very similar to training a neural network.

Computational Complexity

The computational complexity of GloVe is determined by the number of non-zero elements in the matrix \(\mathbf{X}\), denoted \(|X_{\text{nnz}}|\). This is because \(f(0) = 0\). This is significantly smaller than the total number of tokens in the corpus, which is what Word2Vec iterates over. This makes GloVe training substantially faster for large corpora.

Important Note: Iterating over Counts, Not Tokens

If ("cat", "meow") occurs 50 times in the corpus:

  • Word2Vec processes this pair 50 separate times during training iterations.
  • GloVe processes this pair only once in the summation, but with a weight \(f(50)\) in the loss function.

The Two Sets of Vectors

Like Word2Vec, GloVe learns two sets of vectors: the main vectors \(\mathbf{v}\) and the context vectors \(\mathbf{u}\). The GloVe authors found that summing the two vectors provides a more robust final embedding:

\[ \mathbf{v}_{\text{final}} = \mathbf{v}_i + \mathbf{u}_i \]

CBOW: A Mathematical Deep Dive

A Deep, Intuitive, Step-by-Step Mathematical Walkthrough of the Continuous Bag-of-Words Model.

Introduction: The Conceptual Foundation & Word Duality

The Continuous Bag-of-Words (CBOW) model is one of the foundational algorithms of modern Natural Language Processing. Its objective is highly intuitive: predict a center target word based on its surrounding context words.

The Core Intuition: The Two-Vector Duality

To understand CBOW, you must first accept a key fact: every word in our vocabulary possesses two completely distinct vector identities depending on its role in the training pair:

  • When acting as a Context Clue (\(\mathbf{v}\)): The word is part of the surrounding environment. It casts a "vote" or exerts a "pull" toward what the center word should be. These context vectors are stored in the input matrix \(\mathbf{W}_{\text{in}}\).
  • When acting as the Target Candidate (\(\mathbf{v}'\)): The word is the candidate being tested. We measure how well its properties align with the collective pull of the context clues. These target vectors are stored in the output matrix \(\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}\).

Renaming the Matrices for Absolute Clarity

Instead of the confusing symbols \(\mathbf{W}\) and \(\mathbf{W}'\), we define the matrices as:

  • \(\mathbf{W}_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times N}\) is the Input Embedding Matrix. Row \(k\) contains the context vector \(\mathbf{v}_k\) for word \(k\).
  • \(\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times V}\) is the Output Embedding Matrix. Column \(k\) contains the target vector \(\mathbf{v}'_k\) for word \(k\).

Where:

  • \(V\): Vocabulary size (total unique words in the corpus).
  • \(N\): Embedding dimension (the number of features in our hidden space).
  • \(C\): Context window size (total number of surrounding words used).

The Forward Pass: Tracing the Matrix Pipeline

Let’s trace exactly how a set of context words transforms into a probability distribution over the vocabulary.

  1. Step 1: One-Hot Context Vectors (\(\mathbf{x}\))

    For each context word \(c_i\) (where \(i \in \{1, \ldots, C\}$)), we represent it as a sparse one-hot column vector \(\mathbf{x}_{c_i} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times 1}\) containing a single 1 at index \(c_i\), and 0 elsewhere.

  2. Step 2: Projection / Lookup Layer (\(\mathbf{v}\))

    We project each context word from the sparse vocabulary space into the dense hidden embedding space by multiplying the transposed input matrix by the one-hot vector:

    \[ \mathbf{v}_{c_i} = \mathbf{W}_{\text{in}}^T \mathbf{x}_{c_i} \]

    Matrix multiplication with a one-hot vector involves no actual arithmetic; it is a pure indexing operation that instantly plucks out the \(c_i\)-th row of \(\mathbf{W}_{\text{in}}\).

  3. Step 3: The "Bag" Averaging (Forming the Centroid \(\mathbf{h}\))

    We collapse all \(C\) individual context vectors into a single collective hidden state \(\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1}\) by averaging them:

    \[ \mathbf{h} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{i=1}^{C} \mathbf{v}_{c_i} = \frac{1}{C} \mathbf{W}_{\text{in}}^T \left( \sum_{i=1}^{C} \mathbf{x}_{c_i} \right) \]

    Geometric Intuition: The vector \(\mathbf{h}\) represents the geometric centroid of the context clues in the \(N\)-dimensional space. Because addition is commutative, word order is entirely discarded—hence the term "Bag of Words."

  4. Step 4: Scoring All Vocabulary Candidates (\(\mathbf{u}\))

    We now measure how closely aligned our context centroid \(\mathbf{h}\) is to every possible target word’s output representation. We compute raw similarity scores (logits) \(\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times 1}\):

    \[ \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}^T \mathbf{h} \]

    Each component \(u_j = \mathbf{v}'^T_j \mathbf{h}\) is a dot product. If the output vector \(\mathbf{v}'_j\) points in the exact same direction as the context centroid \(\mathbf{h}\), the score \(u_j\) is highly positive.

  5. Step 5: Softmax Normalization (\(\mathbf{y}\))

    To convert raw scores \(\mathbf{u}\) into valid probabilities that sum to 1, we pass them through the Softmax function:

    \[ y_j = P(w_j \mid \text{context}) = \frac{\exp(u_j)}{\sum_{k=1}^{V} \exp(u_k)} \]

The Complete Forward Dimension Flow

The dimensions expand and contract as follows:

\[ \mathbf{x}_{c_i} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times 1} \xrightarrow{\text{Lookup}} \mathbf{v}_{c_i} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1} \xrightarrow{\text{Average}} \mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1} \xrightarrow{\text{Dot Product}} \mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times 1} \xrightarrow{\text{Softmax}} \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times 1} \]

Demystifying Backpropagation: Zero Skipped Steps

Training the network requires taking the derivative of the loss \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to our weights.

Deriving the Fundamental Error Vector (\(\mathbf{e} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{u}}\))

We want to know how changing each raw score \(u_j\) affects the total loss \(\mathcal{L}\). Let’s expand the loss formula fully:

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\log(y_t) = -u_t + \log \sum_{k=1}^{V} \exp(u_k) \]

We take the partial derivative w.r.t. an arbitrary score \(u_j\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u_j} = \frac{\partial}{\partial u_j} (-u_t) + \frac{\partial}{\partial u_j} \log \sum_{k=1}^{V} \exp(u_k) \]
  • Term A (\(\frac{\partial}{\partial u_j} (-u_t)\)): \[ \text{Term A} = \begin{cases} -1 & \text{if } j = t \\ 0 & \text{if } j \neq t \end{cases} \equiv -\mathbb{I}[j = t] \]
  • Term B (Using the chain rule on the log-sum): \[ \text{Term B} = \frac{1}{\sum_{k=1}^{V} \exp(u_k)} \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial u_j} \left( \sum_{k=1}^{V} \exp(u_k) \right) = \frac{\exp(u_j)}{\sum_{k=1}^{V} \exp(u_k)} = y_j \]

Combining Terms A and B, we get the elegant error vector component:

\[ e_j \equiv \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u_j} = y_j - \mathbb{I}[j = t] \]

In vector form:

\[ \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{y}_{\text{true}} \]

For our numerical example, the true target is a one-hot vector \(\mathbf{y}_{\text{true}} = [0, 0, 1, 0, 0]^T\). Thus:

\[ \mathbf{e} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.178 \\ 0.165 \\ 0.291 \\ 0.157 \\ 0.208 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.178 \\ 0.165 \\ -0.709 \\ 0.157 \\ 0.208 \end{bmatrix} \]

Gradients for the Weights & The Outer Product

Now we propagate the error vector \(\mathbf{e}\) backward to our weight matrices.

  • Gradient w.r.t. Output Matrix (\(\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}\)):

    Using the linear layer derivative shortcut (input vector times outgoing error vector):

    \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}} = \mathbf{h} \mathbf{e}^T \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times V} \]

    A specific weight \(W_{\text{out}}(m, j)\) connects hidden unit \(h_m\) to score \(u_j\). Its gradient update is simply \(h_m \cdot e_j\).

  • Gradient w.r.t. Hidden Centroid (\(\mathbf{h}\)):

    The error flowing backward into the hidden state is a weighted sum of the columns of \(\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}}\):

    \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{h}} = \mathbf{W}_{\text{out}} \mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1} \]
  • Gradient w.r.t. Input Matrix (\(\mathbf{W}_{\text{in}}\)):

    Since \(\mathbf{h} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{i=1}^C \mathbf{v}_{c_i}\), the gradient distributes equally to the \(C\) context words active in the window:

    \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{W}_{\text{in}}(c_i, :)} = \frac{1}{C} \left( \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{h}} \right)^T = \frac{1}{C} (\mathbf{W}_{\text{out}} \mathbf{e})^T \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times N} \]

    All other rows in \(\mathbf{W}_{\text{in}}\) receive exactly zero gradient in this step. This sparse row update allows Word2Vec to train extremely fast!

Word Embeddings: Frequency-based vs. Prediction-based

  1. Frequency-based Embeddings:
    • Words are represented by counts of nearby words (e.g., Bag of Words, Count Vector, TF-IDF, Co-occurrence vector).
    • Produce very long, sparse vectors of dimension \(V\) (vocabulary size).
  2. Prediction-based Embeddings:
    • Dense, low-dimensional vectors (typically 100–300 dimensions).
    • Representation is created by training a classifier to predict whether a word is likely to appear nearby (e.g., Word2Vec, GloVe).
    • Leverages self-supervision: surrounding words act as labels, requiring no human annotations.

Static vs. Contextual Embeddings

  • Static Embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe): Learn one fixed vector representation for each word, unable to disambiguate polysemous words (e.g., "bank" in "river bank" vs. "financial bank").
  • Contextual Embeddings (ELMo, BERT, GPT): Compute distinct embeddings for a word depending on its surrounding context (separate embedding for each token occurrence).

Linguistic Regularities & Cosine Similarity:

Using this featurized representation, vector arithmetic like \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{king}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{man}} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{woman}}\) produces a vector very close to \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{queen}}\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} -0.95 \\ 0.95 \\ 0.70 \\ 0.02 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} -1.00 \\ 0.01 \\ 0.03 \\ 0.04 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1.00 \\ 0.02 \\ 0.02 \\ 0.01 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.00 \\ 0.96 \\ 0.69 \\ -0.01 \end{bmatrix} \approx \mathbf{v}_{\text{queen}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.97 \\ 0.95 \\ 0.69 \\ 0.02 \end{bmatrix} \]

This explains the linear substructures found in static word embedding spaces.
