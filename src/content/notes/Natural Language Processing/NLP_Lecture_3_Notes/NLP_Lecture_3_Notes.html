Word Embeddings and Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling

3.1 Overview of Word Embeddings: Static vs. Contextual Representations

3.1.1 Definition and Core Intuition

Hook: How can a computer understand that "king" is to "queen" as "man" is to "woman", or that "apple" and "orange" are related fruits, when all it sees are arbitrary string tokens?

Think of a word embedding as giving every word a set of GPS coordinates in a high-dimensional semantic landscape. Just as physical coordinates allow us to measure the geographical distance between cities, word vectors allow natural language processing systems to measure semantic distance and directional relationships between concepts using linear algebra.

A word embedding is a dense numerical vector representation of a textual word in a continuous vector space \(\mathbb{R}^d\), where words with similar semantic meanings or contextual usage patterns are positioned close to one another.

Historically, word representations in natural language processing evolved through three distinct paradigms:

  1. Frequency-based sparse representations (such as term frequency-inverse document frequency, or TF-IDF, count vectorizers, and co-occurrence matrices calculated over context windows of \(\pm 4\) words): These models represent words as extremely high-dimensional vectors (dimension equal to vocabulary size \(V\)) where each dimension counts raw co-occurrence frequencies across documents or local context windows.
  2. Prediction-based static dense representations (such as Word2Vec models including Skip-Gram, Continuous Bag-of-Words, and GloVe): These models project words into compact, dense vectors of dimension \(d \ll V\) (typically \(d \in [300, 4096]\)) by training neural network objectives to predict surrounding context words given a target word.
  3. Contextual dense representations (such as BERT, GPT, and modern transformer architectures): These models dynamically compute distinct vector representations for a word based on its specific surrounding sentence context using multi-head self-attention mechanisms.

Static vs. Contextual Representations: In static word embeddings, each word in the vocabulary receives a single fixed vector representation regardless of context. For example, the polysemous word "bank" receives the exact same static vector \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{bank}}\) whether it refers to a financial institution ("I deposited money at the bank") or a river bank ("We walked along the river bank"). In contrast, contextual word embeddings dynamically construct distinct vectors for "bank" for each sentence context.

3.1.2 Symbol Registry

  • \(V\) — vocabulary size — total count of unique lexical tokens in the corpus — scalar in \(\mathbb{N}\)
  • \(d\) — embedding dimension — number of latent feature dimensions — scalar in \(\mathbb{N}\), typically \(d \in [300, 4096]\)
  • \(\mathbf{v}_w\) — static word vector — dense vector representation of word \(w\) — vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
  • \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}\) — word vectors — arbitrary dense or sparse vector representations — vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) or \(\mathbb{R}^V\)
  • \(\text{sim}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})\) — cosine similarity — geometric angle measure between vectors — scalar in \([-1, 1]\)
  • \(\|\mathbf{u}\|_2\) — Euclidean norm (\(L_2\)-norm) of vector \(\mathbf{u}\) — scalar in \(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\)

3.1.3 Mathematical Comparison of Vector Paradigms

Frequency-based representations build high-dimensional sparse vectors of size \(V\). For two word vectors \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^V\), the unnormalized dot product measures total co-occurrence overlap:

\[ \text{DotProduct}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{V} u_i v_i \]

Because plain dot products are unnormalized, frequent words or long documents with large vector magnitudes produce artificially high dot products regardless of true semantic alignment.

To normalize for vector length and isolate purely angular alignment, systems compute cosine similarity:

\[ \text{CosineSimilarity}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{V} u_i v_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{V} u_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{V} v_i^2}} \]

Dense static embeddings project each word into a low-dimensional space \(\mathbb{R}^d\) where \(d \ll V\). The dense vector space is learned such that dot products \(\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}\) and cosine similarities directly measure semantic similarity.

3.1.4 Fully Worked Example: Cosine Similarity Calculation

Problem: Given two 2-dimensional static word vectors \(\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\) (representing "cat") and \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\) (representing "dog"), calculate their unnormalized dot product, Euclidean norms, and cosine similarity.

Step 1: Compute unnormalized dot product \(\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}\)

\[ \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v} = (3 \times 4) + (4 \times 3) = 12 + 12 = 24 \]

Step 2: Compute Euclidean norm \(\|\mathbf{u}\|_2\) for vector \(\mathbf{u}\)

\[ \|\mathbf{u}\|_2 = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5 \]

Step 3: Compute Euclidean norm \(\|\mathbf{v}\|_2\) for vector \(\mathbf{v}\)

\[ \|\mathbf{v}\|_2 = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25} = 5 \]

Step 4: Compute Cosine Similarity

\[ \text{CosineSimilarity}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2} = \frac{24}{5 \times 5} = \frac{24}{25} = \mathbf{0.96} \]

Sense-check: Both vectors lie in the first quadrant at angles \(\theta_1 \approx 53.13^\circ\) and \(\theta_2 \approx 36.87^\circ\). The angle between them is \(\Delta \theta = 16.26^\circ\), and \(\cos(16.26^\circ) \approx 0.96\), confirming that the vectors point in nearly the exact same semantic direction.

3.1.5 Assumptions & Scope

Scope & Assumptions:

  1. Static Meaning Assumption: Static word embeddings assume that each lexical word token has a single invariant semantic meaning. This assumption breaks when dealing with polysemous words (words with multiple distinct meanings like "bank" or "apple"), forcing the static vector to represent an inconvenient average of all senses.
  2. Context Window Assumption: Word embeddings assume that words appearing in similar local context windows share similar semantic concepts (the Distributional Hypothesis).
  3. Linearity Assumption: Models assume that semantic relationships (like gender or tense shifts) manifest as constant linear translation vectors across the vector space.

3.1.6 Visual Intuition

When word vectors are projected onto a 2D plane via dimensionality reduction techniques (such as t-SNE or PCA), semantic clusters emerge visually:

  • Words representing animals ("cat", "dog", "tiger") form a tight spatial cluster.
  • Words representing royal titles ("king", "queen", "prince") form a parallel cluster.
  • The offset vector \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{woman}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{man}}\) forms a directional arrow that is nearly parallel and equal in length to \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{queen}} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{king}}\), reflecting linear vector arithmetic in the latent space.

3.1.7 Pitfalls

  1. Confusing Dot Product with Cosine Similarity: Unnormalized dot product \(\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}\) measures both angular alignment AND vector length. High-frequency words with large vector norms can yield large dot products with unrelated words. Always use cosine similarity when comparing pure semantic orientation.
  2. Equating Cosine Similarity with True Synonymy: Words that occur in identical contexts—including antonyms such as "hot" and "cold" or "large" and "small"—often exhibit high cosine similarity because they share context windows.

3.1.8 Student Questions and Answers

Q: In the previous session, we discussed a context window of plus 4 and minus 4 words. If a sentence ends after two words, does the window check the next sentence as well, or is it strictly sentence-bounded?

A: Context windows span across sentence punctuation. The sliding window continues scanning context words into adjacent sentences beyond full stops, maintaining continuous text processing across document boundaries.

Q: Can a word have no vector embedding if it was not included in the training data, but a user mentions it at runtime?

A: Yes, this is known as the out-of-vocabulary (OOV) problem. Words missing from the training corpus vocabulary \(V\) do not possess learned static embeddings and revert to a generic unknown token representation (\texttt{}). Production pipelines handle OOV tokens using subword tokenization (e.g., Byte-Pair Encoding or WordPiece) or hybrid sparse-dense retrieval architectures.

3.1.9 Real-World & Domain Connection

In production search engines, conversational AI platforms, and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipelines, system architects deploy hybrid retrieval models. These systems combine traditional sparse keyword algorithms (such as BM25 or TF-IDF) for exact lexical matching with dense vector index search (using Hierarchical Navigable Small World, or HNSW, graphs) to capture deep semantic intent.

Recap & Bridge: Word embeddings transform discrete text tokens into continuous vectors where geometric proximity reflects semantic similarity. However, representing words as sparse one-hot basis vectors creates severe high-dimensional bottlenecks, leading directly to the dense vector methods explored in Section 3.2.

3.2 One-Hot Encoding and High-Dimensional Sparsity

3.2.1 Mechanics of One-Hot Vectors

Hook: Imagine a library catalog with 100,000 unique words where every single word is given its own dedicated aisle, but every aisle is completely empty except for one book. How much space is wasted, and can the catalog tell you if two books are related?

Think of a one-hot vector like a light switchboard containing 100,000 switches. To represent the word "cat", you flip switch #4,217 ON (1) and leave all other 99,999 switches OFF (0). While simple, this switchboard treats every word as completely isolated—flipping the switch for "cat" provides zero electrical or semantic connection to "kitten" or "dog".

Before dense vector space models were developed, categorical text tokens were represented as one-hot vectors. To construct a one-hot representation over a textual corpus:

  1. Extract all unique words to form a vocabulary list of size \(V\).
  2. Sort the vocabulary lexicographically and assign each word an integer token index \(k \in \{0, 1, \dots, V-1\}\).

A one-hot vector \(\mathbf{e}_w\) for a word \(w\) at index \(k\) is a sparse binary vector of size \(V \times 1\), where the \(k\)-th entry equals \(1\) and all remaining \(V-1\) entries equal \(0\):

\[ \mathbf{e}_w = [0, 0, \dots, \underset{k\text{-th pos}}{1}, \dots, 0]^\top \in \{0, 1\}^V \]

3.2.2 Symbol Registry

  • \(V\) — vocabulary size — total count of unique words in sorted corpus — scalar in \(\mathbb{N}\)
  • \(\mathbf{e}_w\) — one-hot vector — canonical basis vector for word \(w\) — binary vector in \(\{0, 1\}^V\)
  • \(W\) — target embedding weight matrix — mapping matrix from one-hot to dense space — matrix in \(\mathbb{R}^{V \times d}\)
  • \(\mathbf{w}_w\) — dense target embedding vector for word \(w\) — \(k\)-th row of matrix \(W\) — vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
  • \(d\) — embedding dimension — number of latent feature dimensions — scalar in \(\mathbb{N}\)

3.2.3 Computational Limitations and Matrix Dimensionality

One-hot representations suffer from three major theoretical and computational limitations:

  1. Extreme Sparsity: For a standard vocabulary size of \(V = 100{,}000\), every one-hot vector contains \(99{,}999\) zeros and only a single non-zero entry (\(99.999\%\) sparse).
  2. Orthogonality and Lack of Semantic Distance: For any two distinct words \(u \neq v\), their one-hot basis vectors \(\mathbf{e}_u\) and \(\mathbf{e}_v\) are orthogonal:
\[ \mathbf{e}_u^\top \mathbf{e}_v = 0 \]

Consequently, dot products between one-hot vectors yield zero similarity regardless of semantic closeness. The dot product between "apple" and "orange" is exactly zero, identical to the dot product between "apple" and "airplane".

  1. Computational Inefficiency: Storing and multiplying matrices of dimension \(V\) requires massive memory and processing resources that scale poorly as vocabulary size grows.

Dense embedding models overcome these issues by introducing a weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}\), where \(d \ll V\). Multiplying the transposed one-hot vector \(\mathbf{e}_w^\top\) (size \(1 \times V\)) by the weight matrix \(W\) (size \(V \times d\)) extracts the \(k\)-th row vector \(\mathbf{w}_w\) (size \(1 \times d\)):

\[ \mathbf{e}_w^\top W = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \dots & 1 & \dots & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} w_{0,1} & \dots & w_{0,d} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ w_{k,1} & \dots & w_{k,d} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ w_{V-1,1} & \dots & w_{V-1,d} \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{w}_w \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d} \]

Multiplying a one-hot vector by weight matrix \(W\) acts mathematically as a row lookup operation, selecting the dense embedding row for word token index \(k\).

3.2.4 Fully Worked Example: One-Hot Row Lookup Operation

Problem: Consider a tiny vocabulary of \(V = 3\) words: index 0 = "apple", index 1 = "banana", index 2 = "cat". Suppose the target embedding weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}\) is initialized as:

\[ W = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.2 \\ -0.1 & 0.8 \\ 0.9 & -0.4 \end{bmatrix} \]

Calculate the dense embedding vector for the target word "banana" (index \(k = 1\)) using one-hot vector matrix multiplication.

Step 1: Construct the one-hot vector \(\mathbf{e}_{\text{banana}}\)

For index \(k = 1\), the one-hot row vector \(\mathbf{e}_{\text{banana}}^\top\) is:

\[ \mathbf{e}_{\text{banana}}^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Step 2: Perform matrix multiplication \(\mathbf{e}_{\text{banana}}^\top W\)

\[ \mathbf{e}_{\text{banana}}^\top W = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.2 \\ -0.1 & 0.8 \\ 0.9 & -0.4 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ = \left[ (0 \times 0.5) + (1 \times -0.1) + (0 \times 0.9), \quad (0 \times 0.2) + (1 \times 0.8) + (0 \times -0.4) \right] \] \[ = \begin{bmatrix} -0.1 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} \]

Result: The resulting vector is \(\mathbf{\begin{bmatrix} -0.1 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^2\), which exactly matches row index 1 of matrix \(W\).

Sense-check: Because only the second element of the one-hot vector is 1, all other row contributions in \(W\) are zeroed out, isolating row 1 in a single step.

3.2.5 Assumptions & Scope

Scope & Assumptions:

  1. Categorical Independence Assumption: One-hot encoding assumes that all word tokens are fundamentally independent basis vectors. This assumption is mathematically valid for raw token identification, but fails for semantic similarity modeling.
  2. Lookup Efficiency: In theoretical math, embedding extraction is written as matrix multiplication \(\mathbf{e}_w^\top W\). In computational implementations, software systems treat this operation purely as a direct memory array lookup (W[k]), avoiding costly zero-multiplications.

3.2.6 Visual Intuition

Visualizing matrix multiplication \(\mathbf{e}_w^\top W\):

  • The input one-hot row vector acts like a horizontal mask with a single open window at index \(k\).
  • Sliding this mask down the weight matrix \(W\) blocks out all rows except row \(k\).
  • The output compresses a sparse vector of width \(V\) into a dense feature bar of length \(d\).

3.2.7 Pitfalls

  1. Executing Literal Matrix Multiplications in Code: Attempting to multiply full one-hot sparse matrices by weight matrix \(W\) at runtime wastes massive memory and GPU compute. Production frameworks use dedicated embedding lookup layers (nn.Embedding) that index matrix memory directly in \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) time.
  2. Expecting Human-Interpretable Embedding Dimensions: Beginners often assume feature columns \(F_1, F_2, \dots, F_d\) correspond to human concepts like "is animal" or "is verb". In practice, neural networks learn uninterpretable latent dimensions optimized solely for loss minimization.

3.2.8 Student Questions and Answers

Q: How do one-hot vectors connect to dense feature matrices, and how are feature columns determined?

A: Multiplying a one-hot indicator vector by a weight matrix isolates the specific row corresponding to that word token ID. The feature columns \(F_1, F_2, \dots, F_d\) are not hand-engineered linguistic categories; they are continuous latent numeric dimensions learned automatically by neural networks via gradient descent optimization on corpus context prediction tasks.

3.2.9 Real-World & Domain Connection

Modern deep learning frameworks (such as PyTorch torch.nn.Embedding or TensorFlow tf.keras.layers.Embedding) replace high-dimensional one-hot representations with compact dense lookup tables. Large language models like GPT-4 (\(d \in [1024, 4096]\)) use these dense lookup tables as the very first layer of their neural network architecture.

Recap & Bridge: One-hot encodings provide clean token indexing but create high-dimensional sparse vectors with zero semantic distance. Dense weight matrices \(W\) resolve this by mapping tokens to compact vector rows, setting the stage for how Word2Vec learns these weights automatically in Section 3.3.

3.3 Word2Vec and Self-Supervised Learning Mechanics

3.3.1 Self-Supervised Data Generation Framework

Hook: How can an AI learn rich semantic concepts from billions of unlabelled web pages without a single human annotator spending a single second labeling data?

Self-supervised learning in Word2Vec functions like an automated, self-generating fill-in-the-blank game. The algorithm slides across raw text, picks a central word as the "target", and uses the surrounding words as "answers". By turning raw text against itself, the corpus generates millions of labeled training pairs automatically without human intervention.

Word2Vec, introduced by Mikolov et al. at Google, established a computationally efficient framework for learning static word embeddings. Rather than requiring human-labeled datasets, Word2Vec formulates a self-supervised learning objective directly on unannotated raw text corpora.

To understand where self-supervised learning fits in machine learning taxonomy:

  • Supervised Learning: Algorithms learn a mapping function from input features \(\mathbf{x}\) to target labels \(y\) explicitly supplied by human annotators (e.g., spam vs. non-spam labels).
  • Unsupervised Learning: Algorithms discover latent structure in unlabeled data features \(\mathbf{x}\) without any target labels \(y\) (e.g., K-means clustering or principal component analysis).
  • Self-Supervised Learning: Algorithms automatically construct target labels \(y\) from the intrinsic structure of the input data itself without human annotation.

3.3.2 Symbol Registry

  • \(w\) — target word token index — scalar in \(\{0, \dots, V-1\}\)
  • \(c\) — positive context word token index — scalar in \(\{0, \dots, V-1\}\)
  • \(c'\) — negative context word sample token index — scalar in \(\{0, \dots, V-1\}\)
  • \(L\) — context window radius — scalar in \(\mathbb{N}\), typically \(L \in [2, 5]\)
  • \(V\) — vocabulary size — total count of unique words — scalar in \(\mathbb{N}\)
  • \(d\) — embedding dimension — latent feature dimension — scalar in \(\mathbb{N}\)
  • \(\mathbf{w}_v\) — target embedding vector for word \(v\) — row in matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}\) — vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
  • \(\mathbf{c}_v\) — context embedding vector for word \(v\) — row in matrix \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}\) — vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
  • \(\mathbf{v}_v\) — final static word embedding for word \(v\) — vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)

3.3.3 Context Windows and Sample Generation

For every target word \(w\) in a corpus, a sliding context window of radius \(L\) (typically \(L \in [2, 5]\)) scans the surrounding text:

  1. Positive Context Pairs: Any word token \(c\) occurring within the window offset \([w - L, w + L]\) (excluding the target word \(w\) itself) forms a positive training pair ( \(w\), \(c\) ) with binary target label \(y = 1\).
  2. Negative Context Pairs: Any word token \(c'\) drawn randomly from the vocabulary outside the target word's local context window forms a negative pair ( \(w\), \(c'\) ) with binary target label \(y = 0\).

For instance, in the sentence fragment "jam of apricot preserves" with target word \(w = \text{"apricot"}\) and window radius \(L = 2\):

  • Surrounding words within distance 2 are \(\{\text{"jam"}, \text{"of"}, \text{"preserves"}\}\).
  • Positive training tuples (\(y = 1\)): \(\text{("apricot", "jam")}\), \(\text{("apricot", "of")}\), \(\text{("apricot", "preserves")}\).
  • Negative training tuples (\(y = 0\)): \(\text{("apricot", "zebra")}\), \(\text{("apricot", "pimple")}\), \(\text{("apricot", "idle")}\).

3.3.4 Dual-Embedding Architecture: Target and Context Vectors

To mathematically separate prediction parameters and ensure clean gradient updates, Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS) assigns two distinct vector representations to every unique word token \(v \in \{0, \dots, V-1\}\):

  1. \(\mathbf{w}_v \in \mathbb{R}^d\) — Target vector representation, stored in target matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}\). Used when word \(v\) acts as the central target word.
  2. \(\mathbf{c}_v \in \mathbb{R}^d\) — Context vector representation, stored in context matrix \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}\). Used when word \(v\) acts as a surrounding context word.

The total parameter count during training is \(2 \times V \times d\). Once model training completes, the final static embedding \(\mathbf{v}_v\) for word \(v\) is computed as the vector sum of its target and context embeddings:

\[ \mathbf{v}_v = \mathbf{w}_v + \mathbf{c}_v \]

(Alternatively, implementations may retain only target matrix \(W\), setting \(\mathbf{v}_v = \mathbf{w}_v\), or concatenate \([\mathbf{w}_v; \mathbf{c}_v]\)).

Step 1: Identify context window bounds

Target word position = 3 ("makes").

Window radius \(L = 2\).

Window range = \([3 - 2, 3 + 2] = [1, 5]\).

Tokens in range (excluding target at index 3):

  • Index 1: "lemon"
  • Index 2: "juice"
  • Index 4: "sour"
  • Index 5: "taste"

Step 2: Generate positive binary training tuples (\(y = 1\))

  • Pair 1: (\(w = \text{"makes"}\), \(c = \text{"lemon"}\), \(y = 1\))
  • Pair 2: (\(w = \text{"makes"}\), \(c = \text{"juice"}\), \(y = 1\))
  • Pair 3: (\(w = \text{"makes"}\), \(c = \text{"sour"}\), \(y = 1\))
  • Pair 4: (\(w = \text{"makes"}\), \(c = \text{"taste"}\), \(y = 1\))

Sense-check: A radius of \(L=2\) yields at most \(2L = 4\) positive context words per target position (fewer only at document boundaries).

3.3.6 Assumptions & Scope

Scope & Assumptions:

  1. Symmetric Window Assumption: The standard context window assumes equal semantic relevance for words occurring before and after the target word.
  2. Dual-Matrix Separation: Target matrix \(W\) and context matrix \(C\) are treated as independent parameters during optimization. Combining them into a single matrix during SGD training would introduce non-linear quadratic terms in vector dot products.

3.3.7 Visual Intuition

Visualizing context window generation:

  • Imagine a sliding frame moving across a sequence of word blocks.
  • The center block is highlighted in blue (target word \(\mathbf{w}\)).
  • The flanking blocks within distance \(L\) are highlighted in green (positive context words \(\mathbf{c}\)).
  • Random blocks selected from outside the frame are highlighted in red (negative context words \(\mathbf{c}'\)).

3.3.8 Pitfalls

  1. Confusing Self-Supervised Learning with Reinforcement Learning: Reinforcement learning requires environment state actions, reward functions, and policy updates. Self-supervised learning is strictly a classification paradigm where supervision labels are generated directly from data geometry.
  2. Discarding Context Matrix \(C\) Early: Attempting to update a single matrix for both target and context roles causes unstable optimization. Dual matrices are essential for linear gradient updates.

3.3.9 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Is self-supervised learning equivalent to reinforcement learning?

A: No. Reinforcement learning relies on reward signals and trial-and-error environment interactions. Self-supervised learning refers strictly to automated training data generation, where positive target labels are generated from internal data structures (like surrounding context windows) without human annotators.

Q: Why does each word require two separate vector representations during training?

A: Maintaining separate target (\(\mathbf{w}\)) and context (\(\mathbf{c}\)) matrices simplifies partial derivative equations during gradient descent optimization. If a single vector \(\mathbf{v}\) were updated simultaneously as target and context within the same dot product \(\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{v}\), gradient derivations would contain non-linear quadratic terms \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{v}} (\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{v}) = 2\mathbf{v}\). Dual matrices keep dot products \(\mathbf{c}^\top \mathbf{w}\) strictly linear with respect to each parameter set during partial differentiation.

3.3.10 Real-World & Domain Connection

The self-supervised paradigm pioneered by Word2Vec serves as the foundation for modern deep learning models. Pre-training objectives such as Masked Language Modeling in BERT (predicting masked words) and Causal Language Modeling in GPT-4 (predicting next tokens) are direct evolutions of self-supervised context prediction.

Recap & Bridge: Self-supervised context windowing automatically transforms raw text into positive and negative training pairs using dual target and context matrices. Next, Section 3.4 formulates how Skip-Gram uses negative sampling to optimize these vectors efficiently.

3.4 Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS) Formulation

3.4.1 Binary Classification Framework

Hook: Why calculate exact probability distributions over 100,000 dictionary words every millisecond when you can turn a massive 100,000-way multiple-choice question into a few fast True/False tests?

Professor Analogy: Negative sampling operates like a multiple-choice fill-in-the-blank exam question. Instead of asking a model to generate a word out of thin air from the entire dictionary (Softmax), the exam provides one correct answer (the positive context word) alongside \(K\) distractor choices (negative samples). Adding more distractor choices (larger \(K\)) forces the student model to learn finer-grained semantic distinctions.

The Skip-Gram neural network architecture can be formulated under two distinct mathematical objectives:

  1. Full Softmax Formulation: Predict the context word \(c\) given target word \(w\) as a multi-class probability distribution across all \(V\) words in the vocabulary:
\[ P(c \mid w) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{c}_c^\top \mathbf{w}_w)}{\sum_{v=1}^{V} \exp(\mathbf{c}_v^\top \mathbf{w}_w)} \]

Computing the denominator sum \(\sum_{v=1}^{V} \exp(\mathbf{c}_v^\top \mathbf{w}_w)\) requires evaluating dot products across all \(V\) vocabulary words for every single gradient step. When \(V = 100{,}000\), this \(\mathcal{O}(V \cdot d)\) operation becomes a severe computational bottleneck.

  1. Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS): Replace the single \(V\)-way Softmax calculation with \(1 + K\) independent binary logistic regression tasks per positive context word.

3.4.2 Symbol Registry

  • \(w\) — target word index — scalar in \(\{0, \dots, V-1\}\)
  • \(\mathbf{w}\) — target embedding vector for word \(w\) — vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
  • \(c_{\text{pos}}\) — positive context word index — scalar in \(\{0, \dots, V-1\}\)
  • \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}\) — positive context embedding vector — vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
  • \(c_{\text{neg}, k}\) — \(k\)-th negative context sample word index — scalar in \(\{0, \dots, V-1\}\)
  • \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}\) — \(k\)-th negative context embedding vector — vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)
  • \(K\) — negative sampling ratio — number of negative context words drawn per positive word — scalar in \(\mathbb{N}\), typically \(K \in [2, 5]\) for large corpora, \(K \in [5, 20]\) for small datasets
  • \(P_n(w)\) — unigram noise distribution for negative sampling — probability distribution over \(V\)
  • \(\sigma(z)\) — logistic sigmoid function — scalar in \(\left(0, 1\right)\)

3.4.3 Negative Sampling Mechanics and Contrastive Learning

For each true positive context pair \(\left(w, c_{\text{pos}}\right)\), Skip-Gram with Negative Sampling draws \(K\) negative context words \(c_{\text{neg}, 1}, c_{\text{neg}, 2}, \dots, c_{\text{neg}, K}\) randomly from a modified unigram noise distribution \(P_n(w)\) over the vocabulary.

To prevent ultra-frequent stop words (like "the", "is", "of") from dominating negative samples while ensuring rare words are sampled sufficiently, Mikolov et al. defined the noise distribution \(P_n(w)\) by raising unigram word frequencies \(f(w)\) to the \(3/4\) power:

\[ P_n(w) = \frac{f(w)^{3/4}}{\sum_{v=1}^{V} f(v)^{3/4}} \]

Exponentiating unigram frequencies by \(3/4\) increases the relative probability of selecting rare words while dampening the dominance of extremely common tokens.

This contrastive formulation trains the target vector \(\mathbf{w}\) to maximize similarity with true context vector \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}\) while minimizing similarity with noise context vectors \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}\).

3.4.4 Fully Worked Example: Computational Savings of SGNS vs. Softmax

Problem: A text corpus has vocabulary size \(V = 100{,}000\) and embedding dimension \(d = 300\). For a target word with context window radius \(L = 3\) (yielding \(2L = 6\) positive context words) and negative sampling ratio \(K = 5\):

  1. Compute the total number of floating-point dot product operations per target word under full Softmax.
  2. Compute the total number of dot product operations under SGNS.
  3. Determine the speedup ratio achieved by SGNS.

Step 1: Compute Softmax operations

Full Softmax requires evaluating the denominator across all \(V = 100{,}000\) words for each of the 6 positive context words:

\[ \text{Softmax Operations} = 6 \times V = 6 \times 100{,}000 = \mathbf{600{,}000} \text{ dot products} \]

Step 2: Compute SGNS operations

For each of the 6 positive context words, SGNS performs 1 positive binary evaluation and \(K = 5\) negative binary evaluations:

\[ \text{Binary Tasks per Context Word} = 1 + K = 1 + 5 = 6 \] \[ \text{Total SGNS Operations} = 6 \text{ positive words} \times 6 \text{ tasks} = \mathbf{36} \text{ dot products} \]

Step 3: Calculate speedup ratio

\[ \text{Speedup Ratio} = \frac{600{,}000}{36} \approx \mathbf{16{,}666.67 \times} \]

Sense-check: SGNS reduces computation from hundreds of thousands of vector dot products down to just 36 binary operations, enabling Word2Vec to train on billions of words in minutes.

3.4.5 Assumptions & Scope

Scope & Assumptions:

  1. Binary Independence: SGNS assumes that binary decisions across positive and negative context words are conditionally independent given target vector \(\mathbf{w}\).
  2. Noise Distribution Tuning: The \(3/4\) power in unigram sampling \(P_n(w) \propto f(w)^{3/4}\) is an empirical hyperparameter choice that balances stop-word suppression with rare-word exposure.

3.4.6 Visual Intuition

Comparing Softmax vs SGNS architectures:

  • Softmax: A massive funnel taking 1 target vector and computing normalized probabilities across 100,000 output nodes simultaneously.
  • SGNS: A compact array of \(1 + K\) independent binary gates, each taking a dot product \(\mathbf{c}^\top \mathbf{w}\) and passing it through a 2-class sigmoid curve.

3.4.7 Pitfalls

  1. Using Uniform Random Sampling for Negative Words: Draw words uniformly at random (\(1/V\)) samples obscure, long-tail words almost exclusively, creating easy negative distractors that fail to challenge the model.
  2. Using Raw Unigram Frequencies: Sampling proportional to raw frequency \(f(w)\) causes negative samples to consist almost entirely of meaningless stop words ("the", "and", "in"). The \(3/4\) exponent strikes the optimal balance.

3.4.8 Student Questions and Answers

Q: How are negative context words selected, and what happens if a negative sample accidentally appears as a positive context word elsewhere?

A: Negative samples are selected randomly from vocabulary tokens outside the target word's local context window using noise distribution \(P_n(w) \propto f(w)^{3/4}\). While random sampling might occasionally pick a word that appears as a context word in another sentence (a "false negative"), this statistical noise is minor (\(\approx K/V\)) and is easily washed out across large text corpora over millions of training iterations.

Q: Does the negative sampling parameter \(K\) mean we select \(K\) negative words for every single positive context word?

A: Yes. If a target word has \(L = 4\) positive context words in its window and \(K = 2\), the training batch for that target position consists of \(4\) positive pairs (\(y = 1\)) and \(4 \times 2 = 8\) negative pairs (\(y = 0\)), producing \(12\) total binary training tuples.

3.5 Mathematical Optimization and Sigmoid Log-Loss Derivation

3.5.1 Sigmoid Probability Formulation

Hook: How do we write a single mathematical loss function that pulls semantically related words together in vector space while pushing random noise words apart?

Think of the SGNS loss function as a system of invisible springs. When target word vector \(\mathbf{w}\) and true context word vector \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}\) meet, an attractive spring pulls them together. When \(\mathbf{w}\) encounters negative noise words \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}\), repulsive springs push them apart. The loss function measures the total tension in these springs, guiding gradient descent to minimize tension.

The probability that a context word \(c\) co-occurs with target word \(w\) (positive class label \(y = 1\)) is modeled using the logistic sigmoid function \(\sigma(z)\) applied to their vector dot product \(z = \mathbf{c}^\top \mathbf{w}\):

\[ P(+ \mid w, c_{\text{pos}}) = \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w}}} \]

The probability of a negative context pair (\(y = 0\)) is the complement probability \(1 - P(+ \mid w, c_{\text{neg}})\):

\[ \begin{aligned} P(- \mid w, c_{\text{neg}, k}) &= 1 - P(+ \mid w, c_{\text{neg}, k}) = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}}} \\ &= \frac{1 + e^{-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}} - 1}{1 + e^{-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}}} = \frac{e^{-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}}}{1 + e^{-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}}} \\ &= \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}) \end{aligned} \]

Sigmoid Derivative Identity: The fundamental derivative identity of the logistic sigmoid function is:

\[ \frac{d\sigma(z)}{dz} = \sigma(z)\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr) \]

Proof:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dz} (1 + e^{-z})^{-1} &= -(1 + e^{-z})^{-2} \cdot (-e^{-z}) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2} \\ &= \left(\frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\right) \left(\frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}}\right) = \left(\frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\right) \left(1 - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\right) \\ &= \sigma(z)\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr) \end{aligned} \]

This property makes the sigmoid function computationally ideal for gradient descent optimization.

3.5.2 Symbol Registry

  • \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}}\) — negative log-likelihood loss for a single target word — scalar in \(\mathbb{R}^+\)
  • \(\eta\) — learning rate hyperparameter — scalar in \(\mathbb{R}^+\), typically \(\eta \in [0.01, 0.05]\)
  • \(z_{\text{pos}}\) — dot product for positive pair — \(z_{\text{pos}} = \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w}\) — scalar in \(\mathbb{R}\)
  • \(z_{\text{neg}, k}\) — dot product for \(k\)-th negative pair — \(z_{\text{neg}, k} = \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}\) — scalar in \(\mathbb{R}\)
  • \(\text{err}_{\text{pos}}\) — prediction error for positive pair — \(\sigma(z_{\text{pos}}) - 1\) — scalar in \(\mathbb{R}\)
  • \(\text{err}_{\text{neg}, k}\) — prediction error for \(k\)-th negative pair — \(\sigma(z_{\text{neg}, k}) - 0\) — scalar in \(\mathbb{R}\)

3.5.3 Log-Loss Function and Complete Partial Derivative Derivation

Assuming conditional independence across positive and negative context choices, we maximize the joint probability of positive co-occurrences while minimizing negative co-occurrences. Taking the negative logarithm converts probability products into summations to prevent numerical underflow, yielding the SGNS Objective Loss Function:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}} = -\log \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w}) - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}) \]

To optimize vector weights using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), we derive partial derivatives of \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}}\) with respect to model parameter vectors \(\mathbf{w}\), \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}\), and \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}\):

1. Complete Derivation of \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}}}{\partial \mathbf{w}}\) (Target Vector Gradient):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}}}{\partial \mathbf{w}} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{w}} \left[ -\log \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w}) \right] + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{w}} \left[ -\log \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}) \right] \\ &= -\frac{1}{\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w})} \frac{\partial \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w})}{\partial \mathbf{w}} - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{1}{\sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w})} \frac{\partial \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w})}{\partial \mathbf{w}} \\ &= -\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w}) (1 - \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w}))}{\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w})} \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}} - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}) (1 - \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}))}{\sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w})} (-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}) \\ &= -(1 - \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w})) \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}} + \sum_{k=1}^{K} (1 - \sigma(-\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w})) \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k} \\ &= \bigl(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w}) - 1\bigr) \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}} + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}) \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k} \end{aligned} \]

Notice that \(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w}) - 1 = \text{err}_{\text{pos}}\) represents the prediction error for the positive pair (prediction minus target label 1), while \(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}) = \text{err}_{\text{neg}, k}\) represents the prediction error for negative pairs (prediction minus target label 0, since \(1 - \sigma(-z) = \sigma(z)\)).

2. Derivation of \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}}}{\partial \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}}\) (Positive Context Vector Gradient):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}}}{\partial \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}} = \bigl(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w}) - 1\bigr) \mathbf{w} = \text{err}_{\text{pos}} \cdot \mathbf{w} \]

3. Derivation of \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}}}{\partial \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}}\) (\(k\)-th Negative Context Vector Gradient):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{SGNS}}}{\partial \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}} = \sigma(\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}, k}^\top \mathbf{w}) \mathbf{w} = \text{err}_{\text{neg}, k} \cdot \mathbf{w} \]

3.5.4 Fully Worked Example: Symbolic Gradient Verification

Problem: Suppose \(\mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\), and a single negative context vector \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) (\(K=1\)).

  1. Compute positive dot product \(z_{\text{pos}}\) and negative dot product \(z_{\text{neg}}\).
  2. Compute probabilities \(\sigma(z_{\text{pos}})\) and \(\sigma(z_{\text{neg}})\).
  3. Calculate the target gradient \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{w}}\).

Step 1: Compute dot products

\[ z_{\text{pos}} = \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}}^\top \mathbf{w} = (1)(1) + (0)(0) = 1 \] \[ z_{\text{neg}} = \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}}^\top \mathbf{w} = (0)(1) + (1)(0) = 0 \]

\[ \sigma(z_{\text{pos}}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-1}} \approx \frac{1}{1 + 0.3679} \approx 0.7311 \] \[ \sigma(z_{\text{neg}}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^0} = \frac{1}{1 + 1} = 0.5 \]

\[ \text{err}_{\text{pos}} = 0.7311 - 1 = -0.2689 \] \[ \text{err}_{\text{neg}} = 0.5 - 0 = 0.5 \] \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = \text{err}_{\text{pos}} \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\text{pos}} + \text{err}_{\text{neg}} \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\text{neg}} = -0.2689 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + 0.5 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{\begin{bmatrix} -0.2689 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix}} \]

Scope & Assumptions:

  1. Logarithmic Probability Addition: Logarithms map probability multiplication into addition, preventing arithmetic underflow when multiplying thousands of small floating-point probabilities.
  2. Binary Cross-Entropy Equivalence: The SGNS loss objective is mathematically equivalent to binary cross-entropy loss evaluated across 1 positive sample and \(K\) negative samples.

3.5.6 Visual Intuition

Visualizing the Sigmoid curve \(\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\):

  • S-shaped curve bounded in \(\left(0, 1\right)\).
  • At \(z = 0\), \(\sigma(0) = 0.5\).
  • As dot product \(z \to +\infty\) (strong positive alignment), \(\sigma(z) \to 1.0\).
  • As dot product \(z \to -\infty\) (strong negative orthogonality/opposite direction), \(\sigma(z) \to 0.0\).

3.5.7 Pitfalls

  1. Sign Errors in Gradient Formulas: A common error is forgetting that \(\frac{d}{d\mathbf{w}} (-\mathbf{c}^\top \mathbf{w}) = -\mathbf{c}\), which flips the sign of negative sample gradient terms.
  2. Confusing Prediction Errors: Remembering that \(\text{err}_{\text{pos}} = \sigma(z_{\text{pos}}) - 1\) (target label 1) while \(\text{err}_{\text{neg}} = \sigma(z_{\text{neg}}) - 0 = \sigma(z_{\text{neg}})\) (target label 0) prevents sign mistakes during manual derivations.

3.5.8 Student Questions and Answers

Q: Why do we apply log transformations to probability products in the loss function?

A: Multiplying many small probability numbers (such as \(0.005 \times 0.005 \times \dots\)) leads to numerical underflow in computer arithmetic. The logarithm maps probability multiplication into addition (\(\log(A \cdot B) = \log A + \log B\)), creating stable, numerically sound gradient calculations during backpropagation.

Q: Why is Stochastic Gradient Descent preferred over Batch Gradient Descent for training word embeddings?

A: Large text corpora contain billions of tokens. Batch gradient descent requires processing the entire corpus before performing a single weight update. Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) updates parameter vectors immediately after evaluating individual positive-negative sample tuples, accelerating model convergence by millions of iterations.

Scope & Assumptions:

  2. Fixed Learning Rate: Learning rate \(\eta = 0.05\) is assumed constant for this single step; production implementations often apply linear learning rate decay across training epochs.

3.6.6 Pitfalls

  1. Using Updated Weights Mid-Iteration: Updating \(\mathbf{c}_{\text{Stark}}\) using \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{Ned, new}}\) instead of \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{Ned, old}}\) during the same iteration breaks SGD synchrony. All gradient terms in a batch step must be calculated using parameter values from the start of the step.
  2. Accumulating Rounding Errors: Rounding intermediate dot products to 2 decimal places distorts gradient vectors. Always maintain at least 4 to 6 decimal places during manual exam calculations.

3.6.7 Student Questions and Answers

Q: How do we know when to stop Stochastic Gradient Descent training iterations?

A: Training stops when total corpus log-loss converges below a specified threshold \(\epsilon\) (loss change \(\Delta \mathcal{L} < 10^{-5}\)) or when the maximum epoch limit (typically 5 to 20 full corpus passes) is reached.

Q: In exam questions, will numerical calculations require high-dimensional matrices or many iterations?

A: No. Exam questions test conceptual understanding of gradient steps using small dimensions (\(d = 2\) or \(3\)), single positive/negative pairs, and clean decimal numbers (\(\eta = 0.05\) or \(0.01\)). Formatting answers with clear intermediate tables for dot products, sigmoids, and gradients ensures maximum marks.
