POS Tagging: Viterbi Algorithm, MEMM, and Advanced Approaches

7.1 HMM for POS Tagging - The Foundation

Hook: You have 45 possible labels for every word in an English sentence. A typical sentence has 8 words. That means \(45^8\) possible tag sequences — an astronomical number. How does any algorithm find the right one without exploring every possibility?

7.1.1 What is an HMM?

A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) is a statistical tool that finds the most likely hidden sequence when you can only see the output.

Intuition + Analogy: Think of a detective investigating a crime. The detective sees evidence (fingerprints, witness statements) but never directly sees the crime. Each piece of evidence slightly favors one suspect over another. The detective combines: how likely is each suspect to have committed this crime (emission), and how likely is each suspect to commit a crime in the first place (prior)? The HMM does the same thing — it sees words (evidence) and infers POS tags (hidden suspects).

Where the analogy breaks: The detective can interview suspects in any order. An HMM must process words strictly left-to-right, one at a time.

Formal Definition: An HMM has three components built from training data:

  • Observation sequence \(O = (o_1, o_2, \ldots, o_T)\) — the words in a sentence (what we see)
  • State sequence \(S = (s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_T)\) — the POS tags (what we want to predict — hidden)
  • Transition matrix \(A\): \(P(\text{tag}_t \mid \text{tag}_{t-1})\) — how likely one tag follows another
  • Emission matrix \(B\): \(P(\text{word} \mid \text{tag})\) — how likely a word appears given a tag
  • Initial probabilities \(\pi\): \(P(\text{tag}_1)\) — how likely a sentence starts with a given tag

For the first word:

\[ P(\text{tag}_1, \text{word}_1) = \underbrace{\pi(\text{tag}_1)}_{\text{prior}} \times \underbrace{P(\text{word}_1 \mid \text{tag}_1)}_{\text{likelihood}} \]

For every subsequent word:

\[ P(\text{tag}_t, \text{word}_t \mid \text{history}) = \underbrace{P(\text{previous path})}_{\text{stored}} \times \underbrace{P(\text{tag}_t \mid \text{tag}_{t-1})}_{\text{transition}} \times \underbrace{P(\text{word}_t \mid \text{tag}_t)}_{\text{emission}} \]

This is posterior \(\propto\) prior \(\times\) likelihood — the same Bayes' rule pattern that appears throughout machine learning.

7.1.2 The Combinatorial Explosion

The Penn Treebank has 45 POS tags (states). A typical English sentence has 8–10 words (observations). A naive HMM would explore every possible tag sequence: \(45^8\) computations. Even with just 2 states (Hot/Cold) and 3 observations, there are \(2^3 = 8\) sequences.

This exponential growth — \(N^T\) where \(N\) is states and \(T\) is observations — makes the naive approach impossible for real sentences. We need an optimization that cuts this down without losing accuracy.

An HMM is a probabilistic sequence model that computes \(P(\text{tag sequence} \mid \text{word sequence})\) using transition × emission products. But the exponential number of paths (\(N^T\)) makes it impractical — which motivates the Viterbi algorithm.

Domain Connection: HMMs are not just for POS tagging. They power speech recognition (mapping audio frames to phonemes), gene sequencing (mapping DNA base pairs to gene boundaries), and even financial modeling (mapping market observations to hidden economic regimes).

7.2 Viterbi Algorithm - The Core Idea

Hook: What if you could eliminate bad paths early, before they waste your computation? Imagine hiking through a dense forest with 45 possible trails at every step. You'd quickly abandon trails that lead into swamps and only follow the promising ones. The Viterbi algorithm does exactly this — it prunes losing paths at every step.

7.2.1 The Key Insight: Greedy Pruning

Intuition + Analogy: Picture a tournament bracket. After round 1, half the players are eliminated. After round 2, half of the remaining are gone. By the final, you have one winner — but you never needed to compute all possible match outcomes. You only tracked the survivors.

Viterbi works the same way. At each word position, it considers all paths coming from the previous position. It computes each path's probability. Then it keeps only the maximum for each state and discards the rest. When you reach position \(t+1\), those discarded paths are dead forever. You never revisit them.

Viterbi Probability Notation:

\[ v_t(s) \]

Superscript \(s\) = the state (POS tag) at this position. Subscript \(t\) = the observation position (word index).

So \(v_2^{\text{VB}}\) means "the Viterbi probability of being in state Verb at the second word."

A backpointer records which previous state produced the max. After the final word, you follow backpointers to reconstruct the full path.

The Recurrence — where the magic happens:

For the first position (\(t=1\)), no pruning yet:

\[ v_1(j) = \pi(j) \cdot b_j(o_1) \]

For all later positions (\(t > 1\)), the pruning step:

\[ v_t(j) = \max_{i} \left[ v_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij} \cdot b_j(o_t) \right] \]

Where:

  • \(v_{t-1}(i)\) = stored Viterbi probability for previous state \(i\)
  • \(a_{ij}\) = transition probability from state \(i\) to state \(j\) — how likely tag \(j\) follows tag \(i\)
  • \(b_j(o_t)\) = emission probability of word \(o_t\) given tag \(j\) — how likely word \(o_t\) appears under tag \(j\)

The \(\max_i\) operator is the pruning. Among all incoming paths from previous states \(i\), take only the best one.

7.2.2 Complexity Reduction

Approach Computations For POS (N=45, T=8)
Naive HMM \(N^T\) (exponential) \(45^8 \approx 1.68 \times 10^{13}\)
Viterbi \(N^2 \times T\) (polynomial) \(45^2 \times 8 = 16{,}200\)

The reduction is from trillions to thousands. For the POS tagging case, this makes the problem tractable on any modern computer.

Real-World Picture: Viterbi is not just a textbook algorithm. Speech-to-text systems (Siri, Google Assistant) use Viterbi decoding to map audio frames to phoneme sequences. Your phone runs this algorithm every time you dictate a message.

Visual Intuition: Draw a grid. Rows are states (POS tags), columns are observations (words). At column 1, put a dot on every state with its \(v_1\) value. At column 2, for each state, draw arrows from all states in column 1. Compute each arrow's probability. Now erase all arrows except the thickest one entering each state. Move to column 3. Only draw arrows from the thick survivors in column 2. Repeat. By the final column, you have exactly \(N\) surviving paths — one per state. Pick the state with the highest \(v_T\) value and trace backward. The takeaway: the trellis narrows dramatically after each pruning step.

Pitfall 1 — Greedy is not perfect: Viterbi is greedy — at each node, it picks only the maximum path and discards alternatives. A path that looks worse locally (e.g., at word 2) might have led to a much better global sequence. Greedy methods can miss the true global optimum. In practice, Viterbi gives very good accuracy — "it's not like very bad" — but if you need the absolute best and can afford the computation, use full HMM without pruning.

Pitfall 2 — Viterbi vs HMM confusion: HMM and Viterbi are not different algorithms. Viterbi is an optimization of HMM where you greedily select the maximum probability at each state instead of exploring all possibilities. The probability calculations (transition × emission) are identical. The only difference is: HMM keeps all paths, Viterbi keeps only the max.

Pitfall 3 — Emission direction: The emission is always \(P(\text{word} \mid \text{tag})\) — word given tag. Never \(P(\text{tag} \mid \text{word})\). The tag is the hidden state. The word is what we observe. The HMM models how hidden states generate observations. When reading the emission matrix, the left side (rows) is the condition — the "given" part.

Viterbi uses dynamic programming with greedy pruning to reduce POS tagging complexity from exponential \(N^T\) to polynomial \(N^2 T\). It stores intermediate results and backpointers, discarding losing paths at every step. The trade-off: speed for a small risk of missing the global optimum.

Domain Connection: Viterbi decoding is the backbone of the modern internet. Every time you use a search engine's autocomplete, a speech assistant's transcription, or a machine translation system, some variant of Viterbi is running under the hood. It is also used in agentic AI systems — when an LLM agent calls a POS tagging API, that API likely runs Viterbi internally.

7.3 Viterbi Worked Example 1 - Ice Cream and Weather

Hook: Jason ate 3 ice creams on Monday, 1 on Tuesday, and 3 on Wednesday. Can you figure out which days were hot and which were cold — just from his ice cream consumption?

7.3.1 Setup

Intuition: People eat more ice cream on hot days. So if Jason ate many ice creams, the day was probably hot. But weather also has momentum — hot days tend to follow hot days. The Viterbi algorithm balances these two clues: today's ice cream count (emission) and yesterday's weather (transition).

Scenario: Jason eats a certain number of ice creams each day. The number he eats depends on the weather (hot or cold). We observe his ice cream consumption over 3 days and want to infer the most likely weather sequence.

  • States: Hot (H), Cold (C) — 2 hidden states
  • Observations: number of ice creams eaten — (3, 1, 3) over 3 days

Given probabilities (from training data):

Probability Value
\(\pi(H)\) = P(start → Hot) 0.8
\(\pi(C)\) = P(start → Cold) 0.2
\(P(3 \mid H)\) 0.4
\(P(3 \mid C)\) 0.1
\(P(1 \mid H)\) 0.2
\(P(1 \mid C)\) 0.5
\(P(H \rightarrow H)\) 0.7
\(P(H \rightarrow C)\) 0.3
\(P(C \rightarrow C)\) 0.6
\(P(C \rightarrow H)\) 0.4

7.3.2 Day 1: Initial Step

For the first day, Jason ate 3 ice creams.

\[ v_1(C) = \pi(C) \times P(3 \mid C) = 0.2 \times 0.1 = 0.02 \] \[ v_1(H) = \pi(H) \times P(3 \mid H) = 0.8 \times 0.4 = 0.32 \]

Both values are stored. No pruning yet — we need both starting points.

7.3.3 Day 2: First Pruning

Jason ate 1 ice cream on day 2.

For day 2 = Cold — two incoming paths:

  • Path A (Cold → Cold): \(v_1(C) \times P(C \rightarrow C) \times P(1 \mid C) = 0.02 \times 0.6 \times 0.5 = 0.006\)
  • Path B (Hot → Cold): \(v_1(H) \times P(H \rightarrow C) \times P(1 \mid C) = 0.32 \times 0.3 \times 0.5 = 0.048\)

Path B is larger (0.048 > 0.006). Keep Path B, discard Path A. Store \(v_2(C) = 0.048\) with backpointer to Hot.

For day 2 = Hot — two incoming paths:

  • Path C (Cold → Hot): \(v_1(C) \times P(C \rightarrow H) \times P(1 \mid H) = 0.02 \times 0.4 \times 0.2 = 0.0016\)
  • Path D (Hot → Hot): \(v_1(H) \times P(H \rightarrow H) \times P(1 \mid H) = 0.32 \times 0.7 \times 0.2 = 0.0448\)

Path D is larger (0.0448 > 0.0016). Keep Path D, discard Path C. Store \(v_2(H) = 0.0448\) with backpointer to Hot.

When we move to day 3, paths A and C are dead. We will never revisit them.

7.3.4 Day 3: Continued Pruning

Jason ate 3 ice creams on day 3.

For day 3 = Cold — only from surviving day-2 states:

  • From \(v_2(C) = 0.048\): \(0.048 \times P(C \rightarrow C) \times P(3 \mid C) = 0.048 \times 0.6 \times 0.1 = 0.00288\)
  • From \(v_2(H) = 0.0448\): \(0.0448 \times P(H \rightarrow C) \times P(3 \mid C) = 0.0448 \times 0.3 \times 0.1 = 0.001344\)

Max is from Cold → Cold. Store \(v_3(C) = 0.00288\), backpointer to C.

For day 3 = Hot — from surviving day-2 states:

  • From \(v_2(C)\): \(0.048 \times P(C \rightarrow H) \times P(3 \mid H) = 0.048 \times 0.4 \times 0.4 = 0.00768\)
  • From \(v_2(H)\): \(0.0448 \times P(H \rightarrow H) \times P(3 \mid H) = 0.0448 \times 0.7 \times 0.4 = 0.012544\)

Max is from Hot → Hot. Store \(v_3(H) = 0.012544\), backpointer to H.

7.3.5 Final Selection

At \(t=3\), compare \(v_3(H) = 0.012544\) and \(v_3(C) = 0.00288\). Hot wins.

Trace backpointers:

  • Day 3: Hot (backpointer → day-2 Hot)
  • Day 2: Hot (backpointer → day-1 Hot)
  • Day 1: Hot

Result: The most likely weather sequence is Hot → Hot → Hot.

Sense-check: Jason ate lots of ice cream (3, then 1, then 3). Hot days are more likely to produce high ice cream counts (emission of 3 given Hot = 0.4 vs 0.1 for Cold). Hot days also tend to persist (Hot → Hot = 0.7). The result makes intuitive sense.

Visual Intuition: Draw a 2×3 grid (rows: H, C; columns: Day 1, 2, 3). At Day 1, dots at H (0.32) and C (0.02). At Day 2, for state C, two arrows come in — erase the thin one from C (0.006), keep the thick one from H (0.048). For state H, erase the thin one from C (0.0016), keep the thick one from H (0.0448). At Day 3, only the thick survivors extend forward. The final trellis shows a dominant path: H → H → H. The takeaway: after Day 2, the Cold-starting paths are effectively dead.

Common pitfalls in Viterbi hand calculations:

  1. Don't multiply probabilities across different states carelessly. The max is per incoming state — you compare paths to the same destination state, not across different states.
  2. The backpointer records which previous state won, not the probability. You use it only at the end to trace back.
  3. Pruning happens per state, not globally. At day 2, you keep one survivor for Cold AND one survivor for Hot. You don't pick only the global max across both states.

The ice cream example shows Viterbi in microcosm: 2 states, 3 observations, pruning after position 2. The winning path (Hot → Hot → Hot) emerges from combining emission clues (ice cream count) with transition clues (weather persistence). The algorithm never explores the full \(2^3 = 8\) paths — it prunes to 2 survivors after each step.

Domain Connection: This toy weather example is the canonical Viterbi teaching example from the Jurafsky & Martin textbook. The same pattern — hidden states generating observable emissions — appears across many domains. Credit card fraud detection uses it (hidden: fraud/legit, observed: transaction patterns). Sleep stage analysis maps EEG signals to REM, deep, or light sleep. Keyboard autocorrect infers the intended word from typed characters.

7.4 Viterbi Worked Example 2 - "I want to race"

Hook: The word "race" could be a noun (a competition) or a verb (to compete). In the phrase "I want to race," how does the algorithm decide that "race" is a noun and "to" is a preposition — without a dictionary?

7.4.1 Setup

Intuition: Imagine you're reading a sentence one word at a time, blindfolded about everything except POS tags. After "I," you're almost certain it's a pronoun. After "I want," you know "want" is likely a verb following a pronoun. By the time you reach "race," the accumulated evidence from the previous three words — pronoun → verb → preposition — screams that "race" should be a noun. Viterbi accumulates this evidence step by step.

Problem: Find the best POS tag sequence for: "I want to race"

States (POS tags in this toy example):

Tag Meaning
PPSS Pronoun
VB Verb
TO Preposition "to" (Penn Treebank specific)
NN Noun

Given probabilities from training data:

Transition (\(A\))
PPSS VB TO NN
start 0.19 0.0043 0.041 0.067
PPSS 0.004 0.34 0.00014 0.037
VB 0.003 0.0036 0.33 0.031
TO 0.53 0.0 0.0 0.47
NN 0.002 0.033 0.0 0.0014
Emission (\(B\))
I want to race
PPSS 0.37 0.0 0.0 0.0
VB 0.0 0.41 0.0 0.0
TO 0.0 0.0 0.75 0.0
NN 0.0 0.0 0.0 0.13

7.4.2 Step 1: First Word - "I"

For each possible tag of "I":

\[ v_1(\text{tag}) = \pi(\text{tag}) \times P(\text{"I"} \mid \text{tag}) \]
  • \(v_1(\text{PPSS}) = 0.067 \times 0.37 = 0.0248\)
  • \(v_1(\text{VB}) = 0.0043 \times 0.0 = 0\)
  • \(v_1(\text{TO}) = 0.041 \times 0.0 = 0\)
  • \(v_1(\text{NN}) = 0.067 \times 0.0 = 0\)

Only PPSS has a non-zero emission for "I."

Textbook typo alert: Some editions of the Jurafsky & Martin diagram show \(v_1(\text{PPSS}) = 0.055\), which is incorrect. The correct value from multiplying the given transition (0.067) and emission (0.37) is approximately 0.0248. Always verify by computing directly from the matrices — don't blindly trust the diagram.

7.4.3 Step 2: Second Word - "want"

We want \(v_2(\text{VB})\) — probability that "want" is a verb.

For each possible previous tag \(i\):

\[ v_2(\text{VB}) = \max_i \left[ v_1(i) \times P(i \rightarrow \text{VB}) \times P(\text{"want"} \mid \text{VB}) \right] \]
  • From PPSS: \(0.0248 \times 0.34 \times 0.41 = 0.00346\)
  • From VB: \(0 \times \ldots = 0\)
  • From TO: \(0 \times \ldots = 0\)
  • From NN: \(0 \times \ldots = 0\)

Only the PPSS path survives. Store \(v_2(\text{VB}) = 0.00346\) with backpointer to PPSS.

The same process repeats for every possible tag at position 2 (VB, NN, TO, PPSS). At each node, take the max over all incoming paths.

7.4.4 Steps 3-4: Third and Fourth Words

For "to" (position 3): compute \(v_3\) for each tag, taking max over surviving \(v_2\) values. The paths that were not the maximum at position 2 are not carried forward.

For "race" (position 4): same process. After the final word, the backpointers trace back.

Result: "I / want / to / race" → PPSS / VB / TO / NN

Pronoun, Verb, Preposition (Penn Treebank "to"), Noun.

Sense-check: "I" is unambiguously a pronoun (only PPSS emits "I"). "want" follows a pronoun and is a verb. "to" is the infinitive marker. "race" after "to" is a noun — "to race" as in "I want to [attend a] race." The sequence PPSS → VB → TO → NN is syntactically coherent.

Visual Intuition: The trellis is 4×4 (4 states × 4 words). Most cells are zero because most words can only be emitted by one tag. This sparsity makes the Viterbi path almost unique. The winning path is a single bold line: PPSS → VB → TO → NN. All other paths die early because emission probabilities of zero kill them at the first step.

Key exam tip: Exam problems will use simpler numbers than this — typically 2–3 states with many zero values. The goal is to test understanding of the algorithm flow, not arithmetic skill. You'll likely see more zeros to make hand calculation easy.

Common student confusion: "How do I know which previous state to multiply?" You multiply ALL previous states and take the max. Even when some are zero (making the max trivial), you still conceptually consider all incoming paths.

The "I want to race" example shows Viterbi on a real (toy) POS tagging problem. Sparse emission matrices (zeros) dramatically simplify the computation — most paths die at the first word. The surviving path PPSS → VB → TO → NN emerges from cumulative evidence across all four words.

Domain Connection: This example is from the Jurafsky & Martin textbook appendix. The Penn Treebank tagset includes fine-grained distinctions like "TO" (the word "to" as a preposition/infinitive marker) versus other prepositions. Real POS taggers use all 45 Penn Treebank tags, but the Viterbi logic scales identically.

We want the best tag for "doctor." Consider tag = Noun.

For each previous tag \(i\), compute:

\[ v_1(i) \times P(i \rightarrow \text{N}) \times P(\text{"doctor"} \mid \text{N}) \]

7.5.4 Steps 3-4 and Final Selection

Result: "The / doctor / is / in" → DET / N / V / PREP

Determiner, Noun, Verb, Preposition.

Sense-check: "The" is the classic determiner. Determiners strongly predict nouns (transition DET → N = 0.9). "is" is a form of "to be" — a verb. "in" after "is" is a preposition (as in "the doctor is in [the office]"). The sequence is syntactically standard English.

The "The doctor is in" example demonstrates Viterbi with 5 states and 4 words. The sparse emission matrices again make most paths zero, so the surviving path is nearly unique. The algorithm correctly identifies DET → N → V → PREP by combining transition and emission evidence.

7.6 How to Read the Matrices

Hook: One of the most common exam mistakes is reading the matrices backward. Here is the definitive rule so you never get it wrong.

Transition Matrix \(A\): \(P(\text{current tag} \mid \text{previous tag})\)

  • Rows = previous tag (the condition — what we already know)
  • Columns = current tag (what we want to predict)
  • Read as: "Given the previous tag was X, what is the probability the current tag is Y?"

Emission Matrix \(B\): \(P(\text{word} \mid \text{tag})\)

  • Rows = tag (the condition — the hidden state)
  • Columns = word (what we observe)
  • Read as: "Given the tag is X, what is the probability the word is Y?"

The emission is always word given tag, never tag given word. The tag is the hidden state we want to infer. The word is what we observe. The HMM models how hidden states generate observations — hence "emission."

The #1 student mistake: Reading the emission matrix as \(P(\text{tag} \mid \text{word})\) instead of \(P(\text{word} \mid \text{tag})\). In the emission table, the left side (row label) is always the condition — the "given" part. So "doctor given noun" means: look at the noun ROW, doctor COLUMN.

Memory aid: The HMM is a generative model. It imagines: "If I were a noun, what words would I emit?" That is why emission = \(P(\text{word} \mid \text{tag})\). The tag generates the word, not vice versa.

Transition: row = previous, column = current. Emission: row = tag, column = word. Always \(P(\text{word} \mid \text{tag})\) — word given tag. This comes from Bayes' rule: posterior \(\propto\) likelihood × prior.

7.7 Maximum Entropy Markov Model (MEMM)

Hook: An HMM always reads left-to-right. It can never peek at the next word to disambiguate the current one. But sometimes, the word after an ambiguous word is the key to its identity. How do we build a model that can look in both directions?

7.7.1 The Problem with HMM: Unidirectionality

Intuition + Analogy: Imagine translating a sentence from a foreign language. You read "I went to the..." and the next word is cut off. You can't know if it's "bank" (financial institution) or "bank" (river side). But if you could peek ahead and see "...of the river," you'd know instantly. An HMM is like a translator who refuses to look ahead — it can only use what came before. MEMM fixes this by letting the model see words on both sides.

The core relationship: HMM models \(P(\text{word} \mid \text{tag})\) — tag generates word. MEMM directly models \(P(\text{tag} \mid \text{word}, \text{context})\) — context predicts tag. This flips the arrow of inference.

7.7.2 MEMM: Framing POS Tagging as Classification

MEMM treats POS tagging as a discriminative classification problem rather than a generative one.

HMM (Generative) MEMM (Discriminative)
Models \(P(\text{word} \mid \text{tag})\) and \(P(\text{tag}_t \mid \text{tag}_{t-1})\) separately \(P(\text{tag}_t \mid \text{word}_t, \text{previous tags}, \text{surrounding words})\) directly
Approach Builds a story of how tags generate words Learns a boundary between tag classes
Math Multiply emission × transition Any classifier: logistic regression, SVM, Naive Bayes, neural network

The POS tags are treated as class labels. Any multi-class classification algorithm can be used. Logistic regression (using the sigmoid/softmax function) is particularly popular — and the sigmoid function remains widely used even in modern agentic AI systems.

7.7.3 Features in MEMM

Instead of separate transition and emission probabilities, MEMM combines everything into features. These can include:

  • The current word
  • The previous word, the word before that
  • The next word, the word after that (this is the bidirectionality advantage!)
  • The POS tag of the previous word
  • The POS tag of the previous two words
  • … and so on, up to the entire sentence

Because these are just features fed into a classifier, MEMM can look at features from both directions — before and after the current word. This is the fundamental advantage over HMM.

7.7.4 How MEMM Works

  1. Training: Given labeled data (e.g., Penn Treebank — a high-quality manually annotated dataset), learn feature weights using any classifier. The model learns which features predict which POS tags.
  2. Inference: For each word in a new sentence, compute \(P(\text{tag} \mid \text{features})\). Select the tag with maximum probability for each word, greedily moving through the sentence — similar to the Viterbi greedy approach.

Example: Disambiguating "back"

The word "back" can be a noun (body part), verb (to support), adjective (rear side), or adverb (return). Using MEMM features:

  • Previous word = "the" → favors noun or adjective
  • Next word = "of" → strongly favors noun ("the back of the chair")
  • Previous word = "to" → favors verb ("to back the proposal")
  • POS of previous word = determiner → favors noun

All these features together help the classifier pick the right tag for each occurrence.

7.7.5 The Remaining Limitation

MEMM's weakness — missing global sequence context: MEMM looks at surrounding words as features, but it does not truly model the full sequential context the way HMM does. It classifies each word somewhat independently, with features capturing local context. The dependencies between tags across the whole sentence are not fully captured. For "I want to race," MEMM might correctly tag each word individually, but it doesn't explicitly optimize the sequence PPSS → VB → TO → NN as a whole.

This limitation motivates the next class of approaches: neural sequence models that model both local context AND global tag dependencies.

Real-World Picture: MEMM-style feature engineering is used in production NLP pipelines where explainability matters. A financial compliance system that flags suspicious transactions needs to explain why it labeled a word as an "amount" rather than a "date" — and feature-based models provide that transparency.

Visual Intuition: Picture a sliding window over the sentence. At the word "bank," the window shows: ... the [bank] of the river ... Both the left context ("the") and right context ("of the river") are visible as features. The classifier weighs each feature: "previous word = 'the'" gets weight \(w_1\), "next word = 'of'" gets weight \(w_2\), etc. The weighted sum passes through a sigmoid to produce a probability for each tag. The takeaway: MEMM sees a window around each word, not just the left side.

MEMM reframes POS tagging as a classification problem using engineered features from both left and right context. It achieves bidirectionality that HMM cannot — at the cost of not fully modeling global tag sequence dependencies. No mathematical problems on MEMM in exams, only conceptual and application-level questions.

Domain Connection: Maximum Entropy principles appear throughout machine learning. The idea of "the simplest model that fits the constraints" — Occam's Razor formalized mathematically — is the foundation of logistic regression. Logistic regression powers credit scoring, medical diagnosis, and spam filtering. The sigmoid/softmax function from logistic regression is the same activation used in the output layer of modern transformers.

7.8 Bi-LSTM + CRF

Hook: MEMM can look at surrounding words, but it doesn't enforce that the sequence of tags makes sense. What if you could combine a neural network that sees both directions with a layer that enforces "determiners are usually followed by nouns" — getting the best of both worlds?

7.8.1 Two Components, Two Jobs

Intuition + Analogy: Imagine two proofreaders working together. The first proofreader (Bi-LSTM) reads every word in context — both what comes before and after — and suggests a tag. The second proofreader (CRF) checks whether the sequence of tags follows the rules of English grammar. "The" followed by "cat" makes sense. "The" followed by "running" as a noun? The CRF catches that and adjusts.

Component What it captures Analogy
Bi-LSTM Local context: words before and after the current word First proofreader: reads in both directions
CRF Global constraints: dependencies between neighboring POS tags Second proofreader: checks grammar rules

7.8.2 Bi-LSTM: Bidirectional Context

LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) is a recurrent neural network designed for sequences. Unlike simple RNNs, LSTM avoids the vanishing gradient problem using gates:

  • Forget gate: decides what old information to discard
  • Input gate: decides what new information to store
  • Output gate: decides what to output at this step

A standard (unidirectional) LSTM processes input left-to-right and captures about 7–8 words of context.

A bidirectional LSTM (Bi-LSTM) runs two LSTMs:

  • One left-to-right (captures preceding context)
  • One right-to-left (captures following context)
  • Outputs are combined

For the sentence "I want to race", to tag "want":

  • Left LSTM sees: "I" (pronoun, suggests a verb follows)
  • Right LSTM sees: "to race" (infinitive construction, confirms verb)
  • Combined: strong evidence for VB

7.8.3 CRF: Global Label Dependencies

The Bi-LSTM alone predicts each word's tag independently. It does not check whether the sequence of tags is coherent. In English, a determiner ("the") is very often followed by a noun. A standalone Bi-LSTM classifier might miss this.

The Conditional Random Field (CRF) layer sits on top of the Bi-LSTM and models:

  • Emission scores: How likely tag \(y_t\) is for word \(x_t\) (from Bi-LSTM output)
  • Transition scores: How likely it is to move from tag \(y_{t-1}\) to tag \(y_t\) (learned by CRF)

The CRF finds the best sequence of labels by considering both scores together. It performs a Viterbi-like decoding over the tag sequence.

CRF Scoring Function (conceptual):

For a sequence of words \(X = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)\) and candidate tags \(Y = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)\):

\[ \text{Score}(X, Y) = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \left[ \text{Emission}(y_t \mid x_t) + \text{Transition}(y_t \mid y_{t-1}) \right] \] \[ P(Y \mid X) = \frac{\exp(\text{Score}(X, Y))}{\sum_{Y'} \exp(\text{Score}(X, Y'))} \]

This is a softmax over all possible tag sequences — normalized by the sum over every candidate sequence \(Y'\).

Why CRF helps: "The cat runs"

Without CRF (Bi-LSTM only):

  • "The" → DET (correct, high confidence)
  • "cat" → N (correct, high confidence)
  • "runs" → V (correct, high confidence)

Sequence: DET → N → V ✓ (by coincidence, all correct)

Now imagine a harder case: "The running water"

Without CRF (Bi-LSTM only):

  • "The" → DET
  • "running" → V (the Bi-LSTM might favor verb because "running" usually is one)
  • "water" → N

Sequence: DET → V → N ✗ (V after DET is unusual)

With CRF: The CRF's transition score penalizes DET → V (this transition is rare in the training data). The global sequence DET → ADJ → N may score higher overall, correctly tagging "running" as an adjective.

The CRF makes the model say: "Even though 'running' looks like a verb, the whole sentence makes more sense if I tag it as an adjective here."

7.8.4 Performance

Bi-LSTM + CRF achieves approximately 97% accuracy on POS tagging — very strong performance. For many applications, this is good enough without needing more complex transformer models.

Trade-off: Training is slower than transformer-based approaches because RNNs are sequential (each step depends on the previous) and harder to parallelize.

Pitfall — Bi-LSTM + CRF vs MEMM: Both can look at surrounding words, but the mechanism is different. MEMM uses hand-engineered features (previous word, next word, previous POS). Bi-LSTM learns features automatically through neural network weights. Students sometimes conflate these — remember: MEMM = manual feature engineering, Bi-LSTM = automatic feature learning.

Pitfall — CRF is not a separate classifier: The CRF is a layer on top of the Bi-LSTM. It doesn't replace the Bi-LSTM; it complements it. Bi-LSTM handles word-level context, CRF handles tag-level sequence constraints.

Bi-LSTM + CRF is a neural baseline for sequence tagging: Bi-LSTM captures bidirectional word context (up to ~7-8 words each direction), CRF enforces global tag sequence coherence via transition scores. Together they achieve ~97% accuracy on POS tagging.

Domain Connection: Bi-LSTM + CRF is not just for POS tagging. It is the workhorse architecture for Named Entity Recognition (NER) — identifying person names, organizations, locations in text. Every time Google extracts "Apple Inc." as a company from a news article, a Bi-LSTM + CRF variant likely did the work. It is also used in conversational AI systems for intent detection and slot filling.

7.9 BERT and Transformers for POS Tagging

Hook: What if a model could see the entire sentence at once — every word attending to every other word? Instead of just the 7–8 word window that an LSTM can manage. This is the breakthrough that transformers brought. It pushed POS tagging accuracy close to 100%.

7.9.1 Self-Attention: The Breakthrough

Intuition + Analogy: Imagine a committee meeting where everyone can whisper to everyone else simultaneously. When discussing the word "bank," the member representing "bank" can listen to "river" (suggesting river bank), "money" (suggesting financial bank), and "the" (suggesting a noun follows). All these whispers happen in parallel, and "bank" forms its opinion by weighted attention to every other word in the room.

This is self-attention. Every word computes attention scores with every other word. No matter how far apart two words are — even opposite ends of a long sentence — they can directly influence each other. This solves the context-length limitation of LSTMs and the unidirectionality of HMMs in one stroke.

The Transformer architecture, introduced in "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017), uses self-attention as its core mechanism.

Part Job Example model
Encoder Reads input, builds contextual representations BERT
Decoder Generates output, auto-regressive (one token at a time) GPT
  • BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) uses only the encoder. It reads the entire sentence bidirectionally — every word sees every other word.
  • GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) uses only the decoder. It generates text left-to-right, one token at a time.
  • For POS tagging, the encoder part (BERT-style) is used.

Multiple attention heads capture different types of relationships:

  • One head might focus on syntactic dependencies (subject-verb agreement)
  • Another might focus on semantic relationships (word meaning in context)
  • Another might focus on positional patterns (how far apart related words are)

7.9.2 Fine-Tuning for POS Tagging

Large language models like BERT are pre-trained on massive web-scale data with a general objective (masked language modeling — predicting blanked-out words in sentences).

To use them for POS tagging:

  1. Take a pre-trained BERT model (16–32 encoder layers, millions of parameters).
  2. Add a classification layer on top (since POS tagging is a multi-class classification problem over 45 Penn Treebank tags).
  3. Fine-tune on labeled POS data (e.g., Penn Treebank). During fine-tuning, most parameters are frozen — only the last few layers are updated. This avoids the cost of retraining all parameters.
  4. The model learns to map each word's contextual embedding to a POS tag.

Why not retrain everything? If you retrain all 175+ billion parameters, it is equivalent to training from scratch — defeating the purpose of transfer learning. Fine-tuning adjusts only a small subset of parameters, keeping the general language knowledge intact.

7.9.3 Advantages of Transformers

Advantage Why it matters
Near 100% accuracy State-of-the-art on standard POS tagging benchmarks
Parallelizable Encoder layers process all words simultaneously (unlike RNNs, which must go step by step)
Unlimited context Self-attention captures dependencies across any distance in the sentence
No manual feature engineering The model learns relevant features automatically through attention
Rich library ecosystem Hugging Face provides open-source BERT with POS tagging support; spaCy integrates transformer-based taggers

BERT Variants:

Model Key difference
BERT-base 12 encoder layers, 110M parameters
BERT-large 24 encoder layers, 340M parameters
RoBERTa Optimized training of BERT — better performance
DistilBERT 40% smaller, 60% faster, 97% of BERT's performance
ALBERT Parameter-sharing across layers for efficiency

Real-World Picture: When you use spaCy's en_core_web_trf model for POS tagging in Python, you are using a transformer-based tagger. Hugging Face's transformers library lets you load a pre-trained BERT model and fine-tune it for POS tagging with as few as 10 lines of code.

Visual Intuition: Draw a heatmap where rows and columns are words in a sentence. Each cell shows the attention score — how much word \(i\) attends to word \(j\). For the word "race" in "I want to race": high attention to "to" (syntactic: infinitive marker → noun) and moderate attention to "want" (semantic: what is wanted). The diagonals are always bright (every word attends to itself). The takeaway: attention is not uniform — certain word pairs have strong connections that encode grammatical relationships.

Pitfall — BERT is not a POS tagger out of the box: Pre-trained BERT knows about language but doesn't know about the Penn Treebank tagset. You must fine-tune it on labeled POS data. The pre-training gives it general linguistic knowledge; fine-tuning teaches it the specific classification task.

Pitfall — Encoder vs Decoder confusion: POS tagging uses the encoder (BERT-style), not the decoder (GPT-style). The encoder reads and understands; the decoder generates. POS tagging is a reading/understanding task, not a generation task.

Pitfall — Cost: BERT-large has 340M parameters. Fine-tuning requires GPU compute. For simple applications, Bi-LSTM + CRF (97% accuracy) may be sufficient and cheaper.

Transformers use self-attention to let every word attend to every other word, capturing unlimited bidirectional context. Fine-tuning pre-trained BERT on labeled POS data achieves near-perfect accuracy. The encoder-only architecture (BERT) is used for POS tagging, not the decoder (GPT).

Domain Connection: BERT's self-attention mechanism powers virtually all modern NLP. Google Search uses BERT to understand queries. Chatbots use transformer encoders to encode conversation context. Code completion tools use transformer variants to understand programming syntax. The "Attention Is All You Need" paper (2017) is one of the most influential papers in AI history.

7.10 Prompting Approaches

Hook: What if you didn't need to fine-tune a model at all? What if you could just ask a large language model to do POS tagging by giving it instructions in plain English?

7.10.1 Prompting Instead of Fine-Tuning

Intuition + Analogy: Think of a skilled linguist who knows dozens of languages. You don't need to retrain them for every task. You just give them clear instructions: "Label each word in this sentence with its part of speech using the Penn Treebank tagset." The linguist uses their general language knowledge to follow your instruction. Prompting works the same way — the LLM uses its pre-trained knowledge, guided by your prompt.

Instead of fine-tuning, modern LLMs can be used for POS tagging through prompting:

  • Hard prompting: Give explicit instructions. Example: "Tag the following sentence using Penn Treebank POS tags. Output only word-tag pairs: 'The cat sat on the mat.'"
  • Soft prompting: Learn continuous prompt embeddings during training — the prompt is not human-readable text but learned vectors that optimize task performance.
  • Few-shot prompting: Provide 2–5 examples of correctly tagged sentences in the prompt. The model generalizes from these examples to new sentences.

Prompting avoids the need for a separate fine-tuning dataset. It can be done at runtime. However, it incurs per-token API costs and may be less reliable than fine-tuned models for specialized domains.

Pitfall — Prompting is not always reliable: An LLM prompted for POS tagging might produce inconsistent output formats, hallucinate Penn Treebank tags, or struggle with rare words. Fine-tuned models, being specialized, are generally more reliable for production POS tagging.

Pitfall — Cost adds up: Every API call for POS tagging costs tokens. For batch processing millions of sentences, fine-tuning a small specialized model is far cheaper than calling a massive LLM each time.

Prompting lets LLMs perform POS tagging without fine-tuning by providing task instructions in natural language. It is flexible but less reliable and more expensive per-token than specialized models. Expect conceptual-level exam questions on prompting approaches.

7.11 LLM Agents for POS Tagging

Hook: In modern AI systems, POS tagging is rarely the final goal. An agent tasked with sentiment analysis might use POS tagging as one step in a multi-step workflow. It calls specialized APIs — like a manager delegating to experts.

7.11.1 How Agents Use POS Tagging

Intuition + Analogy: Think of a project manager (the LLM agent) who needs to analyze customer feedback. The manager doesn't do every task personally. Instead, she delegates: "You, POS tagging specialist, label every word. You, sentiment analyst, look at the adjectives and score the sentiment. You, NER specialist, extract product names." The manager orchestrates — she breaks the big task into subtasks, calls the right specialists, and combines their outputs.

This is exactly how LLM agents work. They use function calling / tool use to invoke specialized APIs. The agent plans, the tools execute.

7.11.2 The Agent Workflow

Example: Agent analyzes a product review

User: "Analyze the sentiment of this review: 'The camera takes stunning photos but the battery dies too quickly.'"

The agent orchestrates:

  1. Task decomposition: Break into subtasks — POS tag the review, extract adjectives, analyze sentiment, summarize findings.
  2. Function call 1 — POS tagging API: Tag every word in the review.
    • Output: The/DET camera/NN takes/VB stunning/ADJ photos/NN but/CONJ the/DET battery/NN dies/VB too/ADV quickly/ADV
  3. Function call 2 — Extract adjectives: Identify "stunning" (positive) and "too quickly" (negative, adverbial phrase).
  4. Function call 3 — Sentiment API: Score the adjective phrases.
    • "stunning photos" → positive (score: +0.9)
    • "battery dies too quickly" → negative (score: −0.7)
  5. Aggregate: Overall sentiment is mixed — positive about camera quality, negative about battery life.

7.11.3 Why Not Always Use the Biggest Model?

Even when agents have access to powerful LLMs, they often call simpler, specialized APIs for POS tagging because:

  • Cost: An H100 GPU server costs approximately ₹20 lakhs and supports limited compute. Running every POS tagging through a 175B-parameter model is expensive. Smaller specialized models are faster and cheaper.
  • Speed: Smaller, specialized models (like a fine-tuned Bi-LSTM + CRF) are faster per inference.
  • Explainability: Simpler models (HMM, Viterbi, MEMM) produce interpretable results. You can trace exactly why a word got a particular tag. With a black-box LLM, you cannot.
  • Data privacy: Enterprises with NDAs cannot send sensitive financial or medical text to third-party LLM APIs. They need on-premise, specialized models.

LLM agents use POS tagging as a subtask via function calls to specialized APIs. The hybrid approach — agents orchestrate, specialized models execute — balances cost, speed, accuracy, and explainability. All methods from HMM to transformers remain relevant in production.

Domain Connection: Agentic AI frameworks like LangChain, semantic-kernel, and AutoGen use function-calling patterns to delegate tasks to specialized tools. MCP (Model Context Protocol) and A2A (Agent-to-Agent) protocols are emerging standards for this kind of orchestration. In industry, very few systems use LLMs for every subtask — the practical reality is a hybrid stack.

7.12 Modern Challenges in POS Tagging

Hook: With near-100% accuracy on standard English benchmarks, you might think POS tagging is "solved." But real-world accuracy drops dramatically on medical reports or Hindi sentences. The benchmarks don't tell the whole story.

7.12.1 Domain-Specific Vocabulary

Generic POS tagging models fail on specialized text:

  • Medical vocabulary: Drug names (paracetamol, ibuprofen), anatomical terms (tibia, cerebellum), procedural terms — a generic model has never seen these words and doesn't know whether they are nouns, verbs, or something else.
  • Finance vocabulary: Legal terms, financial instruments, regulatory language — the word "call" means something entirely different in finance (a call option) vs. everyday English.
  • Legal vocabulary: Archaic phrasing, specialized jargon, Latin terms — "hereinafter," "pursuant to," "prima facie" break generic models.

The fix — fine-tuning — requires: labeled domain data (expensive to create), compute resources (H100 GPUs are costly), and expertise. These are not always available.

7.12.2 Low-Resource Languages

Most POS tagging research and tooling focuses on English and a few other high-resource languages (Chinese, Arabic, French, German, Spanish). Indian languages and many other languages lack:

  • Large annotated corpora (like the Penn Treebank for English)
  • Pre-trained transformer models (like BERT for Hindi, Tamil, Telugu)
  • Standardized tag sets

These languages present ongoing research challenges. Building POS taggers for low-resource languages is an active area of work, with techniques like cross-lingual transfer learning showing promise.

7.12.3 Cost and Environmental Impact

  • Training and running large models requires substantial compute — H100 GPUs costing ~₹20 lakhs each, with data center energy costs on top.
  • Carbon footprint of large-scale NLP training is a growing concern — training a single large transformer can emit as much CO₂ as several cars over their lifetimes.
  • Per-token API pricing means using LLMs for high-volume POS tagging has a real monetary cost.
  • Prompt caching and other optimization techniques are being developed to reduce these costs.

7.12.4 Explainability

Intuition: When a bank rejects your loan application, they cannot say: "Our 175-billion-parameter model decided so." You — and regulators — demand to know why. Statistical models (HMM, Viterbi, MEMM) are inherently explainable — you can trace the exact probabilities that led to a decision. Transformer-based models are black boxes.

This explainability gap is critical in regulated industries (finance, healthcare, law). Tools like TruLens are emerging to add explainability layers to deep learning models, but simpler models remain the default when transparency is mandatory.

Pitfall — "100% accuracy" is misleading: Benchmarks measure performance on standard test sets (like Penn Treebank sections). Real-world text — especially domain-specific or low-resource — performs much worse. Always consider the deployment context, not just the benchmark number.

POS tagging is not "solved." Domain-specific vocabulary, low-resource languages, computational cost, and explainability requirements keep all approaches — from HMM to transformers — relevant in practice. The right tool depends on the context: accuracy needs, budget, language, and transparency requirements.

Domain Connection: The challenges in POS tagging mirror broader challenges in NLP. The field is not just about pushing benchmark scores higher — it is about making models work reliably in the wild, for all languages, at reasonable cost, with explainable outputs. These are the problems that researchers and practitioners grapple with daily.

Hidden Markov Model (HMM) for POS Tagging

Must-know: An HMM computes \(P(\text{tag sequence} \mid \text{word sequence})\) using the product of transition (\(A\): \(P(\text{tag}_t \mid \text{tag}_{t-1})\)) and emission (\(B\): \(P(\text{word} \mid \text{tag})\)) probabilities. The emission is always word-given-tag, never tag-given-word. The naive HMM explores all \(N^T\) paths — exponential in sentence length — making it impractical without optimization.

\[ P(\text{tag}_1, \text{word}_1) = \pi(\text{tag}_1) \times P(\text{word}_1 \mid \text{tag}_1) \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Reading the emission matrix as \(P(\text{tag} \mid \text{word})\) instead of \(P(\text{word} \mid \text{tag})\). In the emission table, the row label (left side) is the condition — the "given" part.

Self-check: Why does a naive HMM need to explore \(N^T\) possible tag sequences for a sentence with \(T\) words and \(N\) possible tags?

Connects to: Viterbi Algorithm, MEMM, How to Read Matrices

Viterbi Algorithm — Core Mechanism

Must-know: Viterbi is a greedy dynamic programming optimization of HMM. At each state \(j\) and time \(t\), it keeps only the maximum incoming path probability and discards all others. This reduces complexity from exponential \(N^T\) to polynomial \(N^2 T\). For Penn Treebank POS tagging (\(N=45\), \(T=8\)), this means 16,200 computations instead of trillions.

\[ v_t(j) = \max_i \left[ v_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij} \cdot b_j(o_t) \right] \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Thinking Viterbi and HMM are different algorithms. Viterbi is an optimization of HMM — same probability calculations, different path management. Also: pruning happens per state, not globally — you keep one survivor for each state at each time step.

Self-check: For \(N=45\) tags and \(T=8\) words, how many computations does Viterbi perform vs. a naive HMM?

Connects to: HMM for POS Tagging, All Viterbi Worked Examples, Bi-LSTM + CRF

Viterbi Worked Example — Ice Cream & Weather

Must-know: 2 states (Hot, Cold), 3 observations (3, 1, 3 ice creams). Day 1: \(v_1(H) = 0.32\), \(v_1(C) = 0.02\). Day 2: pruning keeps Hot→Cold (0.048) over Cold→Cold (0.006), and Hot→Hot (0.0448) over Cold→Hot (0.0016). Day 3: Hot→Hot (0.012544) wins over all alternatives. Final path: Hot → Hot → Hot. Sense-check: high ice cream counts favor hot days, and weather tends to persist.

\[ v_1(j) = \pi(j) \cdot b_j(o_1), \quad v_t(j) = \max_i [v_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij} \cdot b_j(o_t)] \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Multiplying across different destination states when computing the max. You compare paths to the same destination state — e.g., for day 2 = Cold, you compare only paths that arrive at Cold, not paths arriving at Hot.

Self-check: Walk through the ice cream example with observations (1, 3, 1) — which weather sequence wins?

Connects to: Viterbi Core Mechanism, "I want to race" Example, "The doctor is in" Example

Maximum Entropy Markov Model (MEMM)

Must-know: MEMM reframes POS tagging as a discriminative classification problem: directly models \(P(\text{tag}_t \mid \text{word}_t, \text{previous tags}, \text{surrounding words})\). Its key advantage over HMM is bidirectionality — it can use features from both left and right context (e.g., next word, two words ahead). Uses any classifier (logistic regression, SVM). Limitation: classifies words somewhat independently; does not truly model global tag sequence dependencies.

\[ P(\text{tag}_t \mid \text{word}_t, \text{previous tags}, \text{surrounding words as features}) \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Confusing MEMM's feature-based bidirectionality with Bi-LSTM's neural bidirectional context. MEMM = manual feature engineering (you write: "previous word = X, next word = Y"). Bi-LSTM = automatic feature learning through neural weights.

Self-check: What is the fundamental limitation of HMM that MEMM addresses, and what limitation of MEMM does Bi-LSTM + CRF address?

Connects to: HMM, Bi-LSTM + CRF, BERT and Transformers

Bi-LSTM + CRF

Must-know: Bi-LSTM: Runs two LSTMs (left→right and right→left), capturing ~7–8 words of context in each direction. CRF layer: Adds transition scores between adjacent tags, penalizing unlikely sequences (e.g., DET → V). Together they achieve ~97% accuracy. The CRF performs Viterbi-like decoding over the tag sequence, optimizing the global sequence rather than per-word classification.

\[ \text{Score}(X, Y) = \sum_{t=1}^{n} [\text{Emission}(y_t \mid x_t) + \text{Transition}(y_t \mid y_{t-1})], \quad P(Y \mid X) = \frac{\exp(\text{Score}(X, Y))}{\sum_{Y'} \exp(\text{Score}(X, Y'))} \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Thinking the CRF replaces the Bi-LSTM. They are complementary: Bi-LSTM handles word-level context, CRF handles tag-level sequence constraints. Also: CRF is a layer on top, not a separate classifier.

Self-check: Why does adding a CRF layer improve over using a standalone Bi-LSTM for POS tagging? Give a concrete example.

Connects to: MEMM, Viterbi Algorithm, BERT and Transformers

BERT and Transformers for POS Tagging

Must-know: Transformers use self-attention so every word attends to every other word, achieving unlimited bidirectional context. BERT (encoder-only) is fine-tuned on labeled POS data: add a classification layer on top, freeze most parameters, update only the last few layers. Encoder vs. Decoder: POS tagging uses the encoder (BERT), not the decoder (GPT). Advantages: near 100% accuracy, parallelizable, no manual feature engineering, unlimited context.

\[ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Using pre-trained BERT for POS tagging without fine-tuning. BERT knows language but does not know Penn Treebank tags. Also: confusing encoder (BERT, for understanding) with decoder (GPT, for generation).

Prompting and LLM Agents

Must-know: Prompting: Hard (explicit instructions), soft (learned prompt embeddings), few-shot (2–5 examples). Avoids fine-tuning but is less reliable and more expensive per token. LLM Agents: Use function calling to delegate POS tagging to specialized APIs — the agent orchestrates, tools execute. Hybrid architectures balance cost, speed, accuracy, and explainability. All methods from HMM to transformers remain relevant in production.

None — conceptual topic only.

⚠️ Top pitfall: Assuming prompting always replaces fine-tuning. Prompting is less reliable for consistent output formats, more expensive per token at scale, and may hallucinate tags. For production, fine-tuned specialized models are preferred.

Modern Challenges in POS Tagging

Must-know: Four key challenges: (1) Domain-specific vocabulary — medical, legal, finance terms break generic models. (2) Low-resource languages — most Indian languages lack annotated corpora and pre-trained models. (3) Cost — H100 GPUs cost ~₹20 lakhs; transformer training has significant carbon footprint. (4) Explainability — regulated industries (finance, healthcare, law) need traceable decisions; HMM/Viterbi/MEMM are explainable, transformers are black boxes.

None — conceptual topic only.

⚠️ Top pitfall: Assuming near-100% benchmark accuracy means POS tagging is "solved." Real-world accuracy drops dramatically on domain-specific text or low-resource languages. Always consider deployment context, not just benchmark numbers.

How to Read Matrices (Exam Essential)

Must-know: Transition \(A\): Rows = previous tag, Columns = current tag. \(P(\text{current} \mid \text{previous})\). Emission \(B\): Rows = tag, Columns = word. \(P(\text{word} \mid \text{tag})\). The emission is ALWAYS word-given-tag, never tag-given-word. The HMM is a generative model — the tag "emits" the word. Memory aid: "If I were a noun, what words would I emit?"

\[ A_{ij} = P(\text{tag}_j \mid \text{tag}_i), \quad B_{ik} = P(\text{word}_k \mid \text{tag}_i) \]

⚠️ Top pitfall: The #1 exam mistake: reading emission as \(P(\text{tag} \mid \text{word})\). The row label (left side) is always the condition — the "given" part. "Doctor given noun" → look at the noun ROW, doctor COLUMN.
