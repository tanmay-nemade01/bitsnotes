What is Natural Language Processing?

1.1 Definition

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a branch of Artificial Intelligence that focuses on enabling machines to understand, analyze, and generate human language — in the same way human beings communicate with each other.

Key Concept: NLP is not the same as LLMs (Large Language Models) or Agentic AI. LLMs are a part of NLP — they are one technology within the broader NLP landscape. NLP is an area of study, much like "computer networks" or "cloud computing" is an area — it encompasses multiple techniques, algorithms, and applications.

At its core, NLP asks: Can we teach machines to speak our language instead of us learning theirs? Instead of humans writing code in Java, Python, or C++, we want to communicate with machines in natural language — English, Hindi, Marathi, or any human language — and have the machine interpret and act on that communication.

1.2 The Ultimate Goal

The dream of NLP has always been to make human–machine interaction as natural as human–human interaction. As of today, this dream is largely becoming reality:

  • You can talk to Alexa, Siri, or Google Assistant and they respond like a human would.
  • Search engines are far more efficient than they were a decade ago — they understand intent, not just keywords.
  • Machines can read a newspaper and summarize it for you.
  • Machine translation allows you to communicate across language barriers in real time.
  • Code-generation agents (like GitHub Copilot) take natural language instructions and produce executable code.

1.3 What NLP Draws From

Building NLP applications requires borrowing from several disciplines:

  • Linguistics — structure and grammar of languages
  • Psycholinguistics — how humans process language cognitively
  • Cognitive Science — models of mental representation
  • Mathematics & Statistics — probability, linear algebra, optimization
  • Computer Science — algorithms, data structures, machine learning, deep learning

Artificial Intelligence \(\supset\) NLP \(\supset\) LLMs

Key Concept — Rationalist vs. Empiricist Approaches: The NLP field has been shaped by a fundamental philosophical divide. The rationalist approach (dominant ~1960–1985, championed by Noam Chomsky) holds that key language knowledge is innate — hardwired in the brain by genetic inheritance — and that linguistic theory should describe the ideal "competence" of a native speaker, not the messy reality of actual language use. The empiricist approach assumes the brain starts with general operations for association, pattern recognition, and generalization, and that language structure is learned from rich sensory input. Modern Statistical NLP is firmly empiricist: we specify a general language model and induce its parameters from large corpora using statistical and machine learning methods. The British linguist J.R. Firth captured this ethos with his slogan: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps" — a statement that directly foreshadows modern word embeddings. American structuralists like Zellig Harris similarly sought discovery procedures to automatically uncover language structure from text — a goal that Statistical NLP now pursues at scale.

Why Study NLP?

1.4 The Centrality of Language to Intelligence

What makes human beings intelligent? One of the most accepted answers is: our ability to communicate effectively. When we say one person is "more intelligent" than another, we often mean they have more world knowledge, they understand complex situations better, and they can articulate their understanding clearly. Language is the medium through which this intelligence is expressed.

Since Artificial Intelligence aims to replicate human intelligence, it follows naturally that language understanding must be at the heart of AI.

1.5 From Competence to Performance: Why Corpus-Based Methods Matter

A crucial distinction drives modern NLP: Chomsky drew a line between linguistic competence (the idealized knowledge of language structure in a native speaker's mind — the I-language) and linguistic performance (how language is actually used in the real world, affected by memory limitations, distractions, and context). Generative linguistics focused on describing competence — the abstract rules that license "grammatical" sentences. But as Sapir noted in 1921, "All grammars leak" — no rule system cleanly separates well-formed from ill-formed utterances because people constantly stretch and bend the rules to meet their communicative needs.

Statistical NLP instead asks: "What are the common patterns that occur in language use?" The major tool is counting — statistics and probability theory. Rather than starting from a binary grammatical/ungrammatical divide, we assign probabilities to linguistic events, letting us say which sentences are "usual" and "unusual." This approach accepts that language is inherently non-categorical and that the creative, conventional, and probabilistic aspects of language are not bugs to be abstracted away but the very phenomena worth modeling.

1.6 Text is the Largest Data Repository

As one student in the lecture correctly observed: text is the largest repository of human knowledge. Consider:

  • Emails, news articles, research papers, legal documents, social media posts — all are text.
  • Speech is almost always converted to text (via STT/ASR) before further processing.
  • Even video understanding often relies on transcripts and captions.
  • In every sector — finance, healthcare, e-commerce, education — there exists a vast corpus of textual data waiting to be leveraged.
Key Concept — Zipf's Law and the Long Tail: Word frequencies in any language follow a striking pattern known as Zipf's law: \(f \propto \frac{1}{r}\) — the frequency \(f\) of a word is inversely proportional to its rank \(r\). The 50th most common word occurs roughly 3 times as often as the 150th most common word. More practically: in a typical corpus, the top ~100 words account for over half of all word tokens, while nearly 50% of all word types appear only once (hapax legomena — Greek for "read only once"). Over 90% of word types occur 10 times or fewer. This long tail of rare words is the fundamental reason Statistical NLP is hard: for most words, our data about their use is exceedingly sparse. Only for a few words do we have lots of examples. Mandelbrot later refined Zipf's formula to \(f = P(r + \rho)^{-B}\) for better fit, but the core insight remains: language follows a hyperbolic distribution, not a normal (bell-curve) distribution. This pattern has deep practical consequences — it drives the need for smoothing techniques, subword tokenization, and large-scale corpus collection.

A Brief History of NLP

Understanding history is important because research progresses by identifying gaps in what has been done before. Every new breakthrough builds on the limitations of prior approaches.

1.9 The Eras of NLP

  1. Rule-Based Era (1950s–1970s)
    • ELIZA (1966): One of the earliest NLP systems, developed by Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT. It simulated a psychotherapist by pattern-matching user input against a set of rules. Interestingly, patients reported feeling comfortable interacting with it — an early demonstration that humans can form emotional connections with text-based systems.
    • Turing Test (1950): Alan Turing proposed that if a human cannot distinguish between conversing with a machine and a human, the machine can be said to "think."
    • Early Machine Translation: During the Cold War, rule-based translation between English and Russian was developed and proved useful despite limited compute.
    • Limitation: Required hand-crafted rules; no training data or compute power available.
  2. Symbolic / Knowledge-Based Era (1970s–1980s)
    • Context-Free Grammars (CFGs) and expert systems became popular.
    • Systems encoded linguistic knowledge explicitly — e.g., grammar rules, lexicons.
    • Limitation: Brittle; could not handle the variability and ambiguity of real language.
  3. Statistical NLP Era (1990s–2000s)
    • Data-driven approaches emerged as digital text corpora became available.
    • Probabilistic models: \(n\)-gram language models, Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), probabilistic context-free grammars (PCFGs).
    • Limitation: Required labeled training data and extensive feature engineering.
  4. Machine Learning Era (2000s–2010s)
    • Algorithms like SVM, Naïve Bayes, Decision Trees, and Maximum Entropy models.
    • Still required manual feature engineering — a labor-intensive and domain-specific process.
    • Limitation: Feature engineering was the bottleneck.
  5. Deep Learning Era (2013–2017)
    • Word2Vec (2013, Mikolov et al.): Breakthrough in learning dense vector representations of words automatically — no manual feature engineering.
    • Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), LSTMs, GRUs for sequence modeling.
    • Limitation: Sequential processing was slow; difficulty capturing long-range dependencies.
  6. Transformer Era (2017–Present)
    • "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017) introduced the Transformer architecture with the self-attention mechanism.
    • Enabled parallel processing of sequences and effective modeling of long-range dependencies.
    • Led to BERT (2018), GPT series, T5, and the modern LLM explosion.
  7. Agentic AI Era (Present)
    • LLMs are used as reasoning engines within autonomous agent frameworks.
    • Systems can plan, use tools, access APIs, retrieve information, and execute multi-step workflows.
    • Current Challenge: Pragmatic understanding; emotional intelligence; reliability; cost efficiency.
Note: A key insight: Not every application needs Agentic AI or LLMs. Using a massive transformer for a simple classification task is like "using a rocket to go to the market." The cost (in GPU hours, latency, and carbon footprint) must be justified by the ROI. Classical statistical/ML approaches remain relevant and are often the right choice for well-scoped problems.

NLP Applications

NLP applications are everywhere — wherever there is language, there is an NLP application. Below is a categorized overview.

1.10 Core NLP Tasks

Speech Recognition (STT)
Converting spoken language to text. Modern systems (e.g., OpenAI Whisper) handle noisy environments and background cancellation effectively.
Text-to-Speech (TTS)
Converting text to natural-sounding speech. Can now mimic specific voices (raising deep-fake concerns).
Machine Translation
Translating text from one language to another. Critical for multilingual societies like India, where local language content is rich and valuable.
Information Retrieval (IR)
Searching for relevant documents or passages from a large corpus. Underpins search engines and RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) systems.
Information Extraction (IE)
Extracting structured information from unstructured text: named entities (persons, organizations, locations), relations between entities, and events.
Text Summarization
Automatically generating concise summaries of longer documents while preserving key information.
Sentiment Analysis
Determining the emotional tone or opinion expressed in text (positive, negative, neutral).
Question Answering
Building systems that can answer natural language questions — from FAQ bots to complex reasoning systems.
Document Classification
Categorizing documents into predefined classes — spam detection, topic labeling, genre identification.
Spell & Grammar Checking
Tools like Grammarly that correct spelling and grammar in real time.

1.13 Open-Source NLP Libraries

NLTK

Natural Language Toolkit — the original, most established Python NLP library.
  • Extremely lightweight; needs minimal compute resources.
  • Supports multilingual processing — can do tokenization, stop-word removal, POS tagging for Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, Bengali, and many more.
  • Includes corpora (WordNet, stopwords, etc.) and classic algorithms.
  • Excellent for education and research due to its simplicity and transparency.
  • Now also supports deep learning integrations.
spaCy

Industrial-strength NLP — designed for production use.
  • Faster than NLTK for many tasks.
  • Provides character-level position information (character offsets) alongside tokens — more granular metadata.
  • POS tags include both the tag and a human-readable explanation (e.g., "NNP: proper singular noun").
  • Optimized primarily for English (though multi-language support is growing).
  • Better for dependency parsing and named entity recognition pipelines.
Hugging Face

The ecosystem that democratized transformer models.
  • transformers library: Thousands of pre-trained models (BERT, GPT, T5, LLaMA, etc.).
  • datasets: Standardized access to NLP benchmark datasets.
  • tokenizers: Fast tokenizer implementations (BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece).
  • Model Hub: Community-driven repository of fine-tuned models.
Stanford CoreNLP

Java-based toolkit from Stanford University. Provides a comprehensive pipeline: tokenization, POS tagging, NER, parsing, coreference resolution. Good for Java-based production environments.
TextBlob

A simplified wrapper around NLTK and Pattern. Extremely beginner-friendly — ideal for quick prototyping of sentiment analysis, POS tagging, and noun-phrase extraction.
Note: NLTK vs. spaCy — When to use which?
  • Use NLTK for multilingual tasks, education, research, and when you need maximum transparency and control.
  • Use spaCy for production English pipelines, dependency parsing, and when you need speed and character-level metadata.
  • The two are largely interchangeable — you can build a complete NLP pipeline with either.

Key Corpora and Lexical Resources

1.14 Foundational Corpora

Brown Corpus
The most widely known corpus. A balanced corpus of about 1 million words built at Brown University in the 1960s–70s, designed as a representative sample of American English across genres: press reportage, fiction, science, legal, and more. Balanced means proportional sampling was used — not just whatever text was convenient.
LOB Corpus
The Lancaster-Oslo-Bergen corpus — a British English replication of the Brown corpus, built with the same balanced sampling methodology.
Penn Treebank
A syntactically annotated (parsed) corpus drawn from Wall Street Journal text. Widely used but not free. The Susanne corpus (130K words, a freely available subset of Brown) is an alternative with syntactic annotation.
Canadian Hansards
A bilingual parallel corpus with English and French parliamentary proceedings — critical for statistical machine translation and cross-lingual work.

1.15 Lexical Resources

WordNet
A free electronic dictionary of English. Words are organized into a hierarchy of synsets — groups of words with identical or near-identical meanings. Also defines relations like meronymy (part-whole). A foundational resource for word sense disambiguation and semantic analysis.
Key Concept — Collocations: A collocation is any phrase or accepted usage where the whole is perceived to have an existence beyond the sum of its parts — compound terms ("disk drive"), phrasal verbs ("make up"), and stock phrases ("bacon and eggs"). They're important for lexicography, machine translation (a word may translate differently depending on its collocation partner), and information retrieval (indexing only "interesting" multiword units). Simply counting frequent bigrams doesn't work — function words dominate ("of the," "in the"). Effective collocation discovery requires filtering by part-of-speech patterns: the two most frequent patterns for meaningful collocations are "adjective–noun" and "noun–noun" (e.g., "New York," "vice president," "oil prices"). The general process mirrors the broader Statistical NLP methodology: start with a simple model, analyze results, identify problems, and iteratively refine.

Text Preprocessing

Before feeding text into any NLP model — classical or modern — raw text typically undergoes a series of preprocessing steps. These steps serve to reduce noise, normalize the text, and reduce the token count, which directly translates to cost savings in LLM-based systems.

Caution: Preprocessing is application-dependent. You should not blindly apply all steps. For example:
  • For grammar checking, you must not remove stop words.
  • For information retrieval or text summarization, removing punctuation and stop words often helps.
  • For sentiment analysis, keeping negation words ("not," "never") is critical.

1.16 Tokenization

Tokenization is the process of splitting a larger chunk of text into smaller units called tokens. Tokens can be:

  • Sentences — sentence tokenization
  • Words — word tokenization
  • Subwords — subword tokenization (BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece)
Example: Sentence Tokenization (NLTK): 

from nltk.tokenize import sent_tokenize
text = "Hello all, I'm Dr. Chetana. Welcome to the lab session!"
sentences = sent_tokenize(text)
# ['Hello all, I'm Dr. Chetana.',
#  'Welcome to the lab session!']

Word Tokenization (NLTK): 


from nltk.tokenize import word_tokenize
tokens = word_tokenize("Hello all, I'm Dr. Chetana.")
# ['Hello', 'all', ',', 'I', "'m", 'Dr.', 'Chetana', '.']
Notice that NLTK correctly distinguishes "Dr." (with the period as part of the abbreviation) from a sentence-ending period. This is non-trivial behavior.

1.16.1 Advanced Tokenization Techniques

The simple whitespace/punctuation-based tokenizer shown above is not what modern LLMs use. Advanced subword tokenization methods break words into meaningful subunits:

BPE
Byte-Pair Encoding (used by GPT models). Starts with characters, iteratively merges the most frequent pairs.
WordPiece
Used by BERT. Similar to BPE but merges based on likelihood rather than frequency.
SentencePiece
Language-agnostic; treats the input as a raw stream of characters (used by T5, LLaMA).
Key Concept: Why do tokenization techniques matter so much? Token cost is directly tied to the number of tokens. When you use commercial APIs (OpenAI, Anthropic), you pay per token. A better tokenizer can represent the same text with fewer tokens — so an improved tokenization scheme, like the one DeepSeek introduced, can make an LLM both faster and computationally cheaper.

1.17 Stop Word Removal

Stop words are common words that carry little semantic significance in many NLP tasks. Examples: "the," "is," "at," "which," "on," "a," "an."


from nltk.corpus import stopwords
stop_words = set(stopwords.words('english'))
text = "I am going to the market."
filtered = [w for w in word_tokenize(text)
            if w.lower() not in stop_words]
# ['I', 'going', 'market', '.']
Note: Domain-Specific Stop Words: You can (and should) customize the stop-word list for your domain.
  • In bioinformatics, gene names or common lab terms might be stop words.
  • In finance, domain-specific boilerplate terms can be filtered.
  • NLTK supports stop-word lists for Indian languages (Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, Bengali, etc.) as well as major world languages.

1.18 Lemmatization & Stemming

Both techniques aim to reduce a word to its base/root form, thereby collapsing morphological variants into a single representation. This reduces vocabulary size and helps models generalize better.

1.18.1 Stemming

Stemming uses a set of heuristic rules to strip suffixes. It does not consult a dictionary — it is purely algorithmic.

  • Porter Stemmer: The most widely used. Applies a sequence of rule-based transformations (e.g., "-ing" \(\to\) "", "-ational" \(\to\) "-ate").
  • Snowball Stemmer: An improved, multi-language version of Porter.
  • Lancaster Stemmer: More aggressive; produces shorter stems but may over-stem.
  • Regex Stemmer: User-defined regular expression rules.
Example: Stemming in action:
  • "running" \(\to\) "run"
  • "happily" \(\to\) "happili"   (note: not a real word — stems need not be valid words)
  • "friendship" \(\to\) "friend"
  • "natural" \(\to\) "natur"   (Porter stemmer strips "-al")

1.18.2 Lemmatization

Lemmatization uses a dictionary (lexicon) to find the canonical form (lemma) of a word. It considers the word's part of speech and meaning before stripping.

  • Requires a lexical resource like WordNet.
  • Produces valid dictionary words.
  • Can be guided by a domain dictionary — you specify which terms should be kept intact.
Example: Lemmatization vs. Stemming:
Word Stemming (Porter) Lemmatization
running run running
better better good
studies studi study
processing process processing
natural natur natural

Notice that lemmatization may retain suffixes if the domain dictionary says so (e.g., "processing" kept as-is in an NLP course context).

Table: Stemming vs. Lemmatization
Aspect Stemming Lemmatization
Method Rule-based (algorithmic) Dictionary-based (lexicon lookup)
Speed Fast Slower (needs dictionary)
Output May not be a real word Always a valid word
Domain knowledge None needed Can incorporate domain dictionary
Accuracy Can over/under-stem More accurate, meaning-preserving
Key Concept: The same idea behind stemming and lemmatization — breaking words into sub-word units — underlies modern subword tokenization (BPE, WordPiece). Just as "interest" \(+\) "ing" can be stored as two embeddings to cover "interesting," "interested," and "interests," BPE tokenizers split words into frequent subword units that can be recombined flexibly.

1.19 POS Tagging (Part-of-Speech Tagging)

POS tagging assigns each word a grammatical category: noun, verb, adjective, adverb, etc.


import nltk
tokens = nltk.word_tokenize("Dr. Chetana teaches NLP at BITS.")
tags = nltk.pos_tag(tokens)
# [('Dr.', 'NNP'), ('Chetana', 'NNP'), ('teaches', 'VBZ'),
#  ('NLP', 'NNP'), ('at', 'IN'), ('BITS', 'NNP'), ('.', '.')]
Note: POS tagging is particularly useful for Named Entity Recognition (NER). Named entities (persons, organizations, locations) are typically proper nouns (NNP) or common nouns (NN) — they will never be adjectives or adverbs. POS tagging thus serves as a filtering step for NER pipelines and relationship extraction.

The Challenge of Language

1.20 Why is Language Hard for Machines?

Despite being trivial for humans, language understanding is extraordinarily difficult for machines. Consider this simple four-line dialogue from a movie ticket booking scenario:

User: I want to watch Black Panther. Where is it playing in Mountain View?
System: Black Panther is playing at Century 16, Mountain View.
User: When is it playing there?
System: Showtimes are 2:00 PM, 5:30 PM, and 9:00 PM.
User: Book me for the first show — one adult and two children.
System: That will be $42.50. Confirming: 2:00 PM, 1 adult, 2 children at Century 16.

1.20.1 The Parsing Explosion Problem

A core difficulty: as sentences get longer, the number of possible syntactic parses explodes combinatorially. Consider this seemingly innocent sentence:

Our company is training workers.

A standard NLP parser with a reasonable grammar will find three syntactic analyses: (a) is training as the verb group (the intended reading), (b) is as the main verb with training workers as a gerund (cf. "Our problem is training workers"), and (c) is as the main verb with training modifying workers (cf. "Those are training wheels"). The last two are semantically anomalous, but a purely syntactic parser cannot detect that. Worse, real-world sentences like "List the sales of the products produced in 1973 with the products produced in 1972" have been shown to generate 455 parses in working systems. Maximizing grammar coverage while minimizing resultant ambiguity is fundamentally inconsistent in symbolic NLP systems — every new rule added to cover an obscure construction creates new undesired parses for common sentences.

This is precisely why Statistical NLP's ability to learn lexical and structural preferences from corpora is so powerful. Instead of binary grammatical/ungrammatical rules, statistical models capture collocational knowledge — which words tend to group with which others — providing a window onto deeper semantic relationships that resolve ambiguity gracefully.

1.20.2 Why Grammaticality Judgments Break Down

Even human linguists cannot reliably draw a binary grammatical/ungrammatical line. Consider these sentences from a linguistics textbook:

a. John I believe Sally said Bill believed Sue saw.
b. What did Sally whisper that she had secretly read?
c. John wants very much for himself to win.
d. The books you should read before it becomes difficult to talk about.
e. The books you should read before talking about becomes difficult.
f. Who did Jo think said John saw him?
g. That a serious discussion could arise here of this topic was quite unexpected.
h. The boys read Mary's stories about each other.

Most people disagree with the linguists' judgments on more than one of these. This shows that sentences don't divide neatly into "grammatical" and "ungrammatical." Conventionality matters — native speakers often produce sentences that are syntactically fine but "subtly odd," and non-native speakers can break conventions without breaking rules. The textbook gives an example: "She insisted that women were regarded as a different existence from men unfairly" — perfectly grammatical, but no native speaker would phrase it that way. Frequency and convention, not binary rules, determine what sounds natural.

1.20.3 Non-Categorical Phenomena: Words That Blend Categories

Language is filled with evidence that categorical assumptions fail. Consider the word near: it can be an adjective ("the near future," "We nearly lost") or a preposition ("He lives near the station"). But it can also simultaneously show properties of both — an impossible state in a rigid categorical system:

"He has never been nearer the center of the financial establishment."

"Nearer" (comparative form) is a hallmark of adjectives — other categories don't form comparatives. But it's taking a direct object like a preposition. It's behaving as a category blend. Similarly, the phrases kind of and sort of underwent a gradual shift over centuries from noun+preposition sequences ("a kind of fish") to degree modifiers ("I kind of love you") driven by increasing frequency in ambiguous contexts. These non-categorical phenomena can only be studied through statistical, frequency-based analysis — they're invisible to categorical rule systems.

1.21 Types of Ambiguity in Language

Ambiguity is the central challenge of NLP. Human language is filled with it at every level.

1.21.1 Structural Ambiguity

The same sequence of words can correspond to multiple syntactic structures, leading to different meanings.

Example: Example 1 — Word Order Matters: \begin{align*} \text{Sentence A: } &\text{` amrata thinks she understands me.''} \\ \text{Sentence B: } &\text{``She thinks Namrata understands me.''} \end{align*} In a Bag-of-Words model, both sentences are identical (same set of words). But their meanings are completely different — in A, Namrata is the subject; in B, Namrata is the object. The order of words carries meaning.
Example: Example 2 — Two Parse Trees:
"Visiting relatives can be a nuisance."

This sentence has two interpretations:

  1. Visiting (verb) + relatives (object): The act of visiting one's relatives is a nuisance.
  2. Visiting relatives (compound noun): Relatives who are visiting you are a nuisance.

If "visiting relatives" is parsed as a gerund phrase (verb + object), you get meaning 1. If parsed as a compound noun (adjective + noun), you get meaning 2. Humans disambiguate effortlessly via context; machines need explicit mechanisms.

1.21.2 Grammatical (Morpho-Syntactic) Ambiguity

English, in particular, has grammatical ambiguities that many other languages do not.

Example: Gender Ambiguity:
"I go now."

In English, you cannot tell the gender of the speaker. But in Hindi or Marathi:

  • "Main jaata hoon" \(\to\) male speaker
  • "Main jaati hoon" \(\to\) female speaker

Machine translation from English to a gender-marked language requires resolving this ambiguity — a non-trivial problem.

1.21.3 Lexical Ambiguity (Word Sense Disambiguation)

The same word can have multiple meanings depending on context. The same word can even have multiple parts of speech.

Example: The word "can":
  • Noun: "a can of milk" (container)
  • Modal verb: "Can I go?" (permission/ability)
  • Verb: "to can food" (to preserve in a can)

The word "bank":

  • Financial institution: "I deposited money in the bank."
  • River bank: "We sat on the bank of the Ganga."

Key Concept: This is precisely why contextual word embeddings (Word2Vec \(\to\) ELMo \(\to\) BERT \(\to\) GPT) were a breakthrough. Static embeddings (like early Word2Vec) assigned a single vector to "bank," conflating all its meanings. Contextual embeddings assign different vectors to "bank" depending on surrounding words — capturing the correct sense.

1.21.4 Cultural & Temporal Ambiguity

Language evolves. Words change meaning over time and across cultures.

Example: The word "cool":
  • 1980s/90s: "That's cool" \(\to\) That's interesting/nice (positive).
  • Earlier usage: "He's a cool person" \(\to\) He's emotionally distant (negative).
  • Gen Z usage: Sarcastic "cool" can mean the exact opposite depending on tone and context.

1.21.5 Other Challenges

  • New words: "Tandoori," "yoga," "selfie" — constantly being added to dictionaries.
  • Emoticons and emojis: ":)" vs. sarcastic ":))" carry different meanings.
  • Gen Z jargon: "no cap," "slay," "bet" — context-dependent and rapidly evolving.
  • Exceptions to rules: "i before e except after c" — and then there's "science," "weird," "their" \(\ldots\)
  • Tone of voice: The same words spoken in an angry tone vs. a calm tone convey entirely different meanings.
  • Facial expressions and gestures: In face-to-face communication, much of the meaning is non-verbal.
Note: Machines today can understand language at the word and sentence level, but they are not yet emotionally intelligent. They cannot reliably detect sarcasm, read between the lines, or interpret the complex interplay of tone, gesture, and context that humans use naturally.

Levels of Language Understanding

When humans process language, multiple layers of analysis happen simultaneously and automatically — we are not consciously aware of them. NLP systems attempt to replicate these layers explicitly. The layers are hierarchical: higher layers assume lower layers are already processed.

1.22 Morphological Level

Morphology is the study of how words are formed from smaller units called morphemes — the smallest meaning-bearing units of language.

  • Root/Stem: The core meaning-carrying part. E.g., "teach" in "teacher," "teaching," "teaches."
  • Prefix: Added before the root. E.g., "un-" in "undo," "pre-" in "preview."
  • Suffix: Added after the root. E.g., "-er" in "teacher," "-ing" in "teaching."
  • Infix: Inserted inside the root (rare in English, common in some languages).
Example: Morphological decomposition: \begin{align*} \text{``unhappiness''} &= \underbrace{\text{un}}_{\text{prefix}} + \underbrace{\text{happy}}_{\text{root}} + \underbrace{\text{ness}}_{\text{suffix}} \\ \text{``biology''} &= \underbrace{\text{bio}}_{\text{root (life)}} + \underbrace{\text{logy}}_{\text{suffix (study of)}} \\ \text{``bioinformatics''} &= \text{bio} + \text{informatics} \end{align*} If you know "bio" = life and "logy" = study of, you can infer that "biology" = study of life — even if you have never seen the word before. This is exactly what subword tokenization (BPE) tries to leverage.

1.23 Lexical Level

At the lexical level, we know which affixes can attach to which roots. This is learned over years of language exposure.

Example: Lexical knowledge in action:
  • "friend" + "ship" = "friendship"   correct
  • "friend" + "ful" = "friendful"   incorrect (it's "friendly")
  • "beauty" + "ful" = "beautiful"   correct
  • "beauty" + "ship" = "beautyship"   incorrect

There is no general rule — you just know which combinations are valid. Your neural network (brain) has learned these associations through exposure.

1.24 Syntactic Level

Syntax is the set of rules that govern sentence structure — the grammar of a language.

  • English: SVO (Subject–Verb–Object) — "I eat mango."
  • Hindi/Marathi: SOV (Subject–Object–Verb) — "Main aam khata hoon" (I mango eat).
  • Japanese/Korean: SOV, with topic markers.

If you scramble the word order, the sentence becomes incomprehensible:

"Mango I eat" \(\to\) understandable but awkward.
"Eat I mango" \(\to\) confusing.
"Mango eat I" \(\to\) nearly incomprehensible.

Syntax enables us to distinguish "Dog bites man" from "Man bites dog" — the same words, different meanings, determined purely by structure.

1.25 Semantic Level

Semantics deals with meaning. A sentence can be syntactically perfect but semantically nonsensical.

Example: Noam Chomsky's classic example:
"Colorless green ideas sleep furiously."

This sentence is grammatically flawless (Adj + Adj + N + V + Adv — a perfectly valid English structure). But it is semantically meaningless: ideas cannot be green or colorless; ideas do not sleep; nothing can sleep furiously.

Yet a human instantly recognizes it as nonsense. A machine, without semantic understanding, might process it as just another valid sentence.

Semantic analysis is where the heavy lifting of modern NLP happens:

  • Word embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe): Map words to dense vectors where similar meanings are close in vector space.
  • Contextual embeddings (BERT, GPT): The same word gets different vectors depending on surrounding context — resolving the "bank" ambiguity discussed earlier.
  • Attention mechanisms: Allow the model to focus on relevant parts of the input when computing meaning.

1.26 Pragmatic Level

Pragmatics goes beyond literal meaning to consider world knowledge, intent, and situational context. A statement can be semantically true but pragmatically wrong.

Example: Semantic vs. Pragmatic:
  • Semantically correct: "I cut the banana with a pen."
  • Pragmatically incorrect: You don't cut bananas with pens — you use a knife. This violates world knowledge.

Key Concept: In the Agentic AI paradigm, pragmatic understanding maps to:
  • Episodic memory: Remembering past interactions and outcomes.
  • Semantic memory: General world knowledge (what a knife is, what it's used for).
  • Goal-awareness: Understanding what the user is trying to achieve, not just what they literally said.

We are still struggling at the pragmatic level in current AI systems — this is an active area of research.

1.27 Discourse Level

Discourse concerns how sentences connect to form coherent text. It involves:

  • Coreference Resolution: Identifying which words refer to the same entity. E.g., "Jane told Mary that she passed the exam." — Who does "she" refer to?
  • Coherence: Ensuring the text flows logically from one sentence to the next.
  • Discourse relations: Cause–effect, contrast, elaboration, etc.
Example: Cross-sentence discourse:
"Once upon a time, there lived a king and a queen in a beautiful kingdom. They ruled wisely and were loved by all. They lived happily together."

"They" in the second and third sentences refers back to "the king and queen" — even though the explicit mention was two sentences ago. This is discourse-level coreference. Modern LLMs handle this via their context window (e.g., 128K tokens in GPT-4 Turbo), which allows them to "remember" earlier parts of the text.

NLP System Architecture

Every NLP system — whether a question-answering bot, a machine translation engine, or a text summarizer — has two fundamental components:

  1. Understanding (Analysis): Parse and interpret the input.
  2. Generation (Synthesis): Produce a coherent, appropriate output.

Both components require: grammar/spelling knowledge, domain knowledge, and application-specific knowledge.

1.28 Classical NLP Pipeline

In the classical (pre-deep-learning) approach, each level of language understanding was implemented as a distinct module:

\[ \text{Input} \to \underbrace{\text{Morphological Analysis}}_{\text{tokenization, stemming}} \to \underbrace{\text{Syntactic Analysis}}_{\text{POS tagging, parsing}} \to \underbrace{\text{Semantic Analysis}}_{\text{meaning extraction}} \to \underbrace{\text{Pragmatic Analysis}}_{\text{intent, context}} \to \text{Output} \]

Advantages: Fully explainable, debuggable, no hallucinations, lightweight.
Disadvantages: Requires extensive manual engineering; each module's errors propagate downstream.

1.29 Modern (End-to-End) NLP Pipeline

Modern systems use a transformer-based architecture where syntax and semantics are learned implicitly:

\[ \text{Input} \to \underbrace{\text{Tokenizer}}_{\text{BPE/WordPiece}} \to \underbrace{\text{Transformer Encoder/Decoder}}_{\text{implicit syntax + semantics}} \to \underbrace{\text{Generation Head}}_{\text{produce output tokens}} \to \text{Output} \]

Advantages: No manual feature engineering; state-of-the-art results on most benchmarks.
Disadvantages: Black-box (not explainable); requires expensive GPUs; can hallucinate; billions of parameters.

1.30 Hybrid NLP Pipeline (Production Standard)

In practice, most production-ready systems use a hybrid approach:

\[ \text{Input} \to \underbrace{\text{Lightweight Classical Preprocessing}}_{\text{tokenization, stop words, normalization}} \to \underbrace{\text{LLM / Transformer}}_{\text{understanding + generation}} \to \underbrace{\text{Classical Post-processing}}_{\text{validation, formatting}} \to \text{Output} \]
Key Concept: Why hybrid? Transformers need GPUs. GPUs cost lakhs of rupees (an NVIDIA A100 can cost $10K–$15K; an H100 even more). For a startup or a cost-sensitive application, using a GPU for basic tasks like tokenization or stop-word removal is wasteful. Do the lightweight work with classical methods (NLTK/spaCy), and reserve the transformer for what it does best: deep semantic understanding and fluent generation.

Evaluating NLP Systems

Evaluation is critical. A client will not accept an NLP system that hallucinates, produces biased outputs, or fails on edge cases. Good evaluation tells you when your system works, when it fails, and why.

1.31 Key Evaluation Criteria

Accuracy
The most basic metric: fraction of correct predictions. Limitation: misleading for imbalanced datasets (e.g., 99% of emails are not spam — a model that always predicts "not spam" is 99% accurate but useless).
Fairness
The system must not exhibit biases based on gender, race, caste, religion, etc. Since models learn from human-generated data, they can (and do) amplify societal biases. Garbage In, Garbage Out.
Reliability
The system must produce consistent results. This is extremely difficult with large neural models (145B+ parameters) — identifying the root cause of a specific failure is challenging. This lack of explainability drives the push for AI governance.
Robustness
The system must generalize to diverse, unseen data. Overfitting to the training distribution is a failure mode.
Efficiency
ROI (Return on Investment): If a system costs Rs. 1 crore to deploy (GPUs, infrastructure, development), you expect at least Rs. 2 crores in returns. Latency: Users will abandon a system that takes minutes to respond — the "time to first token" must be low.
Usability
The output must be fluent, coherent, and actionable by the end user.

1.32 Common Evaluation Metrics

Table: NLP Evaluation Metrics by Task
Metric Primary Use Description
Accuracy Classification \(\frac{\text{Correct predictions}}{\text{Total predictions}}\); good for balanced datasets.
Precision IR, IE, Detection \(\frac{TP}{TP + FP}\); of all predicted positives, how many are actually positive?
Recall Medical, Fraud \(\frac{TP}{TP + FN}\); of all actual positives, how many did we catch?
F1 Score General Harmonic mean of Precision and Recall.
BLEU Machine Translation Measures \(n\)-gram overlap between generated and reference translation.
ROUGE Summarization Recall-oriented; measures \(n\)-gram overlap with reference summary.
Perplexity Language Modeling Measures how "surprised" the model is by the test data. Lower = better.

1.32.1 Why Precision and Recall Matter: A Medical Example

Example: Cancer Detection — Both Errors Are Dangerous:
  • False Positive (FP): The model predicts cancer when the patient is healthy.
    • Consequence: Unnecessary mental trauma, invasive follow-up tests, costs.
    • You want high precision: minimize false positives.
  • False Negative (FN): The model predicts healthy when the patient has cancer.
    • Consequence: Missed early treatment window; can cost a life.
    • You want high recall: minimize false negatives.

There is always a precision–recall trade-off. The right balance depends on the application and its tolerance for each type of error.

1.33 The Explainability-Performance Trade-off

Classical Models (Naïve Bayes, Decision Trees, SVM)

\(\to\) High explainability, moderate performance, zero hallucination, low compute.


Hybrid Systems (Classical preprocessing + LLM)

\(\to\) Balanced: explainable pipeline with powerful generation.


Pure LLM / Transformer (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini)

\(\to\) High performance, low explainability, risk of hallucination, high compute cost.
Note: The Black Box Problem: When a transformer with 175 billion parameters produces a wrong output, you cannot easily trace why. You cannot "debug" it in the traditional sense. This is a fundamental challenge for enterprise adoption, especially in regulated industries (finance, healthcare, law).

Key Takeaways

1.37 Summary of Lecture 1

  1. NLP is a branch of AI that enables machines to understand, analyze, and generate human language. It is a broad field, not a single technology.
  2. The history of NLP shows a progression: Rule-based \(\to\) Symbolic \(\to\) Statistical \(\to\) ML \(\to\) Deep Learning \(\to\) Transformers \(\to\) Agentic AI. Each era built on the limitations of the previous one.
  3. Language is fundamentally ambiguous at every level — morphological, lexical, syntactic, semantic, pragmatic, and discourse. Resolving these ambiguities is the central challenge of NLP.
  4. Text preprocessing (tokenization, stop-word removal, lemmatization/stemming, POS tagging) is critical for reducing noise, saving compute, and improving downstream performance — even in the age of LLMs.
  5. NLTK and spaCy are the two dominant open-source NLP libraries. NLTK excels at multilingual tasks and education; spaCy excels at production English pipelines.
  6. Hybrid architectures (classical preprocessing + transformer-based core) are the production standard because they balance cost, explainability, and performance.
  7. Evaluating NLP systems requires more than accuracy. Fairness, reliability, robustness, efficiency, and usability are equally important. The choice of metric (accuracy vs. F1 vs. BLEU vs. ROUGE vs. perplexity) depends on the task.
  8. "Wisdom is not in words; wisdom is meaning within the words." — Khalil Gibran. This quote encapsulates why contextual embeddings were a breakthrough: they capture meaning, not just surface form.
