Software Quality Management

11.1 Software Quality Management Recap: Standards for Product and Process

This section recaps the previous session's foundations of software quality management: what quality management is, why standards are its concrete carriers, and why a quality culture matters more than a borrowed manufacturing process.

11.1.1 Quality management runs in parallel with development

Hook. Can you check the quality of a software product the way you check a car on a production line? The answer is no — and trying to do so is one of the most common traps in software quality management.

Software quality management does not happen after development is finished; it runs in parallel with the software development process, with periodical reviews after every deliverable in the lifecycle. Every work product — requirements, design, code, test cases, user manuals — gets checked as soon as it exists, not all at the end.

A common trap is to borrow the process-based quality approach used in manufacturing and apply it directly to software — it does not carry over. In manufacturing, quality comes from controlling a repeatable production process: once the machines are calibrated and running correctly, product quality naturally follows, so you can measure the product, and if the quality is not good enough you adjust the process and standardize the improved version. The process can be run again and again to produce identical high-quality products.

Software production is not repeatable in the same way. Software is designed, not manufactured. Each project involves novel design decisions, and individual skills and experience strongly influence the result — a creative process cannot be standardized the way machine operations can. External factors such as the novelty of the application or commercial pressure for an early release also affect product quality no matter what process is used. A good process makes good software more likely, but it does not guarantee it the way a calibrated production line guarantees identical cars.

Instead, an organization needs a quality culture in which people, as a team, take responsibility for quality. Quality then belongs to everyone on the project, not to a separate inspection department at the end. This is the core message of the lecture: quality is a team responsibility built into every step of development, not a gatekeeper activity performed by somebody else after the work is done.

Recap. Quality management runs alongside development, reviews happen after every deliverable, and the manufacturing-style idea that "control the process, get the product" must be replaced with a team quality culture, because software is designed rather than manufactured.

11.1.2 Product standards and process standards

Standards are the concrete carriers of quality culture. A standard encapsulates the best practices and wisdom gained from past experience, and provides a framework for defining quality — both process quality and product quality — within the organization or within a project. Standards also give continuity: a new team can understand an organization and its work by understanding the standards the organization uses. When one engineer leaves and another takes over, the shared standard means the newcomer already knows how documents are structured, how code is formatted, and how changes are approved — the learning effort on new work is reduced.

Standards matter for three reasons:

  1. They capture organizational wisdom. Standards are based on knowledge about the best or most appropriate practice for the company, often acquired only after much trial and error. Building that knowledge into a standard lets the company reuse the experience and avoid past mistakes.
  2. They provide a framework for defining quality. Software quality is subjective; standards establish a basis for deciding whether a required level of quality has been achieved — provided the standards themselves reflect user expectations for dependability, usability, and performance.
  3. They support continuity. Uniform practices across the organization reduce the learning effort when work is handed from one person to another.

Two families of standards matter.

Family What it describes Examples from the lecture More examples
Product standards The work products themselves — what the output must look like Design review form, requirements document structure, programming style and method header format, target plan format, change request format Coding standards for a language, documentation standards (comment headers), document structure standards
Process standards How work is carried out — the steps and rules to follow How the design review is conducted, how new code is submitted for system building, how a change gets approved, how a project plan is planned and approved Version release process, change control process, test recording process, project plan approval process

The distinction matters in practice: a product standard tells you what to produce (for example, every method must have a header comment describing its purpose), while a process standard tells you how to produce and verify it (for example, every method header is checked during the design review). Process standards should also include the definition of processes that check whether product standards have been followed — that closes the loop between the two families.

Standards also have to be cost-effective: there is no point in defining standards that are expensive in terms of time and effort to apply and only lead to marginal improvements in quality. Product standards must be designed so that they can be applied and checked cost-effectively.

11.1.3 Why engineers sometimes resist standards

Standards are not always welcomed. Engineers may feel uncomfortable using them, may find them outdated and irrelevant to the project at hand, and may see them as a pile of bureaucratic tasks that delay real work. This resistance usually appears when project standards require tedious documentation and work recording — engineers often agree about the general need for standards while finding good reasons why particular standards do not fit their project.

Pitfalls.

  • Treating standards as fixed forever: standards tend to become enshrined in a company standards handbook and are expensive to change, so they silently become outdated as technology moves on. Standards must be reviewed and modified regularly to reflect changing technologies.
  • Imposing standards without explanation: developers are far more committed to standards they understand. Involve software engineers in the selection of product standards and include commentary in the standards document explaining why each standardization decision was made.
  • Expecting manual conformance checking: developers find standards a bugbear when following them means tedious manual work. Tool support makes conformance cheap — for example, a syntax-directed program editor can enforce program layout standards automatically.

The countermeasure the professor emphasizes is tooling: standards should be supported by an efficient set of tools, so that using the standards actually helps quality management instead of slowing everyone down. With the right tools, following the standard becomes easier than not following it — the standard supports the work instead of adding to its burden.

Recap and bridge. Standards carry the quality culture by capturing best practice, defining quality, and giving continuity, split into product standards (what to produce) and process standards (how to produce and check it). Resistance to standards is normal and is answered with tools that make conformance cheap. That brings us to the most widely used framework for defining both kinds of standards: the ISO 9000 family, the topic of the next section.

11.2 ISO 9001: A Framework for Quality Standards

11.2.1 What ISO 9001 is — and what it is not

Hook. A factory, a bank, and a software company can all be certified against the same quality standard. How can one standard fit all of them? Because ISO 9001 is not a quality standard for products — it is a framework for building quality standards.

The most widely used framework for defining both product and process standards is the ISO 9000 framework. ISO stands for the International Standards Organization (in French, the International Organization of Standards), and it publishes an international set of standards used in the development of quality management systems in all industries. The ISO 9001 standard can be applied to a range of organizations, from manufacturing through service industries to software development companies. The ISO 9001 series is one of the most general sets of standards; it applies to organizations that design, develop, and maintain various kinds of products, including software.

The first idea to fix firmly: ISO 9001 is not a standard for software development. It is a framework for developing software standards. It sets out the general quality principles, describes quality processes in general, and lays out the organizational standards and procedures that an organization should define.

If an organization wants to be ISO 9001 conformant, it must document — all in an organizational quality manual:

  • its quality principles,
  • its quality processes,
  • its organizational standards,
  • the procedures that should be carried out, and
  • the procedures used to check that these processes have been carried out.

It must also document how its own processes relate to the core processes of ISO 9001, and it must define and maintain records that show the defined organizational processes have actually been followed. The company quality manual should also describe the relevant process and the process data that has to be collected and maintained.

In other words, the standard says "you must define how you do quality, and you must be able to prove you follow your own definition." What the definition actually is, the standard leaves to the organization.

Real-world: ISO 9001 is used across manufacturing, services, and software alike, which is why the framework is deliberately general rather than software-specific. A software supplier's quality manual looks very different from a chocolate factory's, yet both can be certified against the same ISO 9001 core processes.

11.2.2 The nine core processes

ISO 9001 defines a set of core processes in two groups. The product delivery processes are the ones related directly to the product, and the processes related to business activities support the organization behind the product:

Group Core processes
Product delivery processes (directly about the product) Business acquisition, design and development, production and delivery, testing, service and support
Processes related to business activities (supporting processes) Business management, supply management, configuration management, inventory management

A note on the supporting processes: the standard ISO 9001 core-process list reads business management, supplier management, configuration management, and inventory management. The lecture's "configuration management" is the correct standard term; the lecture's "supply management" corresponds to the standard's "supplier management". Configuration management means tracking the versions of all work products — code, documents, configuration files — so that the state of the product is always known and changeable in a controlled way.

All nine core processes must be documented in the quality manual. The company must also define and maintain records that show the defined organizational processes have actually been followed, and its quality manual should describe the relevant process and the process data that has to be collected and maintained.

Notice what ISO 9001 does not do: it does not define or prescribe the specific quality processes a company should use. To be conformant, the company defines the types of processes shown in the framework and puts procedures in place that show these quality processes are being followed. That is where the flexibility comes from — the same framework works across industrial sectors, company sizes, and organization units including teams, and quality standards can be defined that are appropriate for the type of software being developed. Practice varies widely between ISO 9001 compliant companies: some run very rigid quality processes and keep detailed records, while others are much less formal with minimal extra documentation. Because of this flexibility, you cannot assume two ISO 9001-compliant companies run the same processes — you have to look at each company's own quality manual.

11.2.3 From the quality manual to quality plans

Intuition. Think of ISO 9001 as the recipe book that tells you a meal must have certain courses, the quality manual as your restaurant's actual menu and kitchen rules, and the quality plan as the shopping and cooking list for one specific dinner party.

The chain that makes the framework operational runs like this:

  1. The ISO 9001 standards describe the core processes.
  2. The organization instantiates those processes in its organizational quality manual.
  3. The quality manual is used to create quality plans for various projects.
  4. Those quality plans support project quality management — which is essentially following the organizational quality processes documented in the quality manual.

In other words, the ISO 9001 quality models are instantiated as an organizational quality manual, which in turn drives the quality plans that each project follows. A project's quality plan sets out the quality goals for that project and defines what processes and standards are to be used for it — how the generic organizational framework is made concrete for one particular system. Quality plans should be kept as short as possible: if the document is too long, people will not read it, and the plan defeats its purpose.

11.2.4 Certification and audits

Some software customers demand that their suppliers be ISO 9001 certified; that gives the customers confidence that the software development company has an approved quality management system in place. Certification is granted through audits:

  • Independent accreditation (audit) authorities — not the company itself — examine the quality management process detail and the process documentation detail.
  • They decide whether these processes cover all the core processes specified in ISO 9001.
  • If everything is defined and there are procedures in place to check that the processes are followed, they certify the company's quality process as defined in its quality manual.

So certification is a third-party verdict that the company's quality manual exists, covers the ISO 9001 core processes, and is actually followed. The audit authority does not judge whether the company's processes are the best possible ones — only whether they are defined and followed.

Real-world: ISO 9001 certification is a routine contractual requirement in customer-supplier relationships, and the audit is carried out by independent accreditation authorities, not by the company itself. In many sectors, an uncertified supplier is simply not considered for contracts.

11.2.5 Certification does not guarantee product quality

Some people mistakenly think that an ISO 9001 certified organization will always produce better products than an uncertified one. That is not the case.

Q: Do you agree that products produced by an ISO 9001 certified company will be better than those from an uncertified company?

A: Not necessarily. Certification means only that the company defined its quality procedures and follows them — it says nothing about whether those procedures are the best software development practices, and it does not guarantee high product quality.

The standard focuses only on ensuring that the organization has defined its quality management procedures in its quality manual and follows those procedures. There is no guarantee that the certified processes use the best software development practices, and no guarantee that they lead to high quality software. Certification defines quality only at the level of conformance to standards; it does not take account of quality as experienced by the users of the software when the software is in execution or operation.

Worked example — a certification that proves nothing about quality. Suppose a company defines this test coverage standard: all methods in the object should be called at least once. Consider a banking class Account with four methods:

  • deposit(amount) — adds money,
  • withdraw(amount) — removes money,
  • getBalance() — returns the balance,
  • applyFee(percent) — charges a percentage fee.

Step 1 — a tester writes the following test calls, one per method:

  1. deposit(1000) → balance becomes 1000.
  2. getBalance() → returns 1000.
  3. withdraw(500) → balance becomes 500.
  4. applyFee(10) → balance becomes 450.

Step 2 — check the standard: every method has been called at least once: deposit ✓, getBalance ✓, withdraw ✓, applyFee ✓. The defined standard is fully satisfied: the test coverage standard is met.

Step 3 — sense-check the test's real coverage. None of the tests uses different method parameters: withdraw(500) never tests an overdraft or a withdrawal larger than the balance; applyFee(10) never tests a fee larger than the balance, a zero balance, or a negative percentage; deposit is never tested with zero or negative amounts. Every error-handling branch of the class is untested.

Result: the defined testing procedures are followed, the test records are maintained, and the coverage standard is satisfied — so the company could be ISO 9001 certified — even though the product quality is low. Certification and high product quality are simply different things.

Exam note: the contrast between conformance-based quality (what ISO 9001 certification proves) and user-experienced quality (what actually matters in operation) is a central idea of this topic. A question asking why certification does not guarantee product quality should be answered with exactly this contrast — the standard checks that procedures exist and are followed, not that the procedures produce good software.

11.2.6 Student questions on ISO 9001 in practice

Q: Does your organization, or any of your clients' organizations, use ISO 9001 or ISO standards for process compliance? Have you heard of ISO 9001?

A: Yes — ISO standards are used for process compliance in the organization. This is typical: in industry, ISO 9001 is most often seen exactly as this question describes — a process compliance framework that organizations adopt and are audited against.

Recap and bridge. ISO 9001 is a deliberately general framework: nine core processes in two groups, instantiated as an organizational quality manual, which drives per-project quality plans; independent authorities audit and certify conformance; and certification proves conformance to standards, never user-experienced quality. Where ISO 9001 certifies that you follow your own defined processes, the next topic — CMMI — pushes organizations to improve those processes continuously.

11.3 CMMI and Continuous Process Improvement

11.3.1 Process improvement as a quality lever

Hook. ISO 9001 asks "do you follow the processes you defined?" — but what if the processes themselves are weak? That is the question CMMI exists to answer.

A second organization-wide standards framework is the capability maturity model — the integrated capability maturity model, or CMMI — which is used for process improvement. It was developed by the Software Engineering Institute (SEI) as a framework that describes the characteristics of an effective software process and gives organizations a way to assess how mature their process is.

The intuition behind it: if you improve the process, the product quality will improve. This holds especially in manufacturing industries, and in software it is most often true as well — a very good process usually leads to a very good product. The relationship is not as tight as in manufacturing (software is designed rather than manufactured, so individual skill and creativity matter), but the direction is the same: better process, fewer defects, better product. Process quality management and improvement can result in fewer defects in the software being developed.

What CMMI provides. CMMI is a process meta-model — a model for modeling and assessing processes. It defines process areas (for example, requirements management, project planning, measurement and analysis, process and product quality assurance, configuration management), and for each process area it defines specific goals and the specific practices required to achieve them, plus generic goals and practices. CMMI exists in two flavors:

  • Staged model — rates the whole organization on a five-level maturity scale (below).
  • Continuous model — rates each process area individually on a five-level capability scale (level 0 Incomplete, 1 Performed, 2 Managed, 3 Defined, 4 Quantitatively managed, 5 Optimized), so different process areas can sit at different levels.

The staged maturity levels, from weakest to strongest:

Level Name What the organization does
1 Initial Few processes are defined; success depends on individual heroic efforts rather than on following a process.
2 Repeatable (Managed) Basic project management processes exist; cost, schedule, and functionality are tracked; new projects are planned using experience from similar ones.
3 Defined Management and engineering processes are documented, standardized, and integrated into an organization-wide standard software process; all projects use an approved, tailored version of it.
4 Quantitatively managed (Managed) Detailed process and product quality metrics establish a quantitative foundation; meaningful variation can be separated from random noise, and trends can be predicted.
5 Optimizing Quantitative feedback systems identify process weaknesses and strengthen them proactively; teams analyze defects to find root causes and update processes so known defect types do not recur.

Moving up the ladder is the point of process improvement: an organization climbs from ad hoc heroics (level 1) toward measured, self-correcting processes (level 5).

Real-world: CMMI is the framework that organizations use to formalize process improvement, particularly in large software organizations that want to move beyond simply documenting their processes toward systematically improving them. It is widely used in large systems engineering, defense, and outsourcing companies, where customers often expect supplier processes to be appraised against CMMI levels.

11.3.2 Improvement never ends

Improvement is not a one-time cleanup. You have to analyze the bottlenecks in the process and improve the process to make it efficient and effective, and this is a continuous process that can run over several months or even years.

Assumptions & scope. CMMI's core assumption is that process quality drives product quality — the process-based quality assumption inherited from manufacturing. It applies best to:

  • Large, plan-driven projects built by distributed teams over months or years, where a common, standardized process is essential for coordination;
  • Organizations with the management commitment and budget to sustain a multi-year improvement effort.

It does not fit every situation: agile teams deliberately keep processes light, and forcing a full CMMI program onto a small creative team would be overkill. Even large organizations should adopt the spirit of CMMI (plan thoroughly, control uniformly, track accurately) even when the full model is too heavy for their culture.

Where organizations do run a CMMI-style improvement program, the same failure modes repeat. The lecture flags the mistakes that typically derail the effort:

Pitfalls.

  • Treating improvement as a one-time cleanup: the bottleneck analysis — improve — re-analyze loop must keep running, or the process regresses.
  • Skipping the assessment step: starting to "improve" without knowing your current state wastes effort; you first have to know where you are.
  • Expecting quick results: climbing maturity levels takes months or years, and more than half of all SPI efforts fail — most often because of missing management support or cultural resistance from technical staff.
  • Measuring without acting: collecting metrics is only the lever; the improvement step must turn measurements into process changes.

For students interested in depth on this, quality management is also offered as a separate course at the higher-degree level.

Recap and bridge. CMMI is the process-improvement counterpart to ISO 9001: a maturity ladder (Initial → Repeatable → Defined → Quantitatively managed → Optimizing) on which organizations continuously analyze bottlenecks and improve their process, on the principle that better processes produce better products. Both frameworks concern organization-wide quality; the next topic turns to the day-to-day quality assurance mechanism that both frameworks rely on: verification and validation through reviews.

11.4 Verification and Validation through Reviews

11.4.1 Execution-based testing versus static verification and validation

Hook. A missing requirement found after release can cost dozens of times more to fix than the same mistake found while the document was still being written. How do you find defects before there is even any code to run?

Verification and validation (V&V) is one of the important aspects of quality assurance, and it comes in two families. Execution-based program testing runs the code and observes its behavior. The other family is static — non-execution-based — verification and validation: reviews and inspections, which can be applied to any physical artifact, like a requirements document, a design document, or a test case document. Reviews and inspections complement execution-based program testing and are an important method of weeding out defects as early as possible in the development lifecycle.

Dimension Execution-based testing Reviews and inspections (static V&V)
What it needs An executable program or prototype Any artifact — even a page of a document
When it can start Only after code is written As soon as even a page of a deliverable exists
How it checks Runs the program, observes behavior, finds syntax/semantic errors through test cases People examine the artifact, looking for errors, omissions, and standards violations
Typical artifacts Code, executable prototypes Requirements, design, test cases, acceptance tests, database designs, UML diagrams, code, documents

The timing advantage is decisive. Execution-based testing can only be carried out after the code has been written. Reviews and inspections can be carried out as soon as even a page of a document is ready — a page of the requirements, a test case, an acceptance test case, a database design, or even UML diagrams. That means you can check the quality of work products, including requirements and design, before coding even begins. These reviews and inspections are the quality assurance activities that check the quality of various project deliverables throughout the software development lifecycle: the software itself, its documentation, and the records of the processes — looking for errors and omissions as well as standards violations. Execution-based testing, by contrast, works on an executable program or an executable prototype: you run the program, check its emergent properties, and discover syntax, semantic, and other errors through test cases. Walkthroughs, like reviews and inspections, are static V&V techniques that can be applied to any document — including code — throughout the lifecycle.

Why early removal pays — defect cost multipliers. Defects amplify as they move through the lifecycle: an error in a design decision propagates into the code, the tests, and the user documentation built on it. Standard industry figures give the relative cost of finding and correcting an error at different points: about 1.5 cost units if caught in design, 6.5 units if caught before testing, 15 units if caught during testing, and 67 units if caught after release. An error that would cost 1.5 units to fix in design costs 67 units once released — about 45 times more. This is why the professor stresses removing defects as early as possible: reviews on a one-page requirements draft are the cheapest defect removal the lifecycle offers.

11.4.2 What can be reviewed

During a review, several people examine the software and its associated documentation, looking for potential problems and areas of non-conformance with the organizational quality standards. The review team then makes informed judgments about the level of quality of the documents or the software, and the project managers may use these assessments to make planning decisions and allocate resources to the development process accordingly.

There are different types of reviews: inspections, reviews, and quality reviews, for products and for quality standards. Quality reviews are based on documents produced during the software development process: specifications, design, code, process models, test plans, configuration management procedures, process standards, user manuals, and test cases can all be reviewed.

The review should check two things about the documents or code under review:

  1. Consistency and completeness — are the artifacts internally consistent and complete with respect to each other?
  2. Conformance to standards — if standards have been defined, have these quality standards been followed?

But reviews are not just about checking conformance to standards — they are also used to help discover problems and omissions in the software or project documentation. The conclusions of the review should be formally recorded as part of the quality management process, and if problems are discovered, the review outcome should be passed on to the author of the software or to whoever is responsible for correcting the various errors and omissions. A review without a recorded outcome and a named owner for the corrections is a review that wastes everyone's time.

11.4.3 Purpose: remove defects, not appraise people

The purpose of reviews and inspections is to remove defects in documents and other work products as early as possible within the lifecycle, and so improve software quality. It is not to assess the performance of the development team.

Reviewing is a public process of error detection, compared with the more private component testing a developer does individually at a workstation. A developer testing their own code discovers (and quietly fixes) their own mistakes; in a review, the whole team sees the errors in the document being walked through. Inevitably, mistakes made by individuals are revealed to the entire development team.

Q: Have you ever been part of a review meeting where your code or your design was reviewed — or even requirements or a project plan? All of these can be reviewed, along with database design, configuration plans, change requests, and more.

A: Yes. And these review meetings should not turn into appraisal meetings — they focus on the quality of the product and the removal of defects rather than looking at the individual who authored the product.

Because errors surface publicly, the human side of reviews matters as much as the technical side. To ensure that all developers engage constructively with the review process, project managers have to be sensitive to individual concerns and develop a working culture that provides support without blame when errors, bugs, or defects are discovered. The guideline "review the product, not the producer" is what keeps a review meeting constructive: errors are pointed out gently, the tone stays loose and positive, and nobody is embarrassed or belittled. If reviewers fear being blamed for their mistakes, they will hide problems instead of surfacing them — and the review loses its entire purpose.

11.4.4 Quality reviews are not progress reviews

Quality reviews are not management progress reviews. Information about software quality may feed into management decisions, but the two review types answer different questions:

Quality review Progress review
Focus Quality of the work product: consistency, completeness, conformance Actual progress against the plan: schedule, budget, deliverables
Core question Is this deliverable good enough, and what defects remain? Will the project deliver useful software on time and within budget?
Outcome Defect lists and correction actions for the author Go/no-go, replanning, or cancellation decisions

Progress reviews may also take external factors into account: changed circumstances may mean the software is no longer required, or has to be radically changed. A project that developed high quality software may still have to be cancelled because of changes to the business or the operating environment. The quality of the software and the fate of the project are decided on different grounds — a technically excellent system can be cancelled for business reasons, and a mediocre system can ship because the business needs it.

11.4.5 The three phases of the review process

Although review practices vary, a typical review process is structured in three phases: the pre-review activities, the review meeting itself, and the post-review activities. All three matter.

The diagram of the process has the shape of a pipeline: planning feeds individual preparation and group preparation, both feed the review meeting; the meeting feeds follow-up checks and error correction, which feed improvement of the artifacts. The three phases are:

  1. Pre-review activities — plan the review and prepare.
  2. The review meeting — walk through the artifact and record findings.
  3. Post-review activities — correct, follow up, and verify.

11.4.6 Pre-review activities

Pre-review activities are preparatory, so that everyone comes prepared to the review meeting: go through the required documents, note down any anomalies, and highlight them during the meeting. These activities cover review planning and review preparation, and they are essential for the review to be effective — otherwise a lot of time is wasted in the meeting, errors are pointed out at random, and many people end up raising the same errors because they did not prepare.

Review planning involves:

  1. Setting up the review team,
  2. Arranging a time and place for the review,
  3. Distributing the documents to be reviewed.

Review preparation involves:

  1. The team may meet to get an overview of the software to be reviewed,
  2. Each individual review team member reads and understands the documents or the software and the relevant standards,
  3. Each reviewer works independently to find errors, omissions, or deviations from standards,
  4. Reviewers who cannot attend the meeting may supply written comments on the software.

The preparation step is what makes the meeting efficient: when every reviewer arrives with their own annotated list of anomalies, the meeting time is spent confirming and prioritizing genuine findings rather than discovering them for the first time.

11.4.7 The review meeting

The review meeting should typically last about an hour, and at most two hours even for a very long review; beyond that it becomes tedious for the participants unless you take a good break and resume. So the set of artifacts walked through in a meeting should be reviewable in less than two hours, with all the errors, corrections, or improvements noted.

The meeting follows a fixed structure:

  1. The author walks through the artifact. During the meeting, the author of the document or program being reviewed generally walks through the document with the review team, explaining the material.
  2. A chairperson runs the meeting. One team member chairs the review. The chairperson is responsible for ensuring that all submitted documents and comments are considered, and should sign the record of the minutes of the review meeting — the comments and actions agreed upon during the meeting. The chair keeps the meeting on schedule and halts any drift into side debates.
  3. A recorder formally records decisions. Another team member formally records all the review decisions and actions to be taken. The review issues list becomes both the record of problem areas and the action checklist that guides the author during correction.

The meeting ends with an agreed status for the artifact: accepted, accepted provisionally with minor corrections, or rejected with required corrections followed by another review. All attendees sign off on the outcome.

11.4.8 Post-review activities and follow-up

After the review meeting ends, the issues and problems raised during the review must be addressed — these post-review activities lead to follow-up:

  1. Fix the issues. Actions may involve fixing software bugs, refactoring the software so that it conforms to quality standards, or rewriting documents.
  2. Escalate when needed. Sometimes the problems discovered in the quality review are significant enough that another management review is necessary, to decide whether more resources should be made available to correct them — the time may be short, and the aim is to fix the bugs quickly and deliver or release the product.
  3. Verify the corrections. After the changes have been made, the review chairperson may check that all the review comments have been taken into account, and sometimes another review is required to check that the changes took care of all the review comments from the previous meeting.

Q: Can you define refactoring — in the context of rewriting or improving the code? Do you refactor code to improve its code quality?

A: Refactoring is rewriting code to improve its code quality — making it more effective, efficient, and elegant. It is something developers do on an ongoing basis to keep the code healthy. Refactoring changes the internal structure of the code without changing its external behavior: the program still does the same things, but the code becomes cleaner, more understandable, and easier to extend — which is why it is the standard corrective action when a review finds that code does not conform to quality standards.

11.4.9 Review teams and roles

Review teams should normally have three to four people selected as principal reviewers. The team composition follows the nature of the material:

  • One member should be an experienced programmer or designer who takes responsibility for making significant technical decisions — especially if the review covers the architectural design or the detailed design, where the architect should be involved in the review meeting.
  • The principal reviewers may invite other developers or project members, like the designers of related subsystems or other developers and testers, to contribute. Some of them may not review the whole document, but should concentrate on those sections that affect their work.
  • If a meeting is not possible, the review team may circulate the document and ask for written comments from a number of project team members.
  • The project manager need not be involved in the review unless problems are anticipated that require changes in the project plan — like schedule, budget, or contracts.

Keeping the team small matters: two heads are better than one, but a large crowd dilutes preparation and turns the meeting into a presentation. Everyone who does attend must have prepared in advance.

11.4.10 Remote and distributed reviews

The review processes described assume the team has a face-to-face meeting. That assumption is outdated: project teams are now distributed across countries and locations, and online meetings are common — especially since COVID, it is convenient to join the review from wherever you are located.

Remote reviewing can be supported with shared technology and tools, where each review member can annotate the document with their comments. The face-to-face walkthrough is replaced by an online session or by asynchronous annotation; either way the same discipline applies. Even so, as in every review meeting, the chairperson is responsible for coordinating all the comments received from everybody and discussing the changes with the review team members.

Real-world: tool-supported distributed reviews with per-member annotation have become the norm for geographically spread project teams. Modern review tools keep a per-reviewer annotated copy of the artifact and merge all comments into one issues list that the chair coordinates — preserving the structure of the three-phase process without the travel.

Recap and bridge. Reviews are static V&V: any artifact can be reviewed as soon as a page exists, defects are removed early and cheaply, and the review process runs in three phases — pre-review planning and preparation, the chaired meeting with author walkthrough and recorded findings, and post-review correction with follow-up. Reviews are about removing defects, not appraising people. The next section looks at the sharpest form of review — program inspections, applied to code line by line.

11.5 Program Inspections

11.5.1 Inspecting code line by line

Reviews can be applied to any document — requirements, design, configuration management, test cases, user manuals, and so on. Program inspections are specifically for code: a team of programmers goes through the code line by line and checks for anomalies with respect to the quality standards, the programming style, or the logic — things that execution-based testing may not catch, like exceptions that are not handled.

Program inspections are peer reviews where team members collaborate to find bugs in the program being developed. They may be part of the software V&V process and they complement execution-based testing because inspections do not require the programs to be executed:

  • You can inspect incomplete versions of the programs — even partial code that will not yet run.
  • You can inspect representations like UML models, which cannot be executed at all.
  • You can also review program test cases — test reviews will often find problems with the tests themselves, and so improve their effectiveness in detecting program bugs. A bad test that asserts the wrong thing or never exercises a branch quietly erodes the confidence you can place in a passing test run.

Several team members from different backgrounds take part, making a careful line-by-line review of the program source code. They look for defects and problems and describe them during an inspection meeting. The defects may be:

  • logical errors,
  • anomalies in the code that might indicate an erroneous condition,
  • or even features that have been omitted.

The review team examines the design model for the program code in detail and highlights anomalies and problems.

11.5.2 One syntax error can mask everything

There is a practical reason inspections can catch more at once than testing can.

Worked example — the masking effect. Suppose a program has five defects planted in different parts of the code: a missing closing brace near the top, a wrong loop boundary in function A, a swapped variable in function B, an unhandled division-by-zero in function C, and a forgotten return value in function D.

Step 1 — execute the program. The interpreter or compiler hits the missing brace first. The parser fails and then tries to recover, producing a long cascade of spurious error messages — say 100 reported errors, most of them downstream noise caused by the one real syntax error. The program never runs, so functions A, B, C, and D are never executed at all. The other four real defects never surface: the single syntax error masked the entire process and all the other errors.

Step 2 — inspect the same code manually. A team of four programmers reads the program line by line. The missing brace is found in minutes, but the readers do not stop there: function A's loop bound is checked against its data structure, function B's swapped operands are spotted, function C's missing error handling is flagged, function D's missing return is noted. All four logic defects are found in the same pass, without any one defect hiding the others.

Result: one execution found (effectively) one error; one inspection found all five. Because inspection is a manual process, you can identify as many errors as possible across various segments of the program simultaneously, without each interfering with the other or masking the other errors — the same masking problem we saw during software testing.

Sense-check: the two techniques are not competitors. Execution exercises the program's behavior on real data — which only exists once the program runs; inspection reads structure and logic directly — which works even before the code runs. Masking is why a single test run can never be trusted to reveal everything.

11.5.3 Inspection checklists

During an inspection you may use a checklist of common programming errors to focus the search for bugs. Such checklists can be located from various sources, including books, or built from knowledge of the defects that are common in the particular application domain. Organizations should develop their own checklists from local standards and practices, and update them as new defect types are found.

A typical inspection checklist covers several fault categories, with representative checks:

Fault category What the checklist asks
Data faults Are all variables initialized before their values are used? Have all constants been named? Is there any possibility of buffer overflow?
Control faults Is each conditional condition correct? Is each loop certain to terminate? Are compound statements correctly bracketed? Are all cases in a case statement accounted for?
Input/output faults Are all input variables used? Are all output variables assigned a value before being output? Can unexpected inputs cause corruption?
Interface faults Do all function and method calls have the correct number of parameters? Do formal and actual parameter types match? Are parameters in the right order?
Storage management faults If a linked structure is modified, are all links correctly reassigned? Is dynamic storage allocated and explicitly de-allocated correctly?
Exception management faults Have all possible error conditions been taken into account?

Checklists also differ by programming language: each language has its own characteristic errors and its own level of compile-time checking. For example, a Java compiler checks that functions are called with the correct number of parameters, while a C compiler does not — so an interface-fault checklist matters differently in each language.

11.5.4 Effectiveness — and why companies still hesitate

Companies that use inspections have found them an effective technique for finding bugs, especially in code and designs, and especially in the early stages of the lifecycle, before the code is delivered. Classic industry data makes the point sharply: early work on inspections at IBM reported that more than 60% of the errors in a program were detected by informal program inspections, compared with a defect detection rate of roughly 25% for unit testing. This is why the professor says inspections are effective — the numbers are on their side.

But while many people agree inspections are effective and complement program testing, you cannot do testing only by inspection — you need execution-based testing as well. The two techniques find different classes of defects: inspection finds what reading can reveal (logic, style, exceptions, omissions), and testing finds what running reveals (behavior, timing, integration, environmental issues). One cannot replace the other.

Despite the well-publicized effectiveness of inspections, many software companies are reluctant to spend too much time or invest in them:

  • Engineers with experience in execution-based testing are sometimes unwilling to accept that an inspection can be more effective than program testing.
  • Project managers may be suspicious because inspections require extra cost during design and development, and they may not want to take the risk that there will be no corresponding savings in program testing costs.
  • A lot of time and effort goes into reviews and inspections, and you still have to spend a lot of time on program testing — you cannot leave out execution-based testing — so the savings are not obvious; what you gain from inspections is discovering bugs early in the lifecycle.

The honest framing is that inspections shift defect discovery earlier and cheaper — the 1.5-versus-67 cost multiplier from the previous section — even though they do not eliminate the testing budget.

11.5.5 Student questions on inspections

Q: Have you been part of program inspections, where you and another team member review the code usually line by line and check that it is correct and complete in various aspects?

A: Yes — a team goes through the code line by line and checks it for correctness, completeness, and conformity with programming style and quality standards. That last part matters: inspections do not only chase logic bugs; they also enforce the organization's style and quality standards on every line.

Q: Have you felt that execution-based testing is better than inspections?

A: Both are required. Inspections complement execution-based testing — you cannot do testing only by inspection; you need execution-based testing as well. This is a standing correction in the lecture: any claim that testing is simply "better" than inspection (or the reverse) misses the point — each technique finds defects the other cannot, and quality assurance uses both.

Recap and bridge. Program inspections are peer reviews of code, line by line, driven by checklists of common fault categories, finding errors that execution masks (one syntax error can hide a hundred other problems). They are effective but do not replace execution-based testing — the two complement each other. The next topic looks at how quality management changes when the development process is agile: minimal documentation, informal control, and a team quality culture instead of inspection departments.

11.6 Quality Management in Agile Development

11.6.1 Team quality culture instead of process bureaucracy

Hook. If ISO 9001 and CMMI are so good for quality, why do the fastest-moving software teams want nothing to do with them?

Quality management approaches — especially process-based quality — are typically applied to plan-driven projects. Agile projects rely on a team quality culture rather than formal quality management. Agile methods focus on the development of code, minimize documentation and processes that are not directly concerned with code development, and emphasize informal communication among team members rather than formal communication based on project documents.

In agile development, quality means essentially code quality: good practices like refactoring and test-driven development are used to ensure high quality code, and so a high quality product. Quality management in agile is informal rather than documents-and-standards-based; it relies on establishing a team quality culture in which the team takes responsibility for product quality at every stage of development.

Dimension Plan-driven quality management Agile quality management
Carrier of quality Formal standards, documents, quality manual Team quality culture and shared good practices
Quality assurance Separate QA reviews, inspections, audits Test-driven development, refactoring, pair programming, check-before-check-in
Communication Formal, document-based Informal, face-to-face
Attitude to ISO 9001 / CMMI Frameworks to conform to or climb Bureaucratic overhead to avoid

More than that, the agile community is fundamentally opposed to what it sees as the bureaucratic overhead of standards-based approaches and quality processes as embodied in ISO 9001 or CMMI. Companies that use agile development are rarely concerned about ISO 9001 certification or CMMI certification, because those frameworks are typically designed for a plan-based approach to development in large projects carried out by distributed teams over several months or years. The two worlds operate on different assumptions: formal frameworks assume large, long, documented projects; agile assumes small, fast, face-to-face teams.

11.6.2 Test-driven development as quality assurance

The defining quality practice in agile is test-driven development: you write a test case first, which forces you to understand the requirements well; you write the correct test case and run it to be successful; and only then do you move on to the next piece of code.

Worked example — one test-driven development cycle. Requirement: a bank Account must refuse a withdrawal that exceeds the balance.

Step 1 — write the test first: withdraw(700) on an account holding 500 must throw an InsufficientFunds error (rather than producing a negative balance).

Step 2 — run the test against the current code (which does not check the balance): the test fails — the withdrawal goes through. The failing test proves the requirement is not yet met and pins down what "met" means.

Step 3 — implement: add the balance check to withdraw(), so a withdrawal larger than the balance raises the error.

Step 4 — run the test again: it passes. The cycle closes, and the next piece of code starts with its own new test.

Result: the requirement is understood before the code is written, the code is verified as it is written, and the test case survives as executable documentation of the requirement. Sense-check: notice the order — the test is written and run first, and only a passing test closes the cycle, so quality is built in continuously rather than checked in later.

Test-driven development ensures product quality as you go ahead — quality is maintained continuously throughout development rather than being checked by quality assurance or testing after the implementation, as in a traditional plan-driven process. The test case itself becomes adequate documentation for the code, and writing the test before the code clarifies the requirement: if you cannot write the test, you do not yet understand what the code should do.

11.6.3 Shared good practices

Agile teams maintain a set of shared good practices that build product quality into the work as the software is developed:

  1. Check before check-in — programmers organize their own code reviews with other team members before the code is checked in to the build system.
  2. Never break the build — do not check in code that may cause the whole system to fail.
  3. Fix problems when you see them — do not keep known problems on hold.
  4. Treat the code as belonging to the team — any team member may fix problems in code written by anyone else.

11.6.4 Never break the build

It is not acceptable for team members to check in code that is defective or that may cause the whole system to fail. Individuals have to test their code changes against the whole system in their private workspace and be confident that these programs work as expected before they check in.

The system is gradually integrated, built component by component, with the whole system tested over and over again through regression testing — so a new module or piece of code should not collapse the structure. Regression testing means re-running the existing test suite every time something new is integrated, so that a change that breaks previously working behavior is caught immediately.

If the build is broken, the person responsible for adding the latest piece of code that caused the break is expected to fix the problem immediately, on top priority. The broken build blocks everyone on the team, so the fix is a first-class emergency, not a scheduled task.

11.6.5 Fix problems when you see them; code belongs to the team

Problems should be fixed when you see them — do not keep problems on hold for a long time. A small problem left unattended grows into a large one as more code is built on top of it, and the context needed to fix it fades.

And the code of the system belongs to the team rather than to individuals: if a programmer discovers problems or obscurities in code developed by someone else, he or she can fix these problems directly rather than referring them back to the original developer, if they can be managed. Everyone in an agile team is aware of everyone else's development work and they work together as a team, so small bugs can be fixed even when the person who wrote the code is not available. This is the opposite of the individual-ownership model: the team owns the code, so the team keeps it healthy.

11.6.6 Scrum retrospectives and sprint improvements

Agile processes rarely use formal program reviews and inspections. In an agile process called Scrum, the development team meets after each iteration to discuss quality issues and problems, and the team may decide on changes to the way they work to avoid quality problems that might have emerged.

A sprint is one cycle of the iteration or incrementation — a fixed-length development increment. The collective decision may be made to focus on refactoring the code and improving quality during a sprint rather than adding a new system function. This is how the retrospective turns into action: the team notices a quality problem, and instead of pushing on with new features, it dedicates a whole sprint to cleaning up the code.

Code review may also be the responsibility of individuals, as in the check-before-check-in practice — quality control is distributed across the team, not concentrated in a separate review department.

11.6.7 Pair programming in extreme programming

For code review, extreme programming (XP) — one of the agile methods — uses the technique called pair programming: an approach in which two people are responsible for code improvement and work together to achieve it. The code developed by one individual is checked by the other pair member, and both look at the code before it is accepted.

Pair programming builds a deep knowledge of the program, because both programmers have to understand the program in detail to continue development — a depth of knowledge that is sometimes difficult to achieve in other inspection and walkthrough processes. Pair programming can find bugs that are sometimes not discovered in formal reviews or inspections: the constant, immediate scrutiny catches defects while the code is being written, at the earliest possible moment.

However, the two people involved in pair programming cannot be as objective as an external inspection team, because they are examining their own work.

Pair programming risks — three ways the pair's closeness can compromise defect discovery:

  1. Mutual misunderstanding: both team members may misunderstand a particular requirement — they may make the same mistake in understanding the requirements, and their discussions may reinforce these errors. Two people holding the same wrong interpretation check each other's work and confirm it.
  2. Pair reputation: the pairs may be reluctant to look for errors in each other because they do not want to slow down the progress of the project. Finding a bug means stopping and fixing — a pair that values momentum over scrutiny will skim past defects.
  3. Working relationships: the pair's ability to discover defects is likely to be compromised by their close working relationship, which often leads to reluctance to criticize a team member or work partner. Nobody wants to tell a close colleague that their code is wrong.

11.6.8 When the informal approach works — and when it does not

The informal approach to quality management adopted in agile methods is particularly effective for software product development where the company developing the software also controls its specification. In that situation there is no need to deliver quality reports or standard reports to an external customer or an auditor, and no need to integrate with other quality management teams. The team defines the product, so informal quality works.

But the same informal approach becomes impractical in three classic situations:

  1. Large system for an external customer. A large customer may have its own quality management process and may expect the software company to report on the progress of the project in a way that is compatible with its process — the development team may have to produce a formal quality plan and quality reports as required by the customer.
  2. Several geographically distributed teams. Especially if they come from different companies, formal communication and a formal approach to quality management become very important, and you have to produce formal documentation — informal communication as used by small agile teams may be impractical in large organizations and large teams. Different companies may also have different quality approaches, so a written agreement on process is needed.
  3. Long-lifetime systems. Where the teams involved in development change over time and multiple teams keep working on the project over a period of time, inadequate documentation that does not conform to standards leaves new members unable to understand why certain development decisions were made — they would not be able to progress further or make changes, especially for maintenance projects that run for a very long duration. The knowledge of "why" lives in documentation when the people who made the decisions have moved on.

11.6.9 Blending agility with formal quality for large projects

The informal agile approach may have to be adapted for smaller teams working in a larger organization and on a long-lifetime project: some quality documentation and formal processes are introduced, and standards are followed.

In general, this adapted approach is integrated with the incremental and iterative development process — instead of developing software in every sprint, one of the sprints or one of the iterations should focus on producing the essential documentation and reviewing it, and it is kept up to date and maintained. The documentation sprint produces the minimum necessary documents — requirements overviews, design decisions, interface agreements — rather than the heavyweight document set of a plan-driven process.

In this way you can practice agile management supported by certain formal communication and some quality documentation, with quality processes and standards being followed to ensure quality — especially in large systems development. The agile core survives (iterative development, team ownership, fast feedback); the formal shell is added where external coordination demands it.

11.6.10 Student question on agile practice

Q: Have you been part of agile teams and practiced these good practices — like check before check-in, never break the build, and fix problems when you see them?

A: You should — agile is the way to go for development, even for large projects. Large projects are broken down through a work breakdown structure into smaller, manageable projects which can be built well using agile methods. Incremental and iterative development is the only realistic way: requirements are not complete, concise, and clear even in the requirement specification document, so the classical waterfall model of capturing all requirements, signing off on them, and then starting design and development afterwards does not work. Agile methods give faster development and better quality — agile can actually achieve the typical constraints of good, fast, and cheap.

This answer carries the lecture's strongest practical claim: even large projects should be decomposed (via a work breakdown structure) into smaller pieces that are developed incrementally, because complete and stable requirements up front are a myth — they are incomplete, ambiguous, and change even while written down in the specification document. Waiting to "finish" requirements before designing and building leaves you waiting forever.

Recap and bridge. Agile quality is informal: a team quality culture, test-driven development, check before check-in, never breaking the build, fixing problems immediately, team-owned code, Scrum retrospectives, and pair programming — with pair programming's three risks (mutual misunderstanding, pair reputation, working relationships) as the known weaknesses. Where external customers, distributed teams, or long lifetimes demand it, agile quality adapts by adding a documentation sprint per iteration. The final topic of the lecture connects quality to the quantitative side: software measurement and the four Ps.

11.7 Software Measurement and the Four Ps

11.7.1 No engineering without measurement

Hook. Can you improve something you cannot count? The professor's answer is blunt: no — anything that cannot be defined and measured cannot be improved.

One of the other important aspects of software quality is measurement — software measurement — which includes both product measurement and process measurement. No engineering can be done without measurement, in any branch of engineering; measurement of all aspects of all entities involved in software development is important, and without measurement there is no engineering.

The principle behind this: anything that cannot be defined and measured cannot be improved — if something cannot be defined and measured, it cannot be analyzed, it cannot be improved, and it cannot be controlled. Measurement is the prerequisite for analysis, and analysis is the prerequisite for improvement and control. This is exactly the answer to the mid-semester question about the difference between software engineering and traditional branches of engineering: without measurement, which engineering can be done? Traditional engineering disciplines measure their materials, loads, tolerances, and processes; software engineering must measure its products and processes for the same reason — otherwise it is not engineering at all.

Exam note: measurement and metrics were flagged as a very important topic — the next session covers software measurement, metrics, and analytics in depth. This section gives the foundations: what to measure (products and processes) and the four Ps that every software engineering effort defines and measures.

11.7.2 Product and process measurements

Q: What kind of measurements?

A: Product measurements and process measurements. Product measurements are things like size and complexity. Process measurements are things like the time taken for a task, the number of occurrences of an event in a given period of time, and the number of resources — the budget required, or the time resources required.

Measurement type What it measures Examples from the lecture
Product measurements Attributes of the software product itself Size (lines of code, number of functions), complexity
Process measurements Attributes of the development process Time taken for a task, number of occurrences of an event in a period, resources required (budget, time)

There are many possible product and process measurements. A software metric is a characteristic of a software system, its documentation, or its development process that can be measured objectively — for example, the number of reported defects in a delivered product, the number of person-days required to develop a component, or the average time to repair a defect. Measurements only become useful when they are compared — against each other, against historical data, or against organizational standards — and when the comparison changes a decision. Measurement without comparison and action is data collection, not quality management.

Related to process improvement, you may have heard of DMAIC — define, measure, analyze, improve, and control — in the context of six sigma process improvement; that is a topic the next session touches on. The five steps are a closed loop: define the customer requirements and goals, measure the existing process to get its current quality performance, analyze the defect data to find the root causes, improve the process by eliminating those root causes, and control the process so the causes are not reintroduced. Notice the pattern: the loop starts with define and measure — exactly the principle of this section — and improvement is impossible without the measurement step.

11.7.3 The four Ps of software engineering

Just as marketing has its famous four Ps — product, price, place, and promotion (now expanded to eight) — software engineering has its own four Ps: product, process, project, and people.

P What it covers Example measurements
Product The software artifacts being built Product metrics: size, complexity
Process How the work is carried out Process metrics: time taken, event occurrences, resources used
Project The delivery effort Project metrics: deadline, budget
People The team doing the work People metrics: capability, efficiency, quality in HR parameters

Like any other engineering, these four are defined and measured in each and every software engineering effort. The measurement maps directly onto the four Ps: product metrics (size and complexity), process metrics (time and event counts), project metrics (deadline and budget), and people metrics (capability, efficiency, and quality expressed as HR parameters). When a software organization says it is "managing" a project, it means it is measuring these four things and acting on the numbers.

Real-world: in industry, project tracking (deadline and budget), process data (time and event counts), and people capability data are collected as metrics for exactly the four Ps. Timesheets feed process metrics; milestone tracking feeds project metrics; skill and performance records feed people metrics; code size and complexity tools feed product metrics.

11.7.4 Why people is the most essential P

Q: What are the four Ps of marketing — and what are the four Ps of software engineering? Which of them is the most essential?

A: For marketing: product, price, place, promotion — the right answer, arrived at fast with the help of a quick Google search. For software engineering: product, process, project, and people. And of these, the most essential one is people — because without people there is no product, no project, and no process.

The logic is a simple dependency chain: a process is executed by people, a project is run by people, and a product is built by people. Remove the people and the other three Ps vanish: no team, no work, no software. This is why the professor places people at the center of software engineering quality — the quality culture discussed throughout this lecture is fundamentally a people phenomenon, and even the best measurement program cannot compensate for the wrong team.

Pitfalls.

  • Measuring only what is easy to count (size, time) while ignoring what is hard to count (capability, quality of people) — the four Ps only work when all four are measured.
  • Collecting metrics without acting on them: measurement exists to feed analysis, improvement, and control — raw data changes nothing.
  • Measuring the product and process but forgetting the project dimension: deadline and budget tracking is how the project is kept alive.

Recap. No engineering without measurement: anything that cannot be defined and measured cannot be analyzed, improved, or controlled. Software engineering measures four Ps — product, process, project, and people — with people as the most essential, because without people there is no product, no project, and no process. The four Ps and the notion of software measurement and metrics are important and extensive; they are taken up in full in the next session, together with software analytics.

Key Industry Applications

  • ISO 9001 certification: used across manufacturing, services, and software; customers routinely require their software suppliers to be certified, and independent accreditation authorities carry out the certification audits.
  • The ISO 9001 chain: core processes, organizational quality manual, quality plans, project quality management — the standard structure for quality management in certified organizations, instantiated from the generic framework into each company's manual and each project's plan.
  • CMMI: the organization-wide framework for continuous process improvement, with bottleneck analysis running over months or years; large software organizations use its maturity levels to formalize and measure improvement.
  • Reviews and inspections: used in industry on requirements, design, code, test plans, and user manuals, guided by domain-specific inspection checklists; remote tool-supported reviews are standard for distributed teams, with per-member annotation coordinated by the review chair.

  • Six sigma DMAIC (define, measure, analyze, improve, control) used for process improvement in industry, with the define and measure steps as the foundation of every improvement cycle.

Verification and Validation through Reviews

Must-know: Static V&V (reviews, inspections, walkthroughs) can start as soon as a page of any artifact exists, unlike execution-based testing which needs executable code; reviews remove defects early, never appraise people; quality reviews are not progress reviews (schedule/budget); review process = pre-review (planning + preparation), meeting (author walkthrough, chair, recorder, max ~2 hours), post-review (corrections, follow-up, possible re-review); teams of 3-4 principal reviewers.

⚠️ Top pitfall: Letting a review become an appraisal meeting (it must focus on product quality, not the author); reviewing without preparation, which wastes the meeting and produces random, duplicated findings.

Self-check: Name the three phases of the review process and one activity in each phase.

Program Inspections

Must-know: Program inspections = line-by-line peer review of code without execution (works on incomplete code and UML models); one syntax error can produce 100 exceptions and mask all other errors at execution, while inspection surfaces many errors simultaneously; checklists cover data, control, input/output, interface, storage, and exception faults; inspections and execution-based testing complement each other — testing only by inspection is impossible.

⚠️ Top pitfall: Claiming execution-based testing is better than inspections (or vice versa): both are required, each finds defects the other misses.

Self-check: Name the six fault categories on a typical inspection checklist and give one example check for each.

Quality Management in Agile Development

Must-know: Agile quality management is informal: team quality culture, quality = code quality; TDD writes the test first, runs it, then writes code; good practices: check before check-in, never break the build (regression testing), fix problems when you see them, code belongs to the team; Scrum retrospectives may dedicate a sprint to refactoring; pair programming has three risks (mutual misunderstanding, pair reputation, working relationships); informal quality must be adapted for external customers, distributed teams, and long-lifetime systems by producing essential documentation in dedicated sprints.

⚠️ Top pitfall: Assuming agile quality management can be fully informal everywhere: external customers, distributed teams, and long-lifetime systems still require formal documentation and processes.

Self-check: List the three risks of pair programming and explain why each reduces defect discovery.

Software Measurement and the Four Ps

Must-know: No engineering without measurement: anything that cannot be defined and measured cannot be analyzed, improved, or controlled. Measurements are product (size, complexity) and process (time taken, event occurrences, resources). The four Ps of software engineering are product, process, project, and people (marketing's are product, price, place, promotion); people is the most essential P because without people there is no product, no project, and no process.

⚠️ Top pitfall: Forgetting the project dimension (deadline, budget) or the people dimension (capability, efficiency) when measuring — all four Ps must be measured; also collecting metrics without acting on them.

Self-check: What are the four Ps of software engineering, and which is the most essential and why?
