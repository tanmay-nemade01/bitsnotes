2.1 Streaming, Social, E-Commerce and Data Growth

Modern applications group into a few demanding families. Streaming and OTT platforms serve video to huge audiences at the same time. Social apps carry photos, posts, messages, notifications, and constant interaction between users. E-commerce sites support browsing, inventory, carts, orders, payments, and shipping. A fourth force cuts across all of them: data deluge — an extremely large and still growing volume of data created every minute through messages, uploads, searches, and transactions.

Hook: What happens when ten million people press play at the same second? The screen must not freeze, the bill must still be right, and nobody waits. This section names the workloads that force that standard.

Think of a stadium final. Gates open, crowds push in together, vendors sell food, and cameras stream the match out. The stadium needs wide gates (throughput), fast entry checks (low latency), and a structure that holds when all seats fill (scale). A modern app faces the same test, with requests in place of fans.

Everyday picture: a stadium on final night. Fans arrive in a burst, buy food, and send photos home, all at once. The map: gates stand for APIs, vendors stand for services, and the crowd surge stands for peak traffic. The picture breaks here: fans leave after the match, but app data stays and keeps growing, so storage pressure never resets.

Family Typical burst Hardest part
Streaming and OTT Millions press play together Moving huge video bytes fast with no stalls
Social One post draws millions of reactions Storing endless content and fetching it back fast
E-commerce Sale minute spikes checkouts Keeping inventory, cart, order, and payment in step

Pick the row that fits, then read its goal: streaming chases smooth playback, social chases fast feeds, and shops chase correct orders.

2.1.1 Streaming and OTT platforms

A streaming platform (a service that sends video over the network to many viewers at once, written as load from viewers) delivers video over the network to many viewers at once. Real-world: Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, and Disney Hotstar. When millions of people open the app together, the system must handle user authentication, video delivery, subscriptions, recommendations, and playback requests side by side.

Streaming platform: a service that delivers video over the network to many viewers at once. Three goals define it. High availability (the service stays reachable even under heavy load) keeps the app open. Low latency (the gap between asking and receiving stays small) keeps controls quick. Scalability (the ability to handle a growing workload by adding capacity) keeps launch night alive.

Three goals dominate here. The first is high availability — the service stays reachable even under heavy load. The second is low latency — the delay between request and response stays small. Latency means the gap between asking and receiving. Buffering is a symptom of weak delivery performance: the video stalls because data does not arrive in time. The third goal is scalability — the ability to handle a growing workload by adding capacity.

A quick size check shows why scale leads. Take 5 million viewers watching at 5 megabits per second each. Total flow is 5,000,000 times 5, which gives 25,000,000 megabits per second, or 25 terabits per second. No single box serves that. The answer is many servers sharing the load, plus copies of popular video stored close to viewers.

Scope: these three goals apply to live launch-night peaks and daily prime-time load. Assumption: viewers hold decent connections and the catalog is ready in advance. When the last-mile link is weak, even a strong back end cannot stop buffering, so players drop video quality to fit the link.

2.1.2 Social applications

A social application (an app where users create and share content and react to each other, written as posts plus reactions) lets users create and share content and react to each other. Real-world: Instagram, Facebook, and Twitter. People upload photos continuously, post messages, send notifications, and interact with other users.

The hard part is bursts. A celebrity post can draw millions of reactions in a short window, so the system must absorb a sudden rise in requests. It must also store enormous amounts of content and fetch it back fast. That pairs two needs: durable storage for photos, posts, and messages, plus efficient retrieval when many readers ask for the same items.

Social application: an app where users create, share, and react to content. Two pressures shape it. Write bursts (many uploads and reactions land at once) stress intake. Hot reads (many readers ask for the same post) stress fetch. Storage must hold all of it without loss.

A numbers sketch makes the burst real. A star posts one photo. One million fans open it within a minute. Each view pulls a 500-kilobyte image. Total bytes moved are 1,000,000 times 500 kilobytes, which is 500 gigabytes in one minute. Cached copies near readers absorb this; a single database cannot.

Scope: burst design covers viral posts and launch minutes, not steady chat. Assumption: reads far outnumber writes for hot posts. When every user writes as much as they read, intake and ordering logic need their own plan.

2.1.3 E-commerce applications

An e-commerce application (a shop system that covers browse, cart, pay, and ship, written as the full buying path) supports the full buying path: browse products, check inventory, fill a cart, place an order, pay, arrange shipping, and send updates. During a major sale such as a Big Billion Day event, traffic climbs steeply. Product pages, availability checks, cart adds, and checkouts all spike together and create a flood of requests to back-end services.

If the payment service fails during that peak, shoppers cannot complete purchases even though browsing still works. That is why separating responsibilities matters: when each duty lives in its own boundary, trouble in one area does not have to stop the rest. Real-world: large sale events on major shopping sites.

E-commerce application: a system that carries the full buying path from browse to ship. Its duties split into catalog, inventory, cart, order, payment, and shipping. Correctness matters as much as speed: charging twice or losing an order costs trust, not just seconds.

The order of duties is fixed. Browse leads to inventory checks, inventory leads to cart adds, carts lead to orders, and orders lead to payment. Each step depends on the last, so a failure late in the chain hurts most: the shopper paid but sees no order. Later sections trace this exact failure and its fixes.

Pitfalls: two traps repeat here. First, testing only the happy path: browsing works in tests, but checkout under load was never tried. Second, treating payment like any other call: money movement needs extra care with retries and duplicate protection, which Section 2.9 covers in full.

2.1.4 Data deluge

Data deluge means data arrives in huge volume and keeps growing. Every minute, users create messages, upload videos, run searches, and generate transactions. Modern systems must stay scalable, process efficiently, and manage data reliably. They must serve enormous numbers of users and hold large amounts of data without giving up reliability or performance.

Q: Are there any questions on these platform needs before moving on? A: No blocking doubt came up at this point, so the discussion moved on from platform examples to the general requirements that apply across all of them.

Recap: streaming, social, and shop workloads each burst in their own way, and data deluge presses on all three. Next, the four general requirements turn these stories into design targets.

Streaming scale, social bursts, shop correctness, and endless data growth all push toward cloud native design: many small units, added on demand, watched closely. That is where this lecture heads.

2.2 General Requirements Across Modern Applications

Four general requirements apply to almost every modern system: zero downtime, shortened feedback cycles, mobile and multi-device support, and data-driven behavior. They describe what users expect no matter which device or front end they use.

Hook: your bank app updates at 2 a.m. while you sleep — and still works at 2:05 a.m. when you check your balance. How does a team change the engine while the car keeps driving?

Think of a hospital that repaints wards and swaps equipment without closing. One wing stays open while the other is refitted, signs point patients to open doors, and work moves in stages. Software teams do the same: spare copies stay live, a router sends users to healthy ones, and updates roll out ward by ward.

2.2.1 Zero downtime

Downtime is the period when an application is unavailable. Zero downtime is the goal of keeping the application reachable at all times, including during updates. A banking application, for example, should stay usable even while developers deploy a new version.

Zero downtime: the goal of keeping the app reachable at all times, even during updates. Three tools carry it. Redundant instances (extra live copies) take over when one copy stops. A load balancer (a router that spreads requests across healthy copies) steers users away from stopped copies. A rolling deployment (updates applied to copies in stages) never stops all copies at once.

Several techniques support this goal. Redundant instances keep extra copies running so one can take over. A load balancer spreads requests across healthy copies. A rolling deployment updates copies in stages instead of all at once. Even with all three, absolute zero downtime is hard to guarantee, so teams treat it as an important design objective rather than a promise.

Picture the math on a small fleet. Six copies run version A. The team updates two at a time: step one leaves four on A and two moving to B, step two leaves two on A and four on B, step three leaves all six on B. Live capacity never drops below four, so users see no gap. Four of six is the lowest live count in this trace.

Absolute zero downtime is hard to guarantee, so treat it as an important design objective: the team designs for no gaps, measures real gaps, and keeps shrinking them, but never prints "zero" as a promise on a contract.

Scope: this trio covers planned updates and single-copy failures. Assumption: copies are truly independent, with no shared switch that all depend on. When the shared database or the balancer itself stops, spare app copies cannot save the day.

2.2.2 Shortened feedback cycles

A feedback cycle (the loop from writing software to releasing it and learning from use, written as code to release to learn) is the loop from writing software to releasing it and learning from use. A team that releases every few months learns slowly. Teams with automated build and deployment practices release far more often. Shorter cycles help developers find problems early and respond to customer needs sooner.

Feedback cycle: the loop from code to release to learning from real use. Shorter loops mean smaller changes per release, faster bug discovery, and quicker response to what customers actually do.

Compare the two rhythms. A team shipping every 12 weeks gets about 4 learning points a year. A team shipping every week gets about 52. The weekly team spots a bad button in days; the quarterly team spots it after a season. The math favors small, frequent steps: 52 learning points beat 4.

Pitfalls: speed without safety is just faster breakage. Teams that shorten cycles must pair them with automated tests and staged rollouts, or every release becomes a gamble.

2.2.3 Mobile and multi-device support

Users reach the same service through the web, phones, tablets, and laptops. The back end must serve all of these front ends. APIs help because many front-end applications can talk to the same back-end services. The back end exposes stable interfaces, and each device builds its own experience on top.

Multi-device support: one back end serving web, phone, tablet, and laptop front ends. APIs (stable interfaces that many front ends call, first named in this lecture the way a restaurant menu lists what the kitchen serves) make this work: the back end keeps one set of duties, and each device builds its own look on top.

The shape is one-to-many. One order service exposes a stable contract; four front ends call it. When the phone team redesigns its screens, the back end does not change. When the back end adds a field, old front ends ignore it until they update.

2.2.4 Data-driven behavior

A data-driven application (an app that uses collected behavior to guide decisions, written as past views guiding next picks) uses collected data to guide decisions. Real-world: Netflix studies viewing behavior and recommends content that fits a viewer's taste. Real-world: shopping sites study purchase and browsing history and suggest similar products, including items a shopper viewed but did not buy. Past views, past purchases, and viewing habits all feed the next recommendation.

Data-driven behavior: the app watches what users do and uses it to shape what they see next. Views, purchases, and browsing history feed the next round of picks.

Trace one loop. A viewer watches two space documentaries fully but skips a comedy after three minutes. The system records two full watches and one skip. The next home screen shows more science titles and fewer comedies. Each visit sharpens the picture: watch, record, adjust, repeat.

Scope: data-driven picks guide suggestions, not guarantees. Assumption: stored behavior reflects real taste. When accounts are shared or clicks are accidental, the signal blurs and picks drift.

Recap: no gaps during updates, faster learning loops, one back end for every screen, and behavior that teaches the next screen. Next, eleven quality attributes turn these goals into testable traits.

Hospitals, print runs, and menus all point one way: keep the service open, learn fast, serve every device, and let behavior teach.

2.3 Quality Attributes

Quality attributes are non-functional requirements: they describe how well the system works, not which features it has. Eleven of them shape modern application design. Each one earns attention because users feel it directly when it is missing.

Hook: two shops sell the same phone at the same price — one page opens in a blink with two-tap checkout, the other stalls and asks for ten forms. Which one gets your money?

Think of buying a flat. The feature list says two bedrooms; the quality check asks how it lives: does water run at 7 a.m., do doors close right, can wiring take a new AC? Features say what it is; attributes say how well it lives. Software attributes work the same way.

2.3.1 Scalability and performance

Scalability is the ability to handle a growing workload. During a sale, more shoppers arrive at once, and the system must absorb them. Performance is speed and efficiency in everyday use. If a product page loads slowly, shoppers leave. Fast page loads and quick responses belong here.

Scalability: the ability to handle a growing workload by adding capacity. Performance: speed and efficiency in everyday use. Scale answers "can it hold the crowd"; performance answers "is each visit quick."

Test the split with numbers. A page that loads in 1 second keeps most shoppers; at 5 seconds many leave. That is performance. A site holding 1,000 users that also holds 100,000 after adding servers shows scalability. One measures each visit; the other measures the crowd: fast visit versus big crowd.

Scope: these two cover request volume and response speed. Assumption: each added unit of capacity adds real headroom. When the database is the single choke point, added app copies queue at the same door and scale stalls.

2.3.2 Availability and reliability

Availability means the service stays reachable when needed. Banking services, for example, must stay around the clock. Reliability means the system behaves correctly over time. If an order is placed, it should be processed consistently, without lost or half-applied work.

Availability: the service stays reachable when needed. Reliability: work completes correctly over time, with no lost or half-applied results. An open door that scrambles orders is available but not reliable.

Put numbers on availability. "99.9 percent up" leaves 0.1 percent down. Over 30 days (43,200 minutes), 0.1 percent is about 43 minutes dark. "99.99 percent" cuts that to about 4 minutes. Each extra nine divides the dark window by ten — and costs real effort.

Pitfalls: teams mix the two up. A login page that always opens but drops every third payment is highly available and badly unreliable. Test both: can users reach it, and does their work finish right?

2.3.3 Interoperability and testability

Interoperability is the ability to work with other systems. Real-world: a payment API integrated with a bank, so the shop and the bank act as one flow. Testability means a single service is easy to test in isolation. Small, well-bounded services are easier to check than one giant unit.

Think of travel plugs. A phone charger that fits sockets in three countries interoperates; one with a fixed odd plug does not. For testability, think of a bicycle versus a train: a loose wheel spins free on a stand, but a train car cannot be tested without the track. Small services are the loose wheels: check one part without starting the whole train.

2.3.4 Usability and modifiability

Usability is ease of use. A simple checkout with few steps is the classic example. Modifiability means a change in behavior is easy to make. Updating recommendation logic or changing a business rule should not require rebuilding everything.

The checkout test is concrete. Count the steps: cart, address, pay, confirm is four. Each added form loses some shoppers. Halving steps from eight to four can rescue a visible share of sales. Modifiability guards the other side: when festival rules change discount logic, one rule file changes and ships alone — no full rebuild.

2.3.5 Security, portability and maintainability

Security means protection against unauthorized access through authentication, encryption, and authorization. Portability means easy movement across environments: a container that runs on one host should run on another host as well. Maintainability means a small service can be improved or updated on its own, so the team can fix one part without disturbing the rest.

Security (authentication proves who you are, encryption scrambles data in transit, authorization sets what you may do), portability (runs the same on any host), and maintainability (one part improves without disturbing the rest) close the list of eleven.

A door lock shows all three at once. The key check is authentication, the frosted glass is encryption, and the staff-only sign is authorization. Portability is the same lock fitting any door; maintainability is swapping one lock without rebuilding the house.

Scope: these eleven name the traits exams probe. Assumption: no single design maximizes all eleven; teams trade them off. Extra security checks can slow responses; extra portability layers can add weight.

Exam note: expect scenario questions that name a symptom and ask which attribute is at stake — slow page loads point to performance, failed checkouts point to reliability and interoperability, and a painful move between hosts points to portability.

Recap: eleven traits, one question each — how well, not what. Next, four concrete shifts show how the industry rebuilt itself to meet them.

Flats, plugs, locks, and checkout counters all ask the same question: not what it does, but how well it lives under pressure.

2.4 Four Shifts in Software Engineering

Four changes over the last several years explain why cloud native practices spread. Each shift moved one layer of the stack: code organization, hosting, packaging, and delivery.

Hook: the same shop that once ran on one big computer in a back room now runs as dozens of small services on rented machines worldwide. What four moves made that possible?

Think of a printing press growing into a newspaper chain. First one big press prints everything; then regional presses split the work; then the chain rents press time instead of owning presses; then each edition ships on its own trucks. Code, hosting, packaging, and delivery each moved the same way.

Shift From To Layer moved
1. Code Monolith Microservices Organization
2. Hosting On-premises Cloud Where it runs
3. Packaging Virtual machines Containers How it ships
4. Delivery Single pipeline Per-service pipelines How it releases

When picking for a scenario, match the pain to the row: slow joint releases point to row 1 and 4, hardware bills point to row 2, heavy slow images point to row 3.

2.4.1 From monolith to microservices

A monolith (many duties in one deployable unit, written as one box shipping together) combines many duties into one deployable unit. An e-commerce monolith might hold users, products, orders, carts, payments, and notifications together. A microservice approach turns each duty into its own service. Teams can then update one service and scale one service without touching the rest.

Monolith: many duties packed into one deployable unit that ships together. Microservice: one business duty per service, each shipping and scaling alone. The split trades one big coordination point for many small network calls.

A concrete split makes it real. One e-commerce box holds six duties. Search traffic grows 10 times while the rest stay flat. The monolith answer copies the whole box 10 times — paying for 10 copies of payments and notifications nobody needs. The microservice answer copies only search 10 times: 10 search copies, 1 copy of the rest.

2.4.2 From on-premises to cloud

On-premises means an organization buys and maintains its own physical servers. Cloud means the organization rents computing capacity and managed services on demand. Real-world: AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud. Instead of buying hardware, teams rent capacity and use managed building blocks as needed.

On-premises: you buy and tend your own servers. Cloud: you rent capacity and managed services on demand and pay for what you use. The shift moves money from big upfront boxes to metered use.

The money story decides many moves. A server box costs a large sum once plus power and staff every year, and sits idle at night. Rented capacity costs by the hour and shrinks after the peak. A shop with one big sale a year rents the peak instead of owning it: own the average, rent the spike.

Scope: the cloud shift covers hosting and managed building blocks, not design. Assumption: network links to the provider stay fast and bills are watched. Lift an unchanged monolith and old scaling limits ride along to new hardware.

2.4.3 From virtual machines to containers

A virtual machine carries its own guest operating system. A container packages the application with its dependencies while sharing the host operating system. Containers are lighter and start faster: a container can start in seconds while a virtual machine can take minutes. That speed matters when many small services must be packed and deployed. Real-world: Docker for packaging and Kubernetes for orchestration.

Virtual machine: a full guest operating system per app, heavy but strongly isolated. Container: the app plus its dependencies sharing the host system, light and fast to start. Docker packs the box; Kubernetes places and watches the boxes.

The start-time gap drives the choice. A virtual machine boots a whole OS in minutes; a container starts its process in seconds. Scaling from 10 to 100 units during a sale takes minutes either way per unit — but seconds-per-unit finishes the fleet far sooner. Roughly: minutes per box versus seconds per box.

2.4.4 From single pipeline to per-service pipelines

Traditional delivery often used one build and one release pipeline for the whole application. With many microservices, each service gets its own pipeline. An order service, a payment service, and a notification service each build, test, and deploy alone. When one service needs an update, only that service is rebuilt. That change touches architecture, infrastructure, packaging, and delivery at once.

Single pipeline: one build and release path for the whole app — one stuck test blocks everyone. Per-service pipelines: each service builds, tests, and ships alone — one stuck test blocks one team.

Count the blast radius. Five teams share one pipeline; the payment team's urgent fix waits behind four other teams' tests. Five pipelines later, the same fix ships in its own lane while others continue. Fewer waiters, smaller rebuilds, faster fixes: one lane blocked versus five lanes blocked.

Pitfalls: per-service pipelines need contract tests between services, or teams ship alone and break each other silently. Independence without agreed interfaces is just separate chaos.

Recap: code split, hosting rented, packages lightened, delivery split — four moves, one stack rebuilt. Next, how teams actually adopted them step by step.

Presses, houses, and trucks all tell the same story: split the work, rent the peak, pack light, ship in separate lanes.

2.5 How Cloud Native Evolved

The move to cloud native was gradual. Development moved from sequential steps to short iterations to automated flow. Architecture moved from one unit to layered tiers to small services. Hosting moved from physical servers to virtual servers to containers. Infrastructure moved from private data centers to hosted rooms to cloud platforms.

Hook: nobody woke up one morning and rewrote everything as microservices — so what small steps piled up into this big change?

Think of learning to cook for a crowd. First you follow one long recipe start to finish. Then you taste and adjust in rounds. Then the kitchen gets labeled stations and a conveyor from prep to plate. Each habit stays; the kitchen just gets faster and safer.

2.5.1 Development from waterfall to agile to DevOps

Waterfall means sequential stages: requirements, then design, then implementation, then testing. Agile means short iterations, often two-week or three-week sprints, with continuous feedback. DevOps connects development and operations and automates the path from code to release.

Waterfall: sequential stages with one big test at the end. Agile: short sprints with feedback every round. DevOps: development plus operations with an automated path from code to release.

The feedback math repeats here. A 6-month waterfall finds a wrong requirement after 6 months. Two-week sprints surface it within 2 weeks — about 13 times sooner. DevOps then removes the manual handoff between "code done" and "code live," so the fix reaches users in hours, not weeks.

Pitfalls: agile without discipline becomes random motion — sprints with no review learn nothing. DevOps without automation is just a new team name on the same manual steps.

2.5.2 Architecture from monolith to tiered to microservices

Early systems were often one monolith. A common next step was a tiered design with a presentation layer, a business layer, and a data-access layer. The microservice step then splits business capabilities into independently deployable services.

Tiered design: code split into layers (presentation, business, data access) that still ship together. Microservices: capabilities split into services that ship alone. Tiers organize code; services organize teams and releases.

The difference is what ships. Three tiers in one box still rebuild together — a data-layer fix redeploys the presentation layer too. Three services ship apart — a pricing fix touches only pricing. Same three boxes on a diagram, very different release days: one ship versus three ships.

2.5.3 Infrastructure from physical to virtual to containers to cloud

Physical servers gave way to virtual servers, which gave way to containers. Each step added flexibility in how resources are assigned and how software is deployed. On the infrastructure side, data-center hosting gave way to hosted infrastructure and then to cloud. Cloud native is the overall engineering approach that brings these strands together: agile habits, DevOps automation, microservices, containers, and cloud.

Cloud native: the overall approach combining agile habits, DevOps automation, microservices, containers, and cloud. Not one tool — the full bundle of habits plus parts.

Stack the gains. Virtual servers cut hardware waste by sharing boxes. Containers cut start time from minutes to seconds. Cloud cuts buying to renting. Together they let a team grow from 10 to 100 units for a peak and shrink back after: pay for the peak hours, not the peak boxes.

Recap: habits shortened, code split, boxes lightened, hosting rented — gradual steps, one combined approach. Next, the three cloud labels, which look alike but differ deeply.

Recipes, kitchens, and timelines agree: small steady steps, kept every round, built the new normal.

2.6 Cloud-Enabled Versus Cloud-Based Versus Cloud-Native

Three cloud application models look similar from the outside but differ in how much changes inside. One adds a cloud touch to an older system. One moves the same system onto cloud hardware. One redesigns the system for cloud strengths.

Hook: three shops all say "we use the cloud" — one still runs its old till, one moved the till to a rented room, one rebuilt the till as small replaceable parts. Which one survives the festival rush?

Think of three kitchens. The first keeps its old stoves but orders sauces from a supplier. The second moves the whole old kitchen into a rented hall. The third rebuilds as food stations, each with its own cook, tools, and counter. Same word "catering," three different kitchens.

Model What moves Design change Scaling
Cloud-enabled Selected duties only None, stays legacy Limited by old design
Cloud-based Whole app to cloud hosts None, same architecture Whole-unit scaling
Cloud-native Whole app redesigned Split into services Per-service scaling

When a scenario names a legacy mainframe with one cloud API, answer enabled. Same code on rented hosts, answer based. Separate services with own pipelines, answer native.

2.6.1 Cloud-enabled applications

A cloud-enabled application (an older system that borrows selected cloud duties through APIs, written as legacy core plus cloud edges) is an existing or legacy application that uses some cloud capability, often through APIs. The core stays where it was; only selected duties reach into the cloud.

Real-world: a banking system that still runs on a mainframe but calls a cloud-hosted account API for part of its work. Real-world: a shop that keeps its main system on its own servers but stores images in S3 or sends notifications through a cloud service. The architecture stays legacy, often monolithic. Deployment is traditional or hybrid. Scaling stays limited by the original design.

Cloud-enabled: the legacy core stays put; selected duties reach into the cloud through APIs. Architecture, deployment rhythm, and scaling limits stay legacy. The cloud is a helper, not the home.

Walk one call. A withdrawal request hits the mainframe. The mainframe checks the balance locally, then calls a cloud-hosted fraud-check API. The API answers in 200 milliseconds. The mainframe approves and dispenses. The path works — but if the mainframe slows, no cloud helper speeds its core: fast edges, slow middle.

Q: Can you give an example of a cloud-enabled application? A: Yes. A legacy banking system running on a mainframe counts when it talks to cloud-hosted services through APIs — the mainframe does the core work and borrows the cloud for parts. Another case is a shop that runs its main system on its own servers but stores images in S3 or sends notifications through a cloud service. The main system does not move; it only borrows cloud duties for selected parts of the work.

2.6.2 Cloud-based applications

A cloud-based application (the same architecture lifted onto cloud hosts, written as same code, new home) runs on cloud infrastructure without changing its internal architecture. A team might lift an e-commerce monolith from its own servers to EC2 instances or Azure hosts. The code stays monolithic; only the hosting changes.

Cloud-based: the same application lifted onto cloud hosts. Hosting changes; architecture does not. The team rents instead of owning but still scales and ships one big unit.

The gains are practical: no need to tend physical servers or backup hardware, ability to scale up and down, auto-scaling, a goal of zero downtime, high availability such as 99.99 percent, and paying for what is used. Limits remain because the design did not change. A monolithic shape still scales as a whole, so scalability depends on application design. Some configuration, software setup, or hardware provisioning work also remains during the move. Deployment is cloud-based, and delivery tends to be slower than a fully automated cloud-native path.

The 99.99 percent figure deserves its own check. Over 30 days (43,200 minutes), 0.01 percent downtime is 0.0001 times 43,200, which is about 4 minutes dark per month. Strong — but still not zero, and one bad deploy of the whole monolith can spend that budget in one night.

Q: If cloud-based already runs in the cloud, why does it still need effort for software or hardware setup, and is hardware still manual? A: The application code moves to the cloud, but the move itself still needs configuration, software setup, and provisioning choices — machine sizes, networks, and storage do not pick themselves. The underlying hardware is managed by the provider, and auto-scaling is available because the system is cloud-based. How much effort remains depends on the architecture and application design: a monolith and a set of microservices behave differently even when both run in the cloud.

2.6.3 Cloud-native applications

A cloud-native application (a system designed for cloud strengths from the start, written as services that scale and ship alone) is designed and run to take advantage of cloud capabilities, not merely hosted in the cloud. The system is split into separate services. An e-commerce system might have product, payment, user, order, cart, and notification services. Each service can be developed, deployed, and scaled alone. If the product service faces more traffic than the user service, the team grows only the product service.

Cloud-native: designed for cloud strengths from the start — split services, container packing, automated delivery, per-service scaling. Targets scalability, elasticity, resilience, and automated deployment together.

Typical technology includes Docker and Kubernetes. The approach targets cloud strengths: scalability, elasticity, resilience, and automated deployment. Scaling is flexible. Delivery is automated with CI/CD and a containerized approach. The path runs from cloud-enabled to cloud-based to cloud-native, with deeper change at each step.

Replay the festival math per service. Product views spike 10 times; user signups stay flat. The team grows product from 2 to 20 copies and leaves user at 2. Total copies move from 4 to 22 instead of copying everything 10 times. Precision beats wholesale: grow the hot service, not the whole shop.

Scope: use these labels for hosting-plus-design shape. Assumption: the scenario names where code runs and whether duties split. A monolith on rented hosts is cloud-based, not cloud-native, no matter how modern the provider sounds.

Recap: borrow edges, move house, or rebuild the kitchen — three depths, one vocabulary. Next, the design rules that make the rebuilt kitchen survive fires.

Kitchens, tills, and houses converge: name what moved and what split, and the label picks itself.

2.7 Cloud-Native Design, Delivery and the Layered Stack

Cloud native is not only a hosting label. It shapes how systems handle failure, how teams ship, how they pay, and how the layers fit. The same ideas return in every part of the design: expect failure, ship in small boxed units, automate the path, and pay for use.

Hook: one movie-ticket service stays up when its payment partner stalls — tickets still sell, seats still book, only pay-later orders queue. What design lets part of the system fail without taking the rest down?

Think of city power. No single cable feeds every home; grids split into zones with breakers between them. One zone trips, breakers open, the rest of the city keeps lights on. Cloud native wiring works the same: split duties, add breakers, keep spare paths ready.

2.7.1 Designing for failure

Distributed applications involve many services plus networking, databases, and infrastructure parts. Any of them can fail. Cloud-native design expects that. A retry tries a failed call again. A circuit breaker blocks repeated calls to a failing service so trouble does not cascade. Timeouts bound how long a caller waits. Redundant instances keep spare capacity ready. Together they form a resilient shape.

Real-world: Netflix keeps playing video even when recommendations are unavailable, which shows fault isolation. One failing service does not have to stop the rest.

Resilient shape: retries for shaky calls, circuit breakers that stop hammering a failing service, timeouts that bound every wait, and spare copies ready to take over. Fault isolation (one failing service does not stop the rest) is the result.

Walk the Netflix moment. Playback asks recommendations for "more like this." The call times out after 300 milliseconds. The breaker counts three slow calls and opens for 30 seconds. Playback stops asking and shows the video with a plain "suggestions paused" strip. When the breaker half-opens and one probe succeeds, calls resume. Viewers never lose the movie: protect the core, degrade the extras.

Scope: these patterns cover shaky networks and slow services. Assumption: failures are partial and temporary. Retries against a truly dead service just add load — breakers and timeouts must cap them.

2.7.2 Building and shipping with containers and pipelines

Implementation centers on containers and automated pipelines. Instead of installing dependencies by hand on each machine, the team builds a container image and deploys that image. Everything the application needs travels inside the package, so delivery is faster and more consistent. A cloud-based lift without redesign still needs setup and provisioning, which makes it slower by comparison.

Container image: the app plus everything it needs, packed once and run anywhere. Automated pipeline: build, test, and ship without hand steps on each machine. Pack once, deploy the same box everywhere.

Compare the two setups for ten machines. Hand setup repeats install steps ten times — ten chances to drift. Image setup builds once and runs the same box ten times — one build, ten identical runs. Drift drops to near zero: one build, ten copies, zero surprises.

Pitfalls: images grow stale like anything else. Teams that never rebuild base images ship old libraries with known holes. Automate rebuilds on a schedule, not just on code changes.

2.7.3 Pricing and cost trade-offs

Cloud native often uses consumption-based pricing: serverless services charge by request count and resources used. That does not mean cloud native is always cheaper. Container clusters and many microservices carry their own operational cost. Cloud-based hosting is often described as pay-as-you-go. With differences in architecture, deployment automation, flexibility, and resource use, there is no guarantee that one approach is always cheaper than the other.

Run the serverless math. One million requests at $0.20 per million cost $0.20, plus a little for time and memory used. A small always-on server for the same month might cost $20 whether requests come or not. Quiet workloads favor per-request billing; hot steady workloads can favor reserved boxes. The shape of traffic picks the winner.

2.7.4 The stack from infrastructure to DevOps

The layered view puts cloud services at the bottom: compute and storage from the cloud. Above that sit containers that package applications with their dependencies. Above that sit microservices that organize the code into smaller capabilities. At the top sits DevOps with release automation for development and testing. An application is developed on its own, packed as a container, run on cloud infrastructure, and shipped through an automated pipeline. That layering shows how the parts relate.

The layered stack: cloud services at the bottom, containers above them, microservices above those, DevOps automation at the top. Each layer uses the one below and serves the one above.

Read it bottom-up like floors of a building. The ground floor (compute and storage) holds weight. The next floor (containers) boxes goods. The next (services) staffs counters. The top (DevOps) runs the conveyor between floors. A crack in a lower floor shakes everything above: strong base, steady top.

Recap: expect failure, pack once, automate shipping, pay for use, stack the layers. Next, the companies and tools that prove this shape at world scale.

Grids, kitchens, and buildings repeat one lesson: split it, box it, automate it, and the city keeps its lights on.

2.9.1 Flipkart Big Billion Day failure

The case centers on a sale launched on 6 October that opened at midnight with discounts across more than 70 categories, flash sales, and promotions. Ordinary traffic is fairly predictable. A major sale is not: millions of shoppers open product pages, check availability, add items, and head to checkout at the same time. That creates a flood of requests to back-end services. Product browsing leads to inventory, inventory leads to orders, and orders lead to payment, so all of these must stay consistent.

Failure chain: browse floods inventory, inventory races the database, carts scatter across servers, payments complete without orders. Each link passed its trouble to the next.

Large events like this need capacity planning, performance testing, and resilient architecture. In this event the site struggled under peak traffic. Products disappeared from carts, some items appeared sold out even after recovery, money was deducted in some cases without completed orders, and complaints followed.

Four linked problems explain the breakdown. The first was too little capacity. A tier sized for 10,000 concurrent requests collapses when 100,000 arrive together, so the server overloads. The fix is to add application instances and spread requests with a load balancer. The second was database bottlenecks. Thousands of shoppers may buy the same product at once, so the inventory database must handle simultaneous reads and writes. Without coordinated updates, the app shows wrong stock — for example still showing stock left after the item is gone. The fix includes transaction updates, concurrency control, and inventory reservation. The third was cart inconsistency. If cart data lives only in the memory of one application server, it vanishes when that server fails. Carts must preserve sessions across multiple instances. The fourth was payment and distributed-transaction trouble. A shopper pays, the payment provider processes the charge, then the order service is unavailable, so the customer sees no order. Safeguards include idempotency keys so a repeated operation does not cause duplicate effects or double charges, durable event queues, and compensating transactions. The lesson is direct: scaling alone is not enough without reliable coordination between services and databases.

Worked example — four breakdowns with numbers: start with capacity. A tier sized for 10,000 concurrent requests faces 100,000 — a 10-times overload. Ten copies where one hundred are needed means each copy takes ten times its share and falls. Next the database: 5,000 shoppers race for 500 phones. Without coordinated updates, all 5,000 see "in stock" and 4,500 orders fail after payment. With inventory reservation, the first 500 lock units and the rest see "sold out" honestly. Next the cart: one server holding 2,000 carts crashes, and 2,000 carts vanish. Shared session storage keeps them alive on any copy. Next payment: a Rs 2,000 charge succeeds but the order write fails. Without an idempotency key, the retry charges Rs 4,000 for zero orders; with one, the retry completes the same order once. Sense-check: every fix removes one multiplier — copies divide load, locks serialize stock, shared carts survive crashes, keys collapse retries into one charge.

Scope: this diagnosis fits flash-sale peaks on coupled commerce stacks. Assumption: traffic spikes far past the sized tier and duties share state. A steady low-traffic shop fails differently — usually on code bugs, not crowd math.

2.9.2 Hotstar peak and the practices that held

The contrast is a sports-driven peak with about 25 million concurrent users. The team predicted traffic, ran stress tests, watched workload signals, and grew the fleet in parallel — for example running on 100 nodes, adding about 100 more for the peak, then shrinking when load fell. Student suggestions during the discussion named the same ideas: prediction, stress testing, extra compute, parallel nodes, doubled capacity, auto-scaling, monitoring, and observability.

Worked example — Hotstar peak absorbed: six practices carried the load. A content delivery network (CDN) (servers placed closer to viewers holding copies of popular content) places content on servers closer to viewers instead of serving everything from one central site, which cuts load on the origin. Load balancing spreads incoming requests across many application instances. Auto-scaling raises capacity when traffic rises, for example during a major match. Caching keeps frequently requested data ready so repeated database work drops. Monitoring tracks latency, error rate, CPU use, and throughput. Adaptive bitrate streaming (video quality that follows each viewer's connection) matches video quality to each viewer's connection: a fast connection gets high-resolution video while a slow connection gets a lower bit rate, which cuts buffering. These are general large-scale streaming techniques, applied before the peak so the surge did not repeat the earlier failure.

Run the fleet math. Base runs on 100 nodes. The team adds about 100 more for the peak, then shrinks when load falls. Peak fleet is 200 nodes for 25 million viewers — about 125,000 viewers per node. Prediction sized it, stress tests proved it, auto-scaling held it, and the shrink afterward stopped the bill from bleeding.

Q: What steps let Hotstar handle about 25 million concurrent users without falling into the same trouble? A: They understood the architectural challenge early and prepared. They used a CDN to distribute content through servers placed closer to viewers, added load balancers to spread requests across instances, used auto-scaling to grow capacity for sports peaks, added caching to reduce repeated database work, and monitored latency, error rate, CPU use, and throughput. They also used adaptive bitrate streaming so quality follows each viewer's connection — a fast connection gets high-resolution video while a slow one gets a lower bit rate, which cuts buffering. Prediction plus stress testing plus these six practices let the peak pass without the same collapse.

Pitfalls: three traps undo good peak plans. First, testing with yesterday's traffic and calling it a stress test. Second, caching pages but not inventory counts, so stock still lies. Third, watching CPU while ignoring latency and error rate — the servers look fine while users fail.

Recap: one night broke on capacity, coordination, carts, and payments; the next held on prediction, edges, balance, scale, cache, watch, and adapt. Next, the monolith that makes the first story possible.

Bridges and toll gates said it first: count the crowd, open lanes early, post spotters, keep a fast lane ready.

2.10 Monolithic Architecture and Its Limits

A monolith packages and deploys the application as one unit. Modules may look separate in code, but they call each other directly inside the same deployment and ship together. If one module changes, the team rebuilds and redeploys the whole thing. Early teams often liked this because it was easy to build, test, and deploy, until the codebase grew large and tightly coupled.

Hook: one typo fix in the delivery message — and the whole food app, payments and all, must rebuild and redeploy. Why does the smallest change cost the biggest ship?

Think of a single-volume encyclopedia. Every topic lives in one binding; fixing one page means reprinting the volume. Early on that is simple — one book, one shelf. At 5,000 pages, one typo reprints a brick. Splitting into volumes costs shelves but saves reprints.

Worked example — food delivery monolith: Real-world: Swiggy and Zomato style food delivery. Business duties include customer management, restaurant management, menu browsing, order placement, payment processing, and delivery tracking. In a monolith all of these belong to one deployable system. A customer places an order, the app checks the restaurant menu, creates the order, processes payment, and updates delivery information. The duties are logically distinct but run inside the same deployment. Even a small bug fix can force a full rebuild, full test pass, and full redeploy — six duties, one ship, every time.

2.10.1 Food delivery monolith

Real-world: Swiggy and Zomato style food delivery. Business duties include customer management, restaurant management, menu browsing, order placement, payment processing, and delivery tracking. In a monolith all of these belong to one deployable system. A customer places an order, the app checks the restaurant menu, creates the order, processes payment, and updates delivery information. The duties are logically distinct but run inside the same deployment. Even a small bug fix can force a full rebuild, full test pass, and full redeploy.

Trace one order through the box. The request enters, checks the menu in-process, creates the order in-process, charges payment in-process, and writes delivery status in-process. No network hops, no version skew — fast and simple. The price arrives at release time: a one-line delivery-text fix rebuilds all six duties, reruns all tests, and redeploys the whole box. One line changed, six duties shipped.

2.10.2 Four limits of the monolith

The first limit is a technology barrier. An app built in Java is hard to extend with Python machine-learning code because runtimes and environments are tightly tied together. Microservices ease this by letting each service use its own stack. The second is scalability. Search may carry far more traffic than user registration, yet a monolith scales as a whole, so extra copies waste resources on quiet parts. The third is complexity. Thousands of classes, functions, and dependencies make it hard to see how one change affects another model. The fourth is maintenance. A new developer must learn a large part of the system, and a change to shared code can break unrelated behavior.

Four limits: technology barrier (one stack for all), whole-unit scaling (quiet parts copy too), complexity (thousands of tangled paths), maintenance (shared code breaks strangers). Each grows with the codebase.

Put numbers on the scaling waste. Search takes 80 percent of traffic; registration takes 5 percent. Copying the whole box 5 times gives search 5 copies and registration 5 copies — but registration needed 1. Four registration copies idle while paid for. Targeted scaling would run 5 search copies and 1 registration copy instead.

Scope: these limits bite large, long-lived codebases with uneven traffic. Assumption: duties share one runtime and one release. A small shop with flat traffic and one team may never feel them — and should not split early.

2.10.3 One pipeline and monolithic hell

Monolith delivery uses one build and one release pipeline. If one module has a critical bug, the whole release waits until that bug is fixed. With five teams feeding restaurant work, payments, orders, and delivery into one shared build, every change must integrate and pass all tests together. A Jenkins CI backlog with one pipeline shows the shape: one failed test can block release. That operational pain under tight coupling is sometimes called monolithic hell. One large codebase creates communication and coordination overhead and is hard to maintain.

Monolithic hell: one shared pipeline where every team queues, one failed test blocks all five teams, and coordination overhead grows with each added developer. The queue, not the code, sets the pace.

Count the queue. Five teams each push twice a day: 10 builds a day through one lane. One red test at 11 a.m. blocks the next 6 builds behind it. Six teams idle, one fix urgent, tempers short. Separate lanes would hold the blast to one team: one red lane versus five frozen teams.

Pitfalls: two mistakes start the descent. First, splitting code into modules but shipping one box and calling it done — modules without separate release are decoration. Second, letting shared database tables couple everything the code split apart.

Recap: one box, fast start, then four limits and one frozen queue. Next, the split that trades the queue for network calls.

Encyclopedias, kitchens, and queues agree: one binding is simple until the book becomes a brick.

2.11 Microservices Architecture and Service Design

A microservice architecture organizes work as small, independently manageable units split by business capability. Four traits define it. Autonomous means each service controls its own implementation and operation. Loosely coupled means services talk through well-defined interfaces with few dependencies. Independently deployable means a service can ship without the whole application. Business-domain aligned means each service maps to one business capability and follows the single responsibility principle: group things that change for the same reason and separate restaurant rules from payment rules.

Hook: the delivery-text typo returns — this time only the notification service rebuilds while orders and payments keep running. What changed?

Think of a food court. Separate stalls for dosa, juice, and dessert each hold their own ingredients, menu, and till. The dosa stall changes its menu without asking the juice stall. One shared doorway guides visitors. Stalls are services; the doorway is the gateway; menus are contracts.

Microservice: one business duty per service with four traits — autonomous (owns its code and runs), loosely coupled (talks through interfaces), independently deployable (ships alone), business-domain aligned (one duty, one reason to change).

2.11.1 Why microservices help

Microservices help when teams need independent scaling, faster releases, and fault isolation. Independent scaling means restaurant search can grow alone when it carries the most load. Faster releases mean a new recommendation algorithm can ship without waiting on other parts. Fault isolation means the system keeps serving when the recommendation service fails. Microservices are not free: network talk, data-consistency work, more infrastructure, and operational complexity all rise. They pay off when independent development and scaling matter more than the cost of distribution.

Score the trade before splitting. Gains: scale hot duties alone, ship one team without waiting, survive one failure. Costs: every in-process call becomes a network call, shared transactions become coordination work, five boxes need watching instead of one. Split when gains from independence beat the cost of distribution.

Scope: the payoff needs uneven traffic or many teams. Assumption: boundaries follow business duties that change for different reasons. Split a tiny shop with flat traffic and the network tax buys nothing.

2.11.2 Request flow and service decomposition

Traffic enters through an API gateway, a common entry point that routes each incoming request to the right service. Behind it sit back-end services reached through APIs or async messaging, often five or so in a teaching sketch. Each service may keep its own database or data model, so capabilities stay managed alone. Real-world: food delivery split into customer, restaurant, kitchen, order, and payment services.

API-first design means interfaces are defined before or alongside internals. An order service might expose GET orders/123 to fetch one order and POST order to create one. Other teams build against those contracts without knowing internals, which cuts integration doubt and lets teams move alone.

Worked example — API-first order contracts: the order service exposes GET orders/123 to fetch one order and POST order to create one. The menu team calls GET orders/123 and receives the order with items, status, and total — one call, one order back. The checkout team calls POST order with items and address and receives a new order id — one call, one id back. Both teams code against these two contracts without reading order internals, so the order team can rebuild its storage while callers keep working.

Trace a food order end to end. The phone app calls the gateway with POST order. The gateway routes to the order service. The order service checks restaurant hours, then calls payment. Payment approves. The order service writes the order to its own store and returns an id through the gateway. Four hops, each owned: gateway, order, payment, store.

2.11.3 Pipelines per service and market context

Each microservice gets its own pipeline with its own build, test, and deployment. A problem in one pipeline does not stop other services from shipping. That raises deployment frequency, shrinks scope, and makes failures easier to isolate. Contract and integration tests plus separate pipelines reduce tight coupling. A restaurant team, for example, can build, test, and deploy a restaurant change without redeploying orders or delivery.

Replay the five-team math with lanes. Five teams push twice a day through five pipelines: each lane holds 2 builds a day. Team C breaks its lane; four lanes keep shipping. Blocked builds drop from 6 to 0 for others. Frequency rises because one red lane blocks one team, not five.

Market pressure pushed the same way. Organizations wanted faster feature releases, support for changing traffic patterns, and fewer dependencies between teams. Cloud infrastructure made provisioning easier. Either shape can run anywhere: microservices can run on-premises and a monolith can run in the cloud. The choice follows needs, not fashion.

Pitfalls: independence needs agreed contracts or lanes diverge. Skipped contract tests let two green pipelines produce one broken system. Version the interfaces and test across them.

Recap: four traits, one gateway, contracts first, lanes per service — independence with agreements. Next, the bill that warns against splitting everything.

Food courts, doorways, and menus keep the lesson: separate stalls, one door, posted menus, no surprises.

2.12 Costs of Microservices and When Monolith Wins

Microservices tax every hop: data must travel between services, serialization adds work, infrastructure grows, and operations get heavier. A video-monitoring workload at Amazon Prime Video showed the other side. The team moved that workload from distributed microservices to a more consolidated single-process shape hosted with ECS for containers. Operational cost fell by about 90 percent because unnecessary distributed talk went away.

Hook: the same team that split everything later glued one workload back together — and the bill fell 90 percent. When does un-splitting win?

Think of office chatter. Five desks in one room settle a question with a glance. Five offices across town need calls, notes, and couriers per question. Distribution buys quiet focus but taxes every exchange. When the work is one constant debate, the single room wins.

Distribution tax: every hop pays network time, serialization work, extra boxes, and watching effort. Consolidation removes the tax where duties chat constantly. The Prime Video move paid off because the chatter never stopped.

When components run together and exchange data directly instead of making remote calls, data transfer drops and both performance and cost can improve. The takeaway is practical: pick the architecture that fits the requirements. A monolith can be simpler, faster, and cheaper for the right workload, while a distributed microservice design fits work that truly needs independent growth.

Worked example — Prime Video 90 percent saving: the video-monitoring workload ran as distributed microservices passing video frames between boxes. Suppose the monthly bill was $100,000, mostly frame shipping and orchestration. The team consolidated the chatty steps into one process where frames pass in memory. The new bill is about $10,000 — a $90,000 drop, about 90 percent. The math: in-memory handoff costs near zero per frame while network handoff costs per byte shipped. Sense-check: savings this large signal the split was the bottleneck, not the code.

Five clues point to the single room. Traffic is steady, not spiky. Duties change together for the same reason. Data hops constantly between steps. One small team owns it all. The bill grows from chatter, not users. Three or more true means consolidate first: chatty, coupled, calm — keep it together.

Scope: this correction fits chatty, coupled, steady workloads. Assumption: the workload was measured before and after. Consolidation without numbers is nostalgia; the 90 percent figure came from a real bill, not a feeling.

Exam note: expect a scenario that asks whether to stay monolithic or split, with cost, traffic shape, and team independence as the deciding clues — steady chatty work on one team points to the monolith, spiky uneven work on many teams points to services.

Recap: every hop taxes; measure the chatter before splitting. Next, the model that removes servers from your side of the bill entirely.

Desks, rooms, and couriers settle it: count the messages before choosing the floor plan.

2.13 Serverless Computing and Function as a Service

Serverless computing is a model where developers run application code without managing servers directly. Servers still exist; the cloud provider handles provisioning and scaling. Instead of keeping a virtual machine warm for image processing, a developer writes a function that runs when an event arrives, such as a new upload.

Hook: a photo lands in storage at 3 a.m., code wakes, resizes it, files the result — and nobody paid for a server idling since midnight. Where did the server go?

Think of a hotel kitchen that cooks only on order. No chef idles all night; the bell rings, the dish cooks, the bill counts that dish alone. The kitchen still exists — you just never rent it by the night. Events are the bell; functions are the dish; the meter runs per plate.

Serverless: code runs on provider-managed servers that wake per event and bill per use. No idle boxes on your bill. Servers still exist — the provider handles provisioning and scaling, and stateless functions carry the logic.

2.13.1 Core flow from gateway to function to data

An application can be built from managed services instead of a self-run application server. A client sends a request to an HTTP gateway. The gateway routes it to a function such as a Lambda function. The function queries the database and returns a response. The developer focuses on application logic while the provider runs the infrastructure. Real-world: Azure Functions, Google Cloud Functions, and AWS Lambda.

A common document flow shows the idea: a user uploads a document, it lands in an S3 bucket, the upload event triggers AWS Lambda, Lambda processes the document, and metadata lands in a store such as DynamoDB. No running virtual machine is kept just for that processing step.

Worked example — upload to metadata: a user uploads a 2-megabyte document. It lands in an S3 bucket. The upload event triggers AWS Lambda. Lambda reads the file, extracts title, size, and date, and writes one metadata row to DynamoDB. Total always-on boxes: zero. The bill counts one run of a few seconds plus one small write — idle hours cost nothing because no box waited.

2.13.2 The three-part serverless stack

A serverless web stack has three parts. Compute runs application logic, for example AWS Lambda or Fargate. A data store holds and returns data, for example DynamoDB, S3, or Aurora Serverless. A gateway receives requests and routes them, for example API Gateway. The client calls the API gateway, the gateway calls the Lambda function, and Lambda talks to the database. Lambda runs the business logic, the store handles persistence, and the gateway handles routing. In the profile example, Lambda fetches the profile and returns it through the gateway.

Worked example — profile fetch: the app calls GET profiles/42 at the gateway. The gateway calls the Lambda function with id 42. Lambda reads row 42 from the database and returns name, photo, and plan. The gateway passes it back. Four stops, one job each: gateway routes, Lambda thinks, store remembers, gateway returns.

Trace the timing. Gateway routing takes about 10 milliseconds. Lambda runs about 60 milliseconds. The database read takes about 15 milliseconds. Total is roughly 85 milliseconds with no server to tend. Cold starts add extra on the first call after idle — the known price of scale-to-zero.

2.13.3 Writing and triggering functions

A function is ordinary code for one action: resize an image, send an e-mail, handle an upload. The developer writes it in a language such as Java or Python, deploys it to Lambda, and lets events trigger it. Triggers include HTTP requests, file uploads, and queue messages. When the event arrives, Lambda runs the function and processes what came in.

Function: ordinary code for one action, deployed to the provider and fired by events. HTTP calls, uploads, and queue messages are the three usual bells.

Q: What actually lives inside that Lambda function — is it a feature, an action, or a replacement for a microservice? A: It is the application logic for one action, written like a normal function. A team might write a function that sends an e-mail or processes an upload in Java or Python, deploy it to AWS Lambda, and attach triggers such as an HTTP request, a file upload, or a queue message. When the trigger fires, Lambda runs the function and the function processes the event — one bell, one dish, no idle kitchen.

Q: Can that function keep state and change resources, or is it stateless? A: It is stateless by design and does not depend on data held inside that one execution. One request might store an order while another fetches it, but memory is not shared that way. Shared state lives outside in storage services such as S3, RDS, or DynamoDB, which many invocations can reach. Each request is processed alone against durable external storage — the function thinks, the store remembers.

Q: Is there a simple demo that shows this working end to end? A: A live walkthrough was deferred to a later webinar session, where setup and triggers will be shown in detail. The flow to expect there: upload lands, function fires, metadata appears in the store.

Scope: functions fit short event-driven actions. Assumption: runs finish quickly and state lives outside. Long always-busy work or chatty steps that call each other in loops fit boxes or consolidated processes better.

Pitfalls: two traps catch newcomers. First, hiding state inside the function and wondering why the next call forgets it — memory does not carry over. Second, chaining functions into long call loops and paying per hop for work one box did in memory.
