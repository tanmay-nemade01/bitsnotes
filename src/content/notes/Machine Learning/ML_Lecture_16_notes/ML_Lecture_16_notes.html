Gaussian Mixture Models and Support Vector Machines

16.1 Context — From K-Means to Gaussian Mixture Models

Hook: You run K-means on your data and get perfect spherical clusters. Then you plot the points — they form cigar-shaped clouds. K-means splits them in half, right through the middle. Why does a perfectly good algorithm fail so badly on real data?

16.1.1 Recap of K-Means Clustering

K-means is an unsupervised learning algorithm — it finds structure in data without labels. It allocates every data point to exactly one cluster. At any given moment, each point belongs to one cluster and one cluster only. This is called hard clustering.

Intuition — The "Post Office" Analogy: Imagine sorting letters into K post boxes. Every letter goes into exactly one box — the one whose slot is closest. A letter near the edge of two slots still goes into only one. But what if a letter is about both sports AND finance? Hard assignment forces a choice. K-means works this way — every point gets one box, no ambiguity.

Where the analogy breaks: Real data often has overlapping categories. A movie can be both comedy and romance. K-means forces it to pick one.

K-means core mechanism: It places K centroids (cluster centers), assigns each point to the nearest centroid, recomputes centroids as the mean of assigned points, and repeats. The objective is to minimize the sum of squared distances from points to their assigned centroids:

\[J = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \sum_{k=1}^{K} r_{nk} \| \mathbf{x}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k \|^2\]

where \(r_{nk} = 1\) if point \(n\) is assigned to cluster \(k\), and \(0\) otherwise. Each \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k\) (mu-sub-k) is the centroid of cluster \(k\).

K-means assumes clusters are spherical (circular in 2D) — it measures only distance from the center, ignoring direction. It assumes equal variance (spread) in all directions. This is a hard limitation: elongated or elliptical clusters get split incorrectly.

Pitfall — Duplicate Records: Before clustering, remove duplicate data points. If the same record appears 100 times among 100 unique records, the mean and summary statistics get distorted by the repeated values. The algorithm will "see" 101 records but only 2 unique individuals, biasing all downstream computations.

16.1.2 Introducing Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM)

A Gaussian Mixture Model is a probabilistic model for clustering. Instead of assigning points by distance to a center, GMM asks a deeper question: "What if the data was generated by mixing together several bell curves?"

Intuition — The "Reverse Engineer" Analogy: You receive a smoothie made by blending unknown numbers of strawberries, bananas, and blueberries. You cannot unmix it, but you can guess the ingredients by taste. GMM does the same: given the final dataset, it reverse-engineers which Gaussian "ingredients" were blended together, in what proportions, and how wide each bell curve was.

Where the analogy breaks: The smoothie is physically blended; GMM data points are individual draws from the mixture — you can still see each point.

GMM assumes all data points are generated from a mixture of \(K\) Gaussian distributions with unknown parameters. If the data has three distinct groups, GMM posits three separate Gaussians underneath:

\[p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \cdot \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)\]

Each component \(k\) has three unknowns: its mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k\) (center), its covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\) (shape/spread), and its mixing weight \(\pi_k\) (how much of the data it generates).

Previous clustering models treat data as coming from one group. Real data often comes from different underlying processes mixed together.

Real-World Example — Heights of Adults: Collect the height of every adult in a city. Plot the heights — the data won't fit a single bell curve. Men's heights follow one Gaussian (mean ~175 cm). Women's heights follow another (mean ~162 cm). The two curves overlap. A person who is 170 cm tall — tall woman or average-height man? GMM models this overlapping, multi-source data naturally by assigning probabilities to each underlying group.

Sense-check: If you fit one Gaussian, you get a single wide bump. With two Gaussians, you recover the two distinct peaks — one for each gender — matching what biology tells us.

16.1.3 Why Not a Single Gaussian?

Scope — When a single Gaussian fails: A single Gaussian can only produce one symmetric bump. If your data has:

  • Multiple separated groups (clusters)
  • Asymmetric shapes
  • Different spreads in different regions

...then one Gaussian gives a terrible fit — large empty spaces between groups are "explained" by stretching the bell curve unnaturally wide. Multiple Gaussians are needed to capture the true structure.

Visual intuition: Picture a histogram of city heights. Instead of one smooth bell, you see two bumps side by side — a bimodal distribution. A single Gaussian would sit awkwardly in the valley between the two peaks, fitting neither group well. Two Gaussians — one centered on each bump — fit perfectly.

Recap: K-means assigns hard labels by distance. GMM asks a richer question — "Which bell curve probably generated this point?" — and gives soft, probabilistic answers. This shift from distance to probability is the foundation of everything that follows.

Real-world connection: GMMs are used in speaker diarization (who spoke when in a recording), customer segmentation (finding natural shopper groups from purchase patterns), and anomaly detection (points with very low probability under the mixture are flagged as unusual). In bioinformatics, GMMs model gene expression data where different cell types produce different expression profiles.

16.2 Gaussian Mixture Model — Core Concepts

Hook: K-means says you belong to cluster A — end of story. But what about a customer who buys both luxury watches AND discount groceries? Forcing them into one box distorts reality. GMM lets a point be "85% luxury shopper, 15% bargain hunter." How does a model express partial belonging?

16.2.1 Hard Clustering vs. Soft Clustering

Hard clustering (K-means): A point belongs to cluster A OR cluster B — definitively. At any moment, exactly one cluster owns each point.

Soft clustering (GMM): A point belongs to multiple clusters with different strengths, expressed as probabilities that sum to 1:

\[\text{Point } i: \quad (r_{i1}, r_{i2}, \ldots, r_{iK}), \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{K} r_{ik} = 1\]

where \(r_{ik}\) (read "r-i-k") is the responsibility — the probability that cluster \(k\) generated point \(i\).

Intuition — The "Citizenship" Analogy: Hard clustering is like holding one passport — you are a citizen of exactly one country. Soft clustering is like ancestry: a person might be 60% European, 30% Asian, 10% African. You do not lose the 30% or 10% — they all count. A point near a cluster boundary is like a person with mixed heritage — belonging to multiple groups at once.

Where the analogy breaks: Citizenship is legal and binary; GMM probabilities are statistical estimates that change as the model learns.

Soft clustering shines for data points in overlapping regions — the boundary cases where hard assignment would be arbitrary. Even a point deep inside cluster A gets a small probability (e.g., 1%) of belonging to cluster B, because the Gaussian tail extends infinitely in all directions.

Concrete example: A data point sits midway between two clusters. GMM says: 50% cluster 1, 50% cluster 2. K-means arbitrarily assigns it to whichever centroid is 0.001 units closer. GMM honestly reports "I cannot decide." This uncertainty is valuable information, not a weakness.

Sense-check: If a point is 50-50 between two clusters, its distance to both centroids is nearly equal. GMM correctly reflects this ambiguity; K-means hides it.

16.2.2 Flexibility in Cluster Shape

K-means uses only the mean (center) — it assumes spherical clusters with equal spread in all directions. GMM uses three pieces of information per cluster:

  • Mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k\) — where the cluster is centered
  • Variance \(\sigma_k^2\) (1D) — how spread out the cluster is along each axis. A tall, thin bell curve has small variance; a short, wide bell curve has large variance.
  • Covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\) (multivariate) — how variables move together. This determines the shape and orientation of the Gaussian: spherical, elliptical, or rotated.

Intuition — The "Play-Doh" Analogy: Think of each Gaussian as a blob of Play-Doh. The mean is where you place the blob on the table. Variance is how hard you press down — high variance flattens it wide. Covariance is how you stretch the blob in a diagonal direction — it turns a round blob into an oval. K-means can only place round blobs; GMM can stretch, rotate, and flatten each blob independently.

Where the analogy breaks: Play-Doh has edges; a Gaussian extends infinitely in all directions, just with vanishingly small probability far from the mean.

GMM allows elliptical clusters — the covariance structure determines whether a cluster is spherical (same variance in all directions), axis-aligned elliptical (different variances per dimension but no rotation), or fully rotated elliptical (off-diagonal covariance).

16.2.3 The Core Idea — Data as a Mixture of Gaussians

GMM models the data as a weighted sum of \(K\) bell curves. Formally:

\[p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \cdot \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)\]

Three sets of unknowns, one per component \(k\):

  • \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k\) (mu-sub-k): the mean vector — center of the \(k\)-th bell curve
  • \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\) (Sigma-sub-k): the covariance matrix — shape and spread of the \(k\)-th bell curve
  • \(\pi_k\) (pi-sub-k): the mixing coefficient — what fraction of all data comes from component \(k\); \(\sum_k \pi_k = 1\), \(\pi_k \ge 0\)

Scope — What GMM assumes: (1) The number of components \(K\) is known in advance. (2) Each component is Gaussian — if the true clusters are not bell-shaped, GMM fits poorly. (3) Data points are independent — no time series or spatial correlation. (4) The mixing weights \(\pi_k\) are constant across the dataset. If these assumptions fail, GMM can produce misleading clusters.

Common trap: GMM will happily fit \(K\) Gaussians to ANY data, even pure noise. Always check if the resulting clusters are meaningful, not just whether the algorithm converged.

Visual intuition: Imagine a 2D scatter plot with two elongated, diagonal clusters that cross each other like an X. K-means would split this into two round blobs, cutting each diagonal in half. GMM with full covariance matrices would correctly identify the two diagonal structures, each as its own Gaussian with a tilted, elliptical shape.

Recap: GMM upgrades clustering from "which center is closest?" to "which bell curve probably made this point?" This soft, probabilistic view — combined with flexible elliptical shapes via covariance — makes GMM vastly more expressive than K-means. Next we see the algorithm that learns these Gaussians from data: Expectation-Maximization.

Real-world connection: GMM's soft assignments are critical in medical diagnosis — a patient's symptoms might suggest multiple conditions with different probabilities. In computer vision, GMMs model background subtraction: each pixel's color over time is modeled as a mixture; a new color with low probability under the mixture is flagged as a moving foreground object.

16.3 The GMM Algorithm — Expectation-Maximization

Hook: You have a bag of red and blue marbles from two unknown urns. You cannot see which urn each marble came from. How do you figure out the proportion of colors in each urn? You guess, then check, then guess again — each time getting closer to the truth. This is the EM algorithm.

16.3.1 Algorithm Overview (Conceptual)

Purpose: The Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm finds the maximum likelihood estimates of GMM parameters when cluster assignments are unknown (hidden/latent). It solves a chicken-and-egg problem: if you knew the clusters, you could compute the Gaussians; if you knew the Gaussians, you could assign clusters. EM breaks this deadlock by iterating between the two.

16.3.2 Visual Analogy — The "Coloring" Process

Intuition — The "Coloring Book" Analogy: Imagine a black-and-white outline drawing. At first, you randomly splash blue and green paint near where you think the clusters are. Then:

  • E-step (Color the points): Look at each point. If it is near the blue splash, color it mostly blue. If near green, color it mostly green. Points in between get mixed blue-green.
  • M-step (Move the paints): The blue paint moves to the average location of all blue-colored points. The green paint moves to the average of green-colored points. The paint blobs also reshape to match the spread of their colored points.

Repeat. Each cycle the paints center better over their true clusters. The process stops when the paints stop moving.

Where the analogy breaks: Real paint doesn't have probabilities — each point gets a percentage of each color. Also, Gaussians extend infinitely; paint blobs have edges.

16.3.3 Formal Algorithm Steps

Step 1 — Initialization: Choose initial values for all \(K\) Gaussians:

  • Mean \(\mu_k\) for each Gaussian (in 1D; \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k\) in multivariate)
  • Variance \(\sigma_k^2\) for each (in 1D; covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\) in multivariate)
  • Mixing coefficient \(\pi_k\) — typically \(\pi_k = 1/K\) (equal start)

Step 2 — E-step (Expectation): Compute responsibilities

For every data point \(i\) and every cluster \(k\), compute the responsibility \(r_{ik}\) — the probability that point \(i\) belongs to cluster \(k\):

\[r_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k \cdot \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j \cdot \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_j, \sigma_j^2)}\]

Why this formula? The numerator \(\pi_k \cdot \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)\) is the "score" for cluster \(k\) — how much this Gaussian contributes at point \(x_i\), weighted by the cluster's importance \(\pi_k\). The denominator sums scores across all clusters, normalizing them so \(r_{i1} + r_{i2} + \cdots + r_{iK} = 1\). This is Bayes' rule in disguise: posterior probability of cluster given data point.

Step 3 — M-step (Maximization): Update parameters

Using the responsibilities from the E-step, recalculate each Gaussian:

Updated mean (weighted average, where weights are responsibilities):

\[\mu_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} r_{ik} \cdot x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} r_{ik}}\]

Updated variance (weighted spread around the new mean):

\[\sigma_k^{2,\text{new}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} r_{ik} \cdot (x_i - \mu_k^{\text{new}})^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} r_{ik}}\]

Updated mixing coefficient (average responsibility for this cluster):

\[\pi_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} r_{ik}\]

Why these formulas? The mean update is a responsibility-weighted average — points with high \(r_{ik}\) pull the mean toward them. The variance update is a responsibility-weighted squared deviation. The mixing coefficient is simply "what fraction of total responsibility does this cluster claim?"

Step 4 — Check convergence: Stop when:

  • The change in all parameters (\(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2, \pi_k\)) between iterations is below a small threshold \(\varepsilon\), or
  • The cluster assignments (which cluster has the highest \(r_{ik}\) for each point) do not change between two consecutive iterations, or
  • The improvement in log-likelihood \(\log p(\mathbf{X} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\sigma}, \boldsymbol{\pi})\) drops below \(\varepsilon\).

Otherwise, return to Step 2.

Complexity & Cost: Each E-step computes \(N \times K\) Gaussian evaluations — \(O(NKd^2)\) for multivariate with full covariance. The M-step is \(O(NKd^2)\). EM typically converges in tens of iterations. For large \(d\), using diagonal covariance (\(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\) with zeros off-diagonal) dramatically reduces cost at the expense of cluster shape flexibility.

When to Use / Alternatives: Use GMM+EM when clusters overlap and you need uncertainty estimates. For well-separated spherical clusters, K-means is faster and simpler. For very large datasets, mini-batch K-means scales better. For non-Gaussian cluster shapes, consider DBSCAN or spectral clustering.

Recap: EM solves the "I don't know which points belong where" problem by iterating: guess assignments (E-step), update Gaussians (M-step), repeat until stable. Each cycle improves the fit. Next we build the mathematical foundation — the Gaussian PDF itself.

Real-world connection: EM is used far beyond GMM — it trains Hidden Markov Models for speech recognition, handles missing data in surveys (treat missing values as latent variables), and estimates parameters in medical imaging (PET scans, where the emission source locations are unknown). The EM pattern of "estimate the hidden, then maximize given the estimate" appears throughout machine learning.

16.4 GMM — Mathematical Foundation

Hook: A Gaussian is just a bell curve, right? But when you move from one dimension (height) to many dimensions (height, weight, age), the bell curve becomes a bell-shaped cloud. Its shape can stretch, tilt, and rotate. How do you write that mathematically?

16.4.1 1D Gaussian Probability Density Function

A Gaussian distribution (also called a normal distribution) in one dimension is defined by two numbers: the mean \(\mu\) (mu) — where the peak sits — and the variance \(\sigma^2\) (sigma-squared) — how wide the bell spreads:

\[\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\]

Breaking it down:

  • \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\) — the normalizing constant. It makes the total area under the curve equal 1. Without it, the curve would not be a valid probability distribution.
  • \(\exp(\cdot)\) — the exponential function \(e^{(\cdot)}\). It creates the bell shape: highest at the mean, dropping off symmetrically on both sides.
  • \(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\) — the squared distance from the mean, scaled by variance. When \(x = \mu\), this is 0, so \(e^0 = 1\) — the peak. As \(x\) moves away, the squared distance grows, the exponent becomes more negative, and the value drops toward zero.

Intuition — The "Dartboard" Analogy: You throw darts at a board, aiming for the bullseye (\(\mu\)). Most darts land near the center. Occasionally one goes wide. The variance \(\sigma^2\) measures your skill: small variance = tight grouping (pro darts player); large variance = scattered shots (beginner). The Gaussian PDF tells you how likely a dart is to land at any specific spot.

Where the analogy breaks: Real dart throws are bounded by the board; the Gaussian assigns non-zero probability to any distance, even a kilometer away.

Visual intuition: Picture a bell curve. The x-axis is the data value. The y-axis is probability density. The curve peaks at \(x = \mu\). The width at half the peak height is proportional to \(\sigma\). About 68% of the probability mass lies within \(\mu \pm \sigma\); about 95% within \(\mu \pm 2\sigma\).

16.4.2 Multivariate Gaussian Probability Density Function

For \(d\)-dimensional data, the Gaussian generalizes from a bell curve to a bell-shaped cloud. The mean becomes a vector \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) (d components). The variance becomes a covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) (d × d):

\[\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{1/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\right)\]

Breaking it down:

  • \(d\) — number of dimensions (features). In 2D, \(d = 2\) and \((2\pi)^{d/2} = 2\pi\).
  • \(|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|\) — the determinant of the covariance matrix. It measures the "volume" of the cloud. A larger determinant means a more spread-out cloud.
  • \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\) — the inverse of the covariance matrix. This appears in the exponent to measure distance in a "covariance-scaled" space (the Mahalanobis distance).
  • \((\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\) — the squared Mahalanobis distance: how far \(\mathbf{x}\) is from \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\), accounting for the shape and orientation of the cloud.

The covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\): For \(d = 2\):

\[\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 \\ \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 & \sigma_2^2 \end{bmatrix}\]

  • Diagonal entries \(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2\) — variances of each feature individually
  • Off-diagonal entry \(\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\) — the covariance between features 1 and 2; \(\rho\) (rho) is the correlation coefficient, ranging from \(-1\) to \(+1\)

When \(\rho = 0\) (no correlation): the Gaussian is axis-aligned (an ellipse whose axes are parallel to the coordinate axes). When \(\rho \neq 0\): the ellipse tilts.

Intuition — The "Stretchy Cloud" Analogy: A 1D Gaussian is a slice through a bell. A 2D Gaussian is a 3D bell-shaped mountain. Looking from above, you see contour lines — ellipses. If \(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\) and \(\rho = 0\), the contours are perfect circles. If \(\sigma_1^2 \neq \sigma_2^2\), they are axis-aligned ellipses. If \(\rho \neq 0\), the ellipses tilt diagonally — like a mountain whose ridge runs at an angle.

16.4.3 The GMM Probability Density Function

The full GMM probability density is the weighted sum (mixture) of \(K\) individual Gaussians:

\[p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \cdot \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)\]

with constraints: \(\pi_k \ge 0\) for all \(k\), and \(\sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k = 1\).

This is an additive mixture — each component contributes independently, scaled by its mixing weight \(\pi_k\). If cluster 1 contains twice as many points as cluster 2, then \(\pi_1\) will be about \(2/3\) and \(\pi_2\) about \(1/3\).

The \(\pi_k\) are prior probabilities — before seeing any data point, the chance that a random point comes from cluster \(k\) is \(\pi_k\).

Scope — Key assumptions of the GMM density: 1. Gaussianity: Each component is Gaussian. If the true data-generating process produces non-Gaussian clusters (e.g., uniform squares, exponential tails), GMM fits the wrong shape. 2. Known K: The number of components is fixed in advance. Using the wrong K leads to underfitting (too few components squash distinct clusters together) or overfitting (too many components split one cluster into meaningless fragments). 3. Independence: Points are drawn independently. Time-series or spatial data with correlation between points violates this assumption. 4. Sum-to-one: The mixing weights must sum to 1 — every point must come from somewhere in the mixture.

Real-world connection: The multivariate Gaussian underpins nearly all of classical statistics. In weather prediction, temperature and pressure at multiple locations are jointly modeled as a high-dimensional Gaussian. 16.5 GMM — Worked Example

Hook: Four numbers — 0, 1, 2, 3 — on a number line. You suspect they come from two groups. Can a computer figure out which points belong together without being told? Let's trace EM step by step on the smallest possible example.

16.5.1 Problem Setup

Data: Four points on a line: \(x_1 = 0, x_2 = 1, x_3 = 2, x_4 = 3\)

Task: Fit a mixture of \(K = 2\) Gaussian components. Find \(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2, \pi_1\) and \(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2, \pi_2\).

16.5.2 Step 1 — Initialization

Use domain knowledge to guess initial parameters. Points 0 and 1 look like one group (small numbers); points 2 and 3 look like another (larger numbers).

  • Component 1: \(\mu_1 = \frac{0+1}{2} = 0.5\), \(\sigma_1^2 = 1\), \(\pi_1 = 0.5\)
  • Component 2: \(\mu_2 = \frac{2+3}{2} = 2.5\), \(\sigma_2^2 = 1\), \(\pi_2 = 0.5\)

Variance is initialized to 1 as a reasonable starting spread. Mixing weights start equal — no prior reason to favor one cluster over the other.

In practice: Python's scikit-learn initializes GMM using K-means: run K-means first, use its centroids as \(\mu_k\), compute the covariance of points in each K-means cluster as \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\), and set \(\pi_k\) proportional to cluster sizes.

16.5.3 Step 2 — E-step: Compute Responsibilities

For point \(x_1 = 0\):

Likelihood under Component 1 (\(\mu_1 = 0.5, \sigma_1^2 = 1\)):

\[\mathcal{N}(0 \mid 0.5, 1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{(0-0.5)^2}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-0.125}\]

Likelihood under Component 2 (\(\mu_2 = 2.5, \sigma_2^2 = 1\)):

\[\mathcal{N}(0 \mid 2.5, 1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{(0-2.5)^2}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-3.125}\]

Compute responsibilities:

\[r_{11} = \frac{0.5 \cdot e^{-0.125}}{0.5 \cdot e^{-0.125} + 0.5 \cdot e^{-3.125}} = \frac{e^{-0.125}}{e^{-0.125} + e^{-3.125}} = \frac{0.8825}{0.8825 + 0.0439} \approx \mathbf{0.95}\]

\[r_{12} = 1 - 0.95 = \mathbf{0.05}\]

Interpretation: Point 0 is very close to \(\mu_1 = 0.5\) (distance 0.5) and far from \(\mu_2 = 2.5\) (distance 2.5). Component 1 claims it with 95% confidence.

For point \(x_2 = 1\):

\[\mathcal{N}(1 \mid 0.5, 1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-0.125}, \quad \mathcal{N}(1 \mid 2.5, 1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-1.125}\]

\[r_{21} = \frac{e^{-0.125}}{e^{-0.125} + e^{-1.125}} = \frac{0.8825}{0.8825 + 0.3247} \approx \mathbf{0.73}\]

\[r_{22} = 1 - 0.73 = \mathbf{0.27}\]

For point \(x_3 = 2\):

Pitfall — Local optima: EM converges to a local maximum of the likelihood, not necessarily the global maximum. With bad initialization (e.g., both means at 0), EM might settle into a poor solution. Always run multiple random initializations and pick the one with the highest log-likelihood.

Pitfall — Singularity: If a Gaussian component collapses onto a single data point, its variance can shrink to near zero, making the likelihood blow up to infinity. This is called a singularity. Using regularization (adding a small constant to the variance) or Bayesian GMM prevents this.

Pitfall — Identifiability: The component labels (1, 2, …, K) are arbitrary. Swapping \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\) gives the same mixture. Do not interpret "Component 1" as meaningful across different runs.

Recap: On the toy dataset {0, 1, 2, 3}, EM correctly recovers two clusters centered near 0.5 and 2.5 after just two iterations. The soft assignments capture the ambiguity at the boundary (points 1 and 2). Next we look at how to choose K, initialize properly, and decide when to stop.

Real-world connection: This 1D worked example scales directly to high dimensions. In customer analytics, GMM clusters shoppers based on dozens of features (age, income, purchase frequency, basket size). The soft assignments let a shopper be "60% luxury, 40% bargain" — which is far more actionable for marketing than a hard either/or label.

16.6 GMM — Practical Considerations

Hook: GMM will happily fit \(K\) Gaussians to any dataset you give it — even pure noise. Choose \(K = 10\) for 10 data points and each Gaussian will hug a single point, giving "perfect" fit and zero generalization. How do you pick the right \(K\)?

16.6.1 Choosing the Number of Components K

Choosing \(K\) is a model selection problem — balancing fit quality against model complexity. Too few components underfit (merge distinct clusters). Too many overfit (split one cluster into meaningless fragments).

Five common approaches:

1. Elbow Method: Run GMM for \(K = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\) and plot log-likelihood (how well the model fits). As \(K\) increases, fit always improves — but with diminishing returns. The "elbow" where improvement levels off is your \(K\).

2. Silhouette Analysis: For each point, compute: \[s = \frac{b - a}{\max(a, b)}\] where \(a\) = mean distance to points in own cluster, \(b\) = mean distance to points in nearest other cluster. Higher \(s\) (closer to 1) = better separated clusters. Pick \(K\) with highest average silhouette score.

3. Cross-Validation: Split data into train/test. Fit GMM on train data for each \(K\). Score on test data using log-likelihood. Pick \(K\) with best test score. This directly measures generalization.

4. Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC): \[\text{BIC} = -2 \log L + k \cdot \ln(n)\]

  • \(L\) = maximized likelihood (higher is better fit)
  • \(k\) = number of free parameters (for 1D GMM with \(K\) components: \(k = 3K - 1\); the \(-1\) is because \(\sum \pi_k = 1\))
  • \(n\) = number of data points

Lower BIC is better. The \(\ln(n)\) penalty grows with dataset size — BIC favors simpler models for larger datasets.

5. Akaike Information Criterion (AIC): \[\text{AIC} = -2 \log L + 2k\]

Lower AIC is better. AIC penalizes complexity less severely than BIC (penalty is \(2k\) vs. \(k \cdot \ln(n)\)). For \(n \ge 8\), BIC's penalty exceeds AIC's.

BIC vs. AIC — Which to use? For a dataset with \(n = 100\) and \(K = 3\) (1D, so \(k = 8\)):

  • BIC penalty: \(8 \cdot \ln(100) \approx 8 \cdot 4.61 = 36.9\)
  • AIC penalty: \(2 \cdot 8 = 16\)

BIC penalizes more heavily — it will pick smaller \(K\). AIC is more generous with complexity. BIC is consistent (picks the true \(K\) as \(n \to \infty\)); AIC is efficient for prediction.

Sense-check: If BIC and AIC agree on \(K\), you are confident. If they disagree, BIC is more conservative — prefer it when interpretability matters.

Q: Several students asked — "Why can't I just pick the \(K\) that gives the highest log-likelihood?"

A: Log-likelihood always increases as you add more components (more parameters = better fit). With \(K = N\) components, each can sit exactly on one data point with zero variance — infinite likelihood! But this model is useless — it has just memorized the data. BIC and AIC add a penalty that offsets the fit improvement, so you only add a component if it improves fit enough to justify the extra parameters. This is the bias-variance tradeoff in action.

16.6.2 Initialization Strategies

EM is sensitive to starting position — it converges to a local maximum near the initialization. Three strategies:

1. Random Initialization: Pick random values for \(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2, \pi_k\). Simple but unreliable — may land in a bad local optimum. Always run multiple random starts and keep the best.

2. Hierarchical Splitting: Start with \(K = 1\) (one Gaussian covering all data). Split the component with the largest variance. Repeat until you have \(K\) components. This is analogous to divisive hierarchical clustering.

3. K-means Initialization (Recommended):

  • Step 1: Run K-means to find \(K\) clusters
  • Step 2: Set \(\mu_k\) = K-means cluster centers
  • Step 3: Set \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\) = sample covariance of points in K-means cluster \(k\)
  • Step 4: Set \(\pi_k\) = (points in cluster \(k\)) / \(N\)

This is what scikit-learn's `GaussianMixture` does by default (`init_params='kmeans'`). It gives EM a warm start near a good solution.

Pitfall — Single random start: Running EM once with random initialization and accepting the result is a common beginner mistake. The likelihood landscape has many peaks. Always use `n_init` (multiple random starts) or K-means initialization in practice.

Pitfall — K vs. the data: If you initialize with K-means but the data has non-spherical clusters, K-means itself will give poor centroids, and EM may struggle to recover. For highly elongated clusters, consider using a dedicated initialization or multiple random starts.

16.6.3 Convergence Criteria

Stop EM when further iterations produce negligible change. Three equivalent criteria:

1. Parameter change: \(\max|\theta^{\text{new}} - \theta^{\text{old}}| < \varepsilon\), where \(\theta\) includes all \(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2, \pi_k\). Typical \(\varepsilon = 10^{-4}\).

2. Assignment stability: The hard clustering (argmax of responsibilities) is identical for two consecutive iterations.

3. Log-likelihood change: \(|\log L^{\text{new}} - \log L^{\text{old}}| < \varepsilon\). This is the most principled criterion — it directly measures whether the model is still improving.

In practice, all three criteria agree — when the log-likelihood stops improving, assignments and parameters also stabilize.

Recap: Picking \(K\) requires balancing fit and complexity (BIC/AIC). Initializing well (K-means) avoids bad local optima. Stopping when log-likelihood plateaus prevents wasted computation. With these practical tools, GMM is ready for real datasets. Now we shift gears to a completely different paradigm: Support Vector Machines.

Real-world connection: BIC and AIC are used across all of statistics, not just GMM. In genomics, researchers use BIC to choose the number of ancestral populations from genetic marker data. In astronomy, GMM with BIC selection classifies stars into types (main sequence, giant, white dwarf) based on brightness and color — where the number of distinct stellar populations is not known in advance.

16.7 Support Vector Machines — Core Concept

Hook: You can draw infinitely many lines that separate your +1 points from your −1 points. All of them get 100% accuracy on the training set. But when new data arrives, some lines fail catastrophically. How do you pick the one line that will still work tomorrow?

16.7.1 The Classification Problem

Consider predicting whether a student gets admission into a top institute. Two features: CGPA (x-axis) and entrance exam score (y-axis). Students who got in are labeled +1 (blue triangles). Students who did not are labeled −1 (red circles).

Given this labeled 2D data, the task is to draw a decision boundary — a line that separates the two classes.

Formal setup: We have \(N\) labeled training examples \((\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\) where \(\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\) (the features) and \(y_i \in \{+1, -1\}\) (the class label). In 2D (\(d=2\)), the decision boundary is a line. In 3D, a plane. In \(d \ge 4\) dimensions, a hyperplane.

16.7.2 Which Line Is Best?

Intuition — The "Three Candidates" Thought Experiment: Three lines L1, L2, L3 all perfectly separate the current data:

  • L1: Hugs the red (−1) points. One small nudge and new red points fall on the blue side.
  • L3: Hugs the blue (+1) points. New blue points get misclassified.
  • L2: Sits squarely in the middle. Gives breathing room on both sides.

L2 is the safest bet for unseen data. The principle: maximize the gap (margin) between the two classes.

16.7.3 Decision Boundary, Margin, and Support Vectors

Three key geometric objects in SVM:

  • Decision boundary: The middle line (or hyperplane). Points exactly on this line get a score of 0 — the model cannot decide their class.
  • Margin: The total distance between the two outer lines that just graze the nearest points of each class. Margin = \(2 \times\) (distance from decision boundary to nearest point on either side).
  • Support vectors: The data points that sit exactly on the margin lines. These are the hardest points to classify — the ones closest to the boundary. They are called support vectors because they alone "support" (define) where the boundary goes.

Critical property: All other data points — the ones comfortably inside their class region — are irrelevant to the decision boundary. Move them, add more of them, remove them — the boundary does not change. Only the support vectors matter.

Visual intuition: Picture a 2D scatter plot. The +1 points cluster in the upper-right; the −1 points cluster in the lower-left. Three parallel lines are drawn: one through the rightmost −1 points, one through the leftmost +1 points, and one exactly halfway between them. The middle line is the decision boundary. The outer two define the margin. The circled points touching the outer lines are the support vectors — maybe 3 to 5 points out of hundreds.

16.7.4 SVM as an Optimization Problem

Intuition — The "Namaste" Analogy (from the professor): You stand between two walls of people, palms together in a namaste. You spread your hands apart as wide as you can without touching anyone. The people your fingertips almost touch on each side — those are the support vectors. How far apart your hands get is the margin. SVM finds the hand position (decision boundary) that lets you spread widest.

Where the analogy breaks: The "walls" are lines of people; real data is scattered in 2D or higher. Also, your hands are in 1D; the decision boundary lives in the same dimension as the data.

SVM solves a maximization problem: find the hyperplane parameters that produce the largest margin — the widest possible "no man's land" between the two classes. Mathematically:

\[\text{Maximize: } \frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|} \quad \text{subject to: every point is on the correct side}\]

This is why SVM is formulated as constrained optimization. The constraint (every point correctly classified with at least 1 unit of margin) bounds the solution; the objective (minimize \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\) to maximize the margin) drives it toward the safest boundary.

16.7.5 Hard Margin vs. Soft Margin

Scope — Hard margin SVM assumptions: 1. The data is linearly separable — a hyperplane exists that perfectly separates +1 from −1. 2. No outliers are permitted — every point must respect the margin. 3. The margin is "hard" — zero tolerance for violations.

What breaks hard margin: A single mislabeled point deep in enemy territory forces the boundary to contort around it, destroying generalization. Real data always has noise. Hard margin SVM is primarily a building block for understanding — in practice, soft margin SVM (next lecture) is almost always used.

Hard margin SVM: Zero misclassifications allowed. The boundary must perfectly separate the classes with a margin. Brittle — one outlier ruins everything.

Soft margin SVM (preview): Allows some points to violate the margin (paying a penalty). This trades a bit of training accuracy for much better generalization. Covered in the next session.

Recap: SVM picks the decision boundary with the biggest safety buffer (margin). Only the closest points (support vectors) matter — all other data is ignored. This sparseness is SVM's secret weapon for high-dimensional data. Next we formalize this geometric intuition into mathematics.

Real-world connection: SVM's margin-maximizing philosophy comes from statistical learning theory — the idea that a classifier with a larger margin has a lower VC dimension and so better generalization guarantees. In practice, SVMs dominated text classification (spam detection, sentiment analysis) for over a decade before deep learning. They remain the go-to choice for small-to-medium datasets with clear class separation, especially in bioinformatics (gene expression classification, protein structure prediction).

16.8 SVM — Mathematical Formulation

Hook: "Maximize the margin" sounds simple — just push the lines apart. But the lines are defined by an equation, and their distance apart is a function of that equation's coefficients. How do you turn "spread your hands" into math a computer can solve?

16.8.1 Setup and Notation

We have \(N\) labeled training examples. For each example \(i\):

  • \(\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\) — the feature vector (e.g., CGPA and exam score, so \(d = 2\))
  • \(y_i \in \{+1, -1\}\) — the class label (+1 = admitted, −1 = not admitted)

In 2D the decision boundary is a line. In 3D it is a plane. In \(d \ge 4\) dimensions, it is called a hyperplane — a \((d-1)\)-dimensional flat surface embedded in \(d\)-dimensional space.

16.8.2 Hyperplane Equations

The equation of any hyperplane in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) is:

\[\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} - b = 0\]

where:

  • \(\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) — the weight vector. It determines the orientation (slope) of the hyperplane.
  • \(b \in \mathbb{R}\) — the bias (intercept). It shifts the hyperplane toward or away from the origin.
  • \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \cdots + w_d x_d\) — the dot product.

In 2D, this is \(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 - b = 0\), which is the familiar equation of a line \(y = mx + c\) in different notation.

Note on convention: The professor writes \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} - b = 0\) (minus \(b\)). The standard Bishop/PRML text writes \(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b = 0\) (plus \(b\)). The two are equivalent with \(b \to -b\). We follow the professor's convention throughout.

The three parallel hyperplanes in SVM:

HyperplaneEquationMeaning
Decision boundary\(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} - b = 0\)Points here: 50-50, cannot decide
Positive margin\(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} - b = +1\)Points on or beyond: classified +1
Negative margin\(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} - b = -1\)Points on or beyond: classified −1

The only unknowns are \(\mathbf{w}\) and \(b\). Once we find good values, SVM is done — classify a new point \(\mathbf{x}\) by checking the sign of \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} - b\).

16.8.3 The Normal Vector

Intuition — The "Perpendicular Arrow" Fact: From vector geometry, \(\mathbf{w}\) is always perpendicular (normal) to the hyperplane \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} - b = 0\). If the hyperplane is a flat wall, \(\mathbf{w}\) is an arrow pointing straight out of the wall — orthogonal to every direction along the wall. This is key: to walk from the decision boundary to the margin, you walk along \(\mathbf{w}\).

Think of a 2D line \(3x + 2y - 5 = 0\). The vector \((3, 2)\) is perpendicular to the line. If you stand on the line and walk in the direction \((3, 2)\), you move away from it in the shortest possible path.

16.8.4 Deriving the Margin Size

Derivation — How far apart are the margin lines?

Start at a point \(\mathbf{x}\) on the decision boundary: \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} - b = 0\).

Walk \(K\) units along the unit vector in the \(\mathbf{w}\) direction. The new position is: \[\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} + K \cdot \frac{\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}\] where \(\|\mathbf{w}\| = \sqrt{w_1^2 + w_2^2 + \cdots + w_d^2}\) is the Euclidean length of \(\mathbf{w}\).

We want this new point to land exactly on the positive margin line: \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x}' - b = 1\).

Substitute: \[\mathbf{w} \cdot \left(\mathbf{x} + K \cdot \frac{\mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}\right) - b = 1\]

Expand the dot product: \[\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + K \cdot \frac{\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|} - b = 1\]

Since \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} - b = 0\) (we started on the decision boundary) and \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{w} = \|\mathbf{w}\|^2\): \[K \cdot \frac{\|\mathbf{w}\|^2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad K \cdot \|\mathbf{w}\| = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad K = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}\]

The total margin (distance between the two outer lines, going from −1 to +1) is \(2K\):

\[\boxed{\text{Margin} = \frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}}\]

Key insight: The margin depends ONLY on \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\) — smaller \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\) means a larger margin. \(b\) controls position; \(\mathbf{w}\) controls margin width.

16.8.5 The Optimization Problem (Primal Form)

Goal: Maximize the margin \(2/\|\mathbf{w}\|\), which is equivalent to minimizing \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\).

For mathematical convenience (to make derivatives cleaner), we minimize \(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2\) — the \(\frac{1}{2}\) and the square do not change the optimal \(\mathbf{w}\), and the derivative of \(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2\) is simply \(\mathbf{w}\) (the factor of 2 cancels).

Hard margin SVM — Primal Problem:

\[\min_{\mathbf{w}, b} \quad \frac{1}{2} \|\mathbf{w}\|^2\]

Subject to the constraints (for all \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, N\)):

\[y_i \cdot (\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i - b) \ge 1\]

How the single constraint works:

  • If \(y_i = +1\): requires \(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i - b \ge 1\) — point must be on or beyond the +1 margin line.
  • If \(y_i = -1\): requires \(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i - b \le -1\) — point must be on or beyond the −1 margin line. Multiplying both sides by \(y_i = -1\) flips the inequality: \(-(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i - b) \ge 1\), which is the same form as above.

Multiplying by \(y_i\) elegantly unifies the two separate constraints into one.

Scope — When the primal problem is solvable: The data must be linearly separable — at least one \((\mathbf{w}, b)\) pair must satisfy all constraints. If no such pair exists (the classes overlap), the problem is infeasible — there is no solution. This is why soft margin SVM (which allows constraint violations) is needed in practice.

This is a rare and precious property in machine learning: SVM training cannot get stuck in bad local optima, unlike neural networks. This convexity is why SVMs were the gold standard for classification before deep learning.

16.9 From Primal to Dual — The Lagrangian Approach

Hook: The primal SVM problem minimizes \(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2\) — a function of \(\mathbf{w}\), a \(d\)-dimensional vector. For an image with 10,000 pixels, \(d = 10{,}000\). But you only have 100 training images. Wouldn't it be better to solve a problem with 100 variables instead of 10,000?

16.9.1 Why Convert to a Dual Problem?

Motivation: In the primal problem, every computation involves the dot product \(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i\) — a \(d\)-dimensional operation. When \(d \gg N\) (many features, few examples), this is wasteful.

By converting to the dual problem, all computations become dot products between pairs of data points: \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j\). The variables become \(\alpha_i\) (one per training example), not \(\mathbf{w}\) (one per feature). Crucially, \(\alpha_i = 0\) for every point that is NOT a support vector — so only a handful of \(\alpha\) values are non-zero.

Example: An image dataset with \(d = 10{,}000\) pixels and \(N = 100\) images. The primal has 10,000 variables; the dual has at most 100, and typically only 5–10 of those are non-zero (the support vectors).

16.9.2 Lagrangian Function

To handle the constraints \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i - b) \ge 1\), we introduce one Lagrange multiplier \(\alpha_i \ge 0\) per constraint and form the Lagrangian:

\[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{2} \|\mathbf{w}\|^2 - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i \left[ y_i (\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i - b) - 1 \right]\]

How it works: The term \(\alpha_i [y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i - b) - 1]\) is a penalty. If the constraint is satisfied (\(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i - b) \ge 1\)), the bracket is \(\ge 0\) and the penalty reduces \(\mathcal{L}\) (since we subtract). If the constraint is violated, the bracket becomes negative, and subtracting a negative increases \(\mathcal{L}\) — penalizing the violation.

The Lagrangian converts a constrained problem (minimize subject to constraints) into an unconstrained saddle-point problem (minimize over \(\mathbf{w}, b\), maximize over \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\)).

16.9.3 KKT Conditions and Stationarity

The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions are necessary conditions for optimality. The stationarity condition requires that at the optimum, the gradient of \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to the primal variables is zero:

Derivative with respect to \(\mathbf{w}\):

\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{w} - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i = 0\]

\[\boxed{\mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i}\]

Interpretation: The optimal weight vector \(\mathbf{w}\) is a linear combination of the training data points. Each point contributes \(\alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i\). Points with \(\alpha_i = 0\) contribute nothing — only support vectors matter.

Derivative with respect to \(b\):

\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial b} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i y_i = 0\]

\[\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i y_i = 0}\]

This constraint says the Lagrange multipliers, weighted by class labels, must sum to zero. It ensures a balance between the +1 and −1 support vectors.

16.9.4 The Dual Problem

Substituting \(\mathbf{w} = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i\) into the Lagrangian and simplifying yields the dual formulation. Here is the full derivation:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L} &= \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 - \sum_i \alpha_i [y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i - b) - 1] \\[4pt] &= \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 - \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b\underbrace{\sum_i \alpha_i y_i}_{=0 \text{ (stationarity)}} + \sum_i \alpha_i \\[4pt] &= \frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i\right)^\top \left(\sum_j \alpha_j y_j \mathbf{x}_j\right) - \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \left(\sum_j \alpha_j y_j \mathbf{x}_j\right)^\top \mathbf{x}_i + \sum_i \alpha_i \\[4pt] &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_i\sum_j \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j (\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j) - \sum_i\sum_j \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j (\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j) + \sum_i \alpha_i \\[4pt] &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j (\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j) \end{aligned}\]

The dual optimization problem:

\[\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j (\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j)\]

Subject to:

  • \(\alpha_i \ge 0\) for all \(i\)
  • \(\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i y_i = 0\)

Equivalently, as a minimization (the form solved by QP solvers):

\[\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \quad \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j (\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j) - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i\]

16.9.5 Why the Dual Is More Efficient

Three key advantages of the dual:

1. Variable count: \(N\) variables (\(\alpha_i\)) instead of \(d\) variables (\(\mathbf{w}\)). When \(d \gg N\), this is a massive reduction.

2. Support vector sparsity: Only a small fraction of \(\alpha_i\) are non-zero (the support vectors). The solution is sparse — most training points are irrelevant after training.

3. Kernel trick gateway: The dual involves ONLY dot products \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j\). This means we can replace \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j\) with any kernel function \(K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)\) — enabling non-linear classification without ever computing coordinates in a high-dimensional feature space. This is the topic of the next lecture.

16.9.6 The Classification Function

After solving the dual and obtaining \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\), the classification function for a new point \(\mathbf{x}\) is:

\[f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i y_i (\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}) + b\]

  • If \(f(\mathbf{x}) \ge 0\) → classify as +1
  • If \(f(\mathbf{x}) < 0\) → classify as −1

The weight vector is recovered as: \(\mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i\) (only support vectors with \(\alpha_i > 0\) contribute).

Computing \(b\): From the KKT conditions, for any support vector \(\mathbf{x}_s\) with \(\alpha_s > 0\), the margin constraint holds with equality: \(y_s(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_s - b) = 1\). Since \(y_s^2 = 1\):

\[\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_s - b = y_s \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_s - y_s\]

For numerical stability, average this over all support vectors: \[b = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|} \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} (\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_s - y_s)\] where \(\mathcal{S}\) is the set of support vector indices.

Note on sign convention: The professor's classification function uses \(+b\). The derived formula \(b = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_s - y_s\) is correct for this convention. If you encounter the standard Bishop formulation (which uses \(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b = 0\)), the bias formula flips sign: \(b = y_s - \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_s\). Keep track of which convention you are using.

Pitfall — Sign convention confusion: The SVM sign convention for \(b\) trips up many students. The professor writes the hyperplane as \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} - b = 0\) and the classification function as \(\sum \alpha_i y_i (\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}) + b\). The standard Bishop convention writes \(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b = 0\) and the classification function as \(\sum \alpha_i y_i (\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}) + b\). In both cases the classification function has \(+b\), but \(b\) has opposite sign! For exam problems, stick to the professor's convention and the formula \(b = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_s - y_s\).

Pitfall — All points are support vectors in small examples: In toy problems with 3-4 points, every point may be a support vector (\(\alpha_i > 0\) for all \(i\)). This is an artifact of tiny datasets. In real datasets with hundreds of points, typically only 5-15% are support vectors.

Recap: The dual transforms SVM from a \(d\)-variable primal to an \(N\)-variable dual, where only the support vectors (\(\alpha_i > 0\)) matter. The dual expresses everything as dot products \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j\) — setting the stage for the kernel trick. Next we work through a complete numerical example.

Real-world connection: The dual formulation's dependence on dot products is what makes SVMs work with kernel functions — functions that compute similarity in implicit high-dimensional spaces. This allows SVMs to find non-linear decision boundaries (circles, wavy curves) while still solving a convex optimization problem. The kernel trick — replacing \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j\) with \(K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)\) — made SVMs the dominant classifier of the 2000s for applications from handwriting recognition (MNIST) to protein classification.

16.10 SVM — Worked Example

Hook: Three points on a 2D plane — two blue (+1) above, one red (−1) below. Where does SVM draw the line? Let's trace every calculation by hand, from dot products to the final decision boundary.

16.10.1 Problem Setup

Data: Three points in 2D feature space:

Point\(\mathbf{x}_i\)\(y_i\)
\(\mathbf{x}_1\)\((0, 1)\)−1
\(\mathbf{x}_2\)\((−1, 3)\)+1
\(\mathbf{x}_3\)\((1, 3)\)+1

Visual: Points 2 and 3 sit at height \(x_2 = 3\) (one at \(x_1 = -1\), one at \(x_1 = 1\)). Point 1 sits below at height \(x_2 = 1\). Intuitively, the decision boundary should be a horizontal line somewhere between \(x_2 = 1\) and \(x_2 = 3\).

Augmented vectors: Add a column of 1s to absorb the bias term into the weight vector:

Point\(\mathbf{x}_i^{\text{aug}}\)\(y_i\)
\(\mathbf{x}_1\)\((0, 1, 1)\)−1
\(\mathbf{x}_2\)\((−1, 3, 1)\)+1
\(\mathbf{x}_3\)\((1, 3, 1)\)+1

The augmented weight vector will be \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{aug}} = (w_1, w_2, -b)\), so \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{aug}} \cdot \mathbf{x}_{\text{aug}} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 - b\).

16.10.2 Step 1 — Write the System of Linear Equations

The professor solves this using a direct linear system: for each point, the classification function evaluated at that point must equal its label (since these are support vectors where the margin constraint is active).

\[\sum_{j=1}^{3} \alpha_j y_j (\mathbf{x}_j^{\text{aug}} \cdot \mathbf{x}_i^{\text{aug}}) = y_i \quad \text{for } i = 1, 2, 3\]

(Note: The \(+b\) term is absorbed into the augmented dot product — the last component of each augmented vector handles it.)

Compute all dot products \(\mathbf{x}_j^{\text{aug}} \cdot \mathbf{x}_i^{\text{aug}}\):

\(\mathbf{x}_1\): (0,1,1)\(\mathbf{x}_2\): (−1,3,1)\(\mathbf{x}_3\): (1,3,1)
\(\mathbf{x}_1\)\(0+1+1 = \mathbf{2}\)\(0+3+1 = \mathbf{4}\)\(0+3+1 = \mathbf{4}\)
\(\mathbf{x}_2\)\(0+3+1 = \mathbf{4}\)\(1+9+1 = \mathbf{11}\)\(-1+9+1 = \mathbf{9}\)
\(\mathbf{x}_3\)\(0+3+1 = \mathbf{4}\)\(-1+9+1 = \mathbf{9}\)\(1+9+1 = \mathbf{11}\)

Equation 1 (for \(i = 1\), \(y_1 = -1\)): \[\alpha_1(-1)(2) + \alpha_2(+1)(4) + \alpha_3(+1)(4) = -1\] \[-2\alpha_1 + 4\alpha_2 + 4\alpha_3 = -1 \quad \text{(1)}\]

Equation 2 (for \(i = 2\), \(y_2 = +1\)): \[\alpha_1(-1)(4) + \alpha_2(+1)(11) + \alpha_3(+1)(9) = 1\] \[-4\alpha_1 + 11\alpha_2 + 9\alpha_3 = 1 \quad \text{(2)}\]

Equation 3 (for \(i = 3\), \(y_3 = +1\)): \[\alpha_1(-1)(4) + \alpha_2(+1)(9) + \alpha_3(+1)(11) = 1\] \[-4\alpha_1 + 9\alpha_2 + 11\alpha_3 = 1 \quad \text{(3)}\]

16.10.3 Step 2 — Solve the System

Subtract (3) from (2): \[(-4\alpha_1 + 11\alpha_2 + 9\alpha_3) - (-4\alpha_1 + 9\alpha_2 + 11\alpha_3) = 1 - 1\] \[2\alpha_2 - 2\alpha_3 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\alpha_2 = \alpha_3}\]

Substitute \(\alpha_3 = \alpha_2\) into (1): \[-2\alpha_1 + 4\alpha_2 + 4\alpha_2 = -1\] \[-2\alpha_1 + 8\alpha_2 = -1 \quad \text{(4)}\]

Substitute \(\alpha_3 = \alpha_2\) into (2): \[-4\alpha_1 + 11\alpha_2 + 9\alpha_2 = 1\] \[-4\alpha_1 + 20\alpha_2 = 1 \quad \text{(5)}\]

Solve (4) and (5) simultaneously:

From (4): \(-2\alpha_1 = -1 - 8\alpha_2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \alpha_1 = \frac{1 + 8\alpha_2}{2}\)

Substitute into (5): \[-4\left(\frac{1 + 8\alpha_2}{2}\right) + 20\alpha_2 = 1\] \[-2(1 + 8\alpha_2) + 20\alpha_2 = 1\] \[-2 - 16\alpha_2 + 20\alpha_2 = 1\] \[4\alpha_2 = 3\] \[\boxed{\alpha_2 = \frac{3}{4} = 0.75}\]

Then: \(\alpha_3 = \alpha_2 = \mathbf{0.75}\)

And: \(\alpha_1 = \frac{1 + 8(0.75)}{2} = \frac{1 + 6}{2} = \frac{7}{2} = \mathbf{3.5}\)

All \(\alpha\) values: \(\alpha_1 = 3.5\), \(\alpha_2 = 0.75\), \(\alpha_3 = 0.75\)

Verification — all three are non-zero: In this small example, all three points are support vectors. This is expected — with only 3 points and a perfectly separable configuration, every point helps define the margin. In larger datasets, most \(\alpha_i\) would be zero.

16.10.4 Step 3 — Compute \(\mathbf{w}\) and \(b\)

Compute the augmented weight vector: \[\mathbf{w}_{\text{aug}} = \sum_{i=1}^{3} \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i^{\text{aug}}\]

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbf{w}_{\text{aug}} &= 3.5 \cdot (-1) \cdot (0, 1, 1) + 0.75 \cdot (+1) \cdot (-1, 3, 1) + 0.75 \cdot (+1) \cdot (1, 3, 1) \\[4pt] &= (-3.5)(0, 1, 1) + (0.75)(-1, 3, 1) + (0.75)(1, 3, 1) \\[4pt] &= (0, -3.5, -3.5) + (-0.75, 2.25, 0.75) + (0.75, 2.25, 0.75) \\[4pt] &= (0 - 0.75 + 0.75, \; -3.5 + 2.25 + 2.25, \; -3.5 + 0.75 + 0.75) \\[4pt] &= \boxed{(0, \; 1.0, \; -2.0)} \end{aligned}\]

Interpretation of the augmented vector: The augmented weight vector is \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{aug}} = (w_1, w_2, -b)\). Therefore:

  • \(w_1 = 0\), \(w_2 = 1\) → the weight vector is \(\mathbf{w} = (0, 1)\)
  • \(-b = -2\) → the bias is \(\boxed{b = 2}\)

Verification using the bias formula: From the KKT conditions, for support vector \(\mathbf{x}_2\): \[b = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_2 - y_2 = (0,1) \cdot (-1,3) - 1 = 3 - 1 = \mathbf{2} \;\checkmark\]

Check \(\sum \alpha_i y_i\): \(3.5(-1) + 0.75(+1) + 0.75(+1) = -3.5 + 1.5 = -2.0\). This equals \(-b\), as required by the augmented vector method (where the last component of \(\mathbf{w}_{\text{aug}}\) gives \(\sum \alpha_i y_i = -b\)).

16.10.5 Step 4 — The Decision Boundary

With \(\mathbf{w} = (0, 1)\) and \(b = 2\):

\[\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} - b = 0 \cdot x_1 + 1 \cdot x_2 - 2 = 0\] \[x_2 = 2\]

The decision boundary is the horizontal line \(y = 2\).

Classification rule:

  • Points with \(x_2 > 2\) (above the line) → classified as +1
  • Points with \(x_2 < 2\) (below the line) → classified as −1

Verification against training data:

Point\(x_2\)PredictionTrue LabelCorrect?
\(\mathbf{x}_1 = (0, 1)\)1\(1 < 2\) → −1−1✓
\(\mathbf{x}_2 = (−1, 3)\)3\(3 > 2\) → +1+1✓
\(\mathbf{x}_3 = (1, 3)\)3\(3 > 2\) → +1+1✓

Margin: The margin lines are at \(x_2 = 1\) (negative side) and \(x_2 = 3\) (positive side). The decision boundary at \(x_2 = 2\) sits exactly midway. Margin size = \(2 / \|\mathbf{w}\| = 2 / 1 = 2\) units.

Sense-check: The boundary is horizontal (\(w_1 = 0\)) because the two +1 points share the same \(x_2 = 3\) and differ only in \(x_1\). The \(x_1\) coordinate provides no class separation — only \(x_2\) matters. SVM correctly identified this.

16.10.6 Interpretation

Recap: On three points, SVM finds the decision boundary \(y = 2\) — a horizontal line exactly halfway between the −1 point at \(y = 1\) and the +1 points at \(y = 3\). All three points are support vectors (\(\alpha_i > 0\)). The margin is 2 units wide. This matches our geometric intuition perfectly.

Pitfall — Solving vs. optimizing: The professor's direct linear-system approach works for tiny examples where ALL points are support vectors. In real SVMs, you solve the full quadratic programming dual problem. The direct approach is a simplification for hand calculation on exams. Do not confuse it with the actual SVM training algorithm.

Pitfall — Augmented vector convention: The professor's method of adding a "1" column and absorbing \(b\) into \(\mathbf{w}\) is a non-standard shortcut for hand computation. The standard approach keeps \(\mathbf{w}\) and \(b\) separate. For exam problems, follow whichever convention your professor uses — the final decision boundary will be the same either way.

Real-world connection: This 3-point example scales to thousands of dimensions. In text classification, each feature might be a TF-IDF word weight, and the weight vector \(\mathbf{w}\) tells you which words push the decision toward +1 (positive weights) or −1 (negative weights). A weight of zero means that word is irrelevant — just like \(w_1 = 0\) in our example meant the \(x_1\) coordinate did not matter.

16.12 Key Industry Applications and Real-World Connections

Gaussian Mixture Models in Practice

  • **Height and gender modeling:** Adult height data across a population naturally follows a mixture of two Gaussians (male and female distributions). GMM identifies these underlying groups without being told about gender — it discovers the structure purely from the data.
  • **Background subtraction in video:** Each pixel's color over time is modeled as a GMM. A new pixel color with low probability under the mixture is flagged as a foreground object (person, car). This is used in surveillance systems and traffic monitoring.
  • **Customer segmentation:** Retailers use GMM to cluster shoppers based on dozens of behavioral features (purchase frequency, average basket size, browsing time, return rate). The soft assignments let a customer be "60% luxury, 40% budget" — far more useful for targeted marketing than a hard label.
  • **Speaker diarization:** In multi-speaker audio recordings, GMM clusters audio segments by voice characteristics, answering "who spoke when?" without knowing the speakers in advance.
  • **Anomaly detection:** Any data point with very low probability density under the GMM is flagged as anomalous — used in fraud detection (unusual transaction patterns) and equipment monitoring (abnormal sensor readings).
  • **Python implementation:** Scikit-learn's `GaussianMixture` uses K-means initialization by default, supports multiple covariance types (`full`, `tied`, `diag`, `spherical`), and provides BIC/AIC for automatic model selection. A typical workflow: loop over K, fit GMM, record BIC, pick the K with minimum BIC.

Support Vector Machines in Practice

  • **Text classification:** SVMs dominated spam detection, sentiment analysis, and document categorization for over a decade. With TF-IDF features, document vectors are extremely high-dimensional (vocabulary size) but sparse — the dual formulation with its support vector sparsity is ideal for this regime.
  • **Bioinformatics:** Gene expression datasets typically have thousands of genes (features) but only dozens of patients (samples). SVM handles this \(d \gg N\) scenario naturally through the dual. Applications include cancer subtype classification and protein function prediction.
  • **Image recognition:** Before deep learning, SVMs with hand-crafted features (HOG, SIFT) were the state of the art for object detection and face recognition. The kernel trick allowed non-linear boundaries while keeping training convex.
  • **Handwriting recognition:** The MNIST dataset of handwritten digits was a classic SVM benchmark — with an RBF kernel, SVMs achieved ~1.4% error rate, competitive with early neural networks.
  • **Dual formulation and the kernel trick:** The dual's reliance on dot products \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j\) is what enables the *kernel trick* — replacing the dot product with a kernel function \(K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \phi(\mathbf{x}_i)^\top \phi(\mathbf{x}_j)\) that implicitly maps data to a higher-dimensional space. This allows finding non-linear decision boundaries (circles, wavy curves) while still solving a convex optimization problem. Common kernels include the RBF (Gaussian) kernel \(K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \exp(-\gamma \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2)\) and the polynomial kernel.

Bridging GMM and SVM

Though GMM (unsupervised clustering) and SVM (supervised classification) solve different problems, they share deep connections:

  • **EM and dual optimization** are both iterative algorithms that converge to local optima of their respective objective functions.
  • **Both rely on dot products:** GMM uses Mahalanobis distances \((\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\); SVM uses data dot products \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j\).
  • **Both are building blocks:** GMM is a generative model (it models how data is generated). SVM is a discriminative model (it models the decision boundary directly). Understanding both paradigms is essential for a complete machine learning toolkit.

Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM)

Must-know: GMM is a probabilistic clustering model that assumes data is generated from a mixture of K Gaussian distributions. Unlike K-means, it produces soft assignments (responsibilities) and supports elliptical cluster shapes via covariance matrices.

\[p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \cdot \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)\]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Using K too large — GMM will split a real cluster into meaningless fragments. Use BIC to select K. Also: EM converges to a local optimum, so always use multiple initializations.

Self-check: Why does GMM handle overlapping clusters better than K-means?

Connects to: K-means clustering, Maximum Likelihood Estimation.

Expectation-Maximization (EM) Algorithm

Must-know: EM iteratively estimates Gaussian parameters when cluster assignments are unknown. The E-step computes responsibilities (posterior probabilities). The M-step updates means, variances, and mixing weights using responsibility-weighted averages.

\[r_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k \cdot \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j \cdot \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_j, \sigma_j^2)}\]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Forgetting that the variance update uses the new mean, not the old one. Also: the responsibilities must sum to 1 across clusters for each point.

Self-check: In the M-step, why is the mean update a weighted average rather than a simple average?

Connects to: GMM, Hidden Markov Models, Missing data imputation.

Gaussian PDF (1D and Multivariate)

Must-know: The 1D Gaussian PDF is defined by mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). The multivariate Gaussian PDF uses a mean vector \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), which controls the shape (spherical/elliptical) and orientation of the cluster.

\[\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{1/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\right)\]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Confusing variance (\(\sigma^2\)) with standard deviation (\(\sigma\)). In the Gaussian PDF, the denominator uses \(\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}\), not \(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma\). Also: forgetting the normalizing constant leads to invalid probabilities.

Self-check: What does the off-diagonal entry in a covariance matrix represent?

Connects to: Normal distribution, Mahalanobis distance, Central Limit Theorem.

Support Vector Machines — Core Concept

Must-know: SVM finds the decision boundary (hyperplane) that maximizes the margin between two classes. Only support vectors (data points on the margin lines) define the boundary — all other points are irrelevant.

\[\text{Margin} = \frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \min_{\mathbf{w}, b} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \text{ s.t. } y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i - b) \ge 1\]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Using hard margin on non-separable data — the optimization becomes infeasible. Real data almost always requires soft margin SVM. Also: confusing the sign convention for b.

Self-check: Why do only support vectors matter for the decision boundary?

Connects to: Convex optimization, Generalization theory, VC dimension.

SVM Dual Formulation

Must-know: The dual problem expresses SVM using \(N\) variables (\(\alpha_i\), Lagrange multipliers) instead of \(d\) variables (\(\mathbf{w}\)). The weight vector is recovered as \(\mathbf{w} = \sum \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i\). Only support vectors have \(\alpha_i > 0\).

\[\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j (\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j) \quad \text{s.t. } \alpha_i \ge 0,\ \sum \alpha_i y_i = 0\]

⚠️ Top pitfall: Sign convention confusion — the professor uses \(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} - b = 0\) while Bishop uses \(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b = 0\). The bias formula flips: \(b = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_s - y_s\). Stick to one convention.

Self-check: Why is the dual formulation more efficient when the number of features exceeds the number of training examples (d >> N)?

Connects to: Lagrangian duality, KKT conditions, Kernel trick.
